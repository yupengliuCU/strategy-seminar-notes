One-sentence summary

This case shows that a venture-backed, for-profit company can build a platform for a fast-moving technical field by giving volunteer contributors real control over the roadmap — Hugging Face grew from a 2016 chatbot startup into the hub where the machine-learning community stores, shares, and downloads open models (100,000+ daily users and 9 million downloads per day by fall 2022), while earning money only at the edges, from roughly 1,000 paying companies that wanted MLOps help.

Reading guide — why this case anchors the "Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy" topic. This topic reads the Hugging Face ecosystem as an economic object: who contributes models and data, why they contribute for free, how the platform governs a community it cannot command, and where value is captured. The quantitative papers in the topic (e.g., Laufer et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025) measure the Hub after the fact; this case is the descriptive baseline they build on. It supplies the institutional story the numbers cannot: the volunteers' motives, the firm's deliberately weak authority over contributors, the asynchronous Slack-based organization, the freemium revenue logic, and the 2022 GPT-4chan episode in which community freedom collided with platform responsibility. Read it as a company self-portrait — every figure is a claim the company or its founders made to casewriters, and every exhibit is either a company document or casewriter computation — then ask which of its claims the Hub-anatomy literature confirms, qualifies, or contradicts.

1. Background and founding: from chatbot to NLP hub to multi-modal platform

Hugging Face was founded in 2016 by three friends leaving earlier startups: Clément Delangue (CEO), Julien Chaumond (CTO), and Thomas Wolf (CSO). The market was full of chatbot startups, and the founders found the existing assistants uninspiring. Wolf recalled that "chatbots were all very hyped and everyone was doing them, yet they were built with very, very, simple technology," and the team set out to apply recent AI advances to make a "fun, conversational AI," in contrast to what Delangue called the "really boring, very productivity-driven" Siri and Alexa (case p. 5 of the PDF). The company took its name from the hugging face emoji, chosen to signal the friendliness the founders wanted in the technology. The chatbot itself exceeded expectations, winning the Conversational Intelligence Challenge (ConvAI2) at NeurIPS 2018, a well-known ML conference.

Two choices made the company. First, the founders open-sourced their work: their best-known library, Transformers, which made recent ML models accessible to ordinary programmers, was released in April 2018 under the Apache License 2.0. Second, they treated open source as a contribution to a community rather than as a marketing tactic. Delangue: "We were releasing things without thinking too much about it, more as a way to pay it forward for the community. But, very quickly, the community started to gather around the platform, started to contribute like open-source contributors, and then progressively they started to share their models" (case p. 5 of the PDF). The community around Transformers was, in the case's phrase, a "happy accident." The founders also chose investors who would not force early monetization; Wolf said the team said "no" to people who wanted a short-term monetization plan because it would have constrained them "too much in terms of scope" (case p. 6 of the PDF). An angel round of $1.2 million closed in March 2017.

By 2019 the observed growth in use of the open-source libraries — Wolf: "We saw the exponential that you always want in your startup—exponential in the number of users, exponential in the number of installs of the library" (case p. 6 of the PDF) — outweighed the chatbot plan, and the team pivoted to a new end-goal: a coherent open-source ML ecosystem that nurtured others. The founders stopped hiring engineers to build models directly and instead built a framework within which a broader community could work; a second library, Datasets, aggregated training data; and the original chatbot became one of many projects hosted on the platform. Delangue described the shift as evolutionary: "The underlying abstraction that we built for the chatbot became the early innings of what Hugging Face is today" (case p. 6 of the PDF).

The industry backdrop the case sketches. The case spends its early pages locating Hugging Face in three contexts: deep learning (the ImageNet competition that began in 2010, the 2012 breakthrough by Hinton's group on convolutional networks with an error rate far below 25%, and error rates falling below the 5% human benchmark within a few years), the NLP applications boom (translation, chatbots, automatic text generation), and the economics of open source (LAMP-era software, TensorFlow released by Google in 2015, PyTorch released by Meta in 2017). It also reports market context: experts estimated the AI technology market at roughly $60 billion in 2021, with the most optimistic forecasts reaching $422 billion by 2028; AWS supplied at least 30% and Azure roughly 20% of cloud services; open-source-based services revenue was estimated at $17 billion in 2019 and expected to reach $30 billion by 2022. Two precedents frame the financing story: GitHub, founded in 2008 with 28 million contributors by 2018, was acquired by Microsoft for $7.5 billion in June 2018, and IBM acquired Red Hat in June 2019. Freemium, the case notes, was the standard way open-source projects monetized — basic services free, advanced or enterprise features and consulting charged for (case pp. 3–5 of the PDF).

Modality expansion and the 2021–22 platform. The platform's content stayed community-driven even as its scope widened: Hugging Face added audio processing in February 2021 and vision processing in March 2021, expanding beyond NLP. On the research side the company contributed to large language models by helping organize BigScience, the collaboration that released BLOOM, described in the case as one of the first purely open-source LLMs — "not generated by a single organization, but [with] many contributors, with Hugging Face working as an organizer of the many contributions," and with the French government paying for the compute (case Exhibit 12). By fall 2022 the site hosted open-source ML technology used by more than 100,000 daily users and downloaded 9 million times per day (case p. 1 of the PDF).

2. The platform model: a community-contributed model hub

The case describes Hugging Face as a multi-sided platform realized through an open-source ecosystem: it connects users (researchers, companies, and individuals) with ML technology resources that range from datasets and untrained model architectures to pre-trained models and fully functional demos. The most popular resources are pre-trained models — models that already solve general-purpose problems and can be adapted to specific use-cases through fine-tuning, a simpler method than training from scratch. A model built to process text could, for example, be fine-tuned into a customer-service bot. Resources are reachable through two doors: the user-friendly website and the technical libraries (Transformers and Datasets). Most model code is hosted on GitHub, while the model weights — the numbers that encode what a model learned — live on Hugging Face's own website, and the site's Spaces let users run demos directly in the browser. Delangue described the design logic: "There's an easy flow between these three components; they inform and build off one another. For instance, someone can visit the site and see a popular Spaces demo, discover which models were used, pick a new dataset to fine-tune it with, generate a new model, and (maybe) build a new Spaces demo" (case p. 8 of the PDF). Use of the platform was free and unrestricted: any user could download, use, and modify any model or dataset.

Who supplies this content? Mostly volunteers. The opening of the case states the model plainly: the technologies on the platform were "mostly created by volunteers from its open-source community, who contributed for free, usually developing new ML models." Their motives were "accelerating the development of AI technology, honing their skills on the technical frontier, and helping others," all within "a broader open-source ethos prevalent in the ML community that emphasized collaboration, transparency, and teamwork" (case p. 1 of the PDF). The decisive organizational fact follows: "Hugging Face had little power to direct its contributors; instead, the firm adapted its development goals to its open-source community" (case p. 1 of the PDF). The community's breadth of expertise is what let a small firm offer state-of-the-art models across many use-cases.

The case draws the contrast with the large-technology-company model of AI development through Wolf:

We try very hard not to reproduce the mistakes of some open-source ecosystems in ML. For example, Google just makes software for itself. It’s hard to care about people outside of Google when you’re so big. Usually, these new large AI models make it difficult to actually do research on this model, and try to understand how they work, where they don’t work, how to improve them. They are only studied by the team who created them, which is like five or six people. So it’s a very small foundation.— Thomas Wolf, co-founder and CSO, quoted in the case (case p. 7 of the PDF)

The economic logic of giving the product away was stated by Delangue as a deliberate value theory:

With open source, you don’t have to capture 100% of the value you create. The platform creates 1000x more value being open than if it were closed. Even capturing 1% of this value has the potential to be more successful than the traditional proprietary approach.— Clément Delangue, co-founder and CEO, quoted in the case (case p. 8 of the PDF)

Community participation was also a capability strategy for keeping up with a frontier that moves faster than any company can staff. Hugging Face's open-source lead, Lysandre Debut:

If we have ten experts in machine learning, the field moves so fast that our experts cannot implement everything that comes out. Enabling our community means that we can keep up to date, because an interested community member can work on new technology, provide it as a tool, and make it make sense to the rest of our community.— Lysandre Debut, Hugging Face open-source lead, quoted in the case (case p. 8 of the PDF)

The case describes several deliberate mechanisms for keeping contributors engaged. When an employee and a contributor built similar models, the contributor's model "usually won out in order to encourage volunteer contributions." Every six months the company surveyed contributors about satisfaction and interest in new features (an April 2021 survey showed strong recommendation rates for the libraries, with more satisfaction for PyTorch integration than TensorFlow). The company also ran "sprints": short, hackathon-style bursts of focused work. A 2021 sprint around JAX, an open-source numerical computing framework from Google, involved groups of five to ten employees and volunteers, 800 participants in total, and many projects over one month. One output of that sprint was DALL-E mini, a test program that generated images from text prompts and was released publicly in spring 2022, where it went viral. Delangue:

Someone from our community kept working on the model, uploaded it on the platform, and it went crazy viral. I think it generated like 200 million images in the past seven days. That’s something that we wouldn’t have prioritized as a team and that we might have missed, but the community enthusiasm meant that someone came up with it anyway. This enables us to adapt to the changing topics and to stay at the cutting edge.— Clément Delangue, co-founder and CEO, quoted in the case (case p. 9 of the PDF)

3. Operating structure and governance: decentralization as design

The case treats Hugging Face's internal organization as the firm-level counterpart of its community model. The company was registered in New York City with office space in New York and Paris, but most of its more than 150 employees worked remotely, and the company "prioritized asynchronous discussions on Slack channels over synchronous meetings"; video meetings were recorded and posted on Slack (case p. 1 of the PDF). A later section adds texture: in 2022 the three founders were based in Paris, Miami, and the Netherlands, most employees worked around the globe, and the company supported small collocated offices in Bern, Montreal, New York City, Paris, and San Francisco. With employees spread across time zones, teams relied on Slack internally and on Discord, GitHub, the community tab, online forums, Slack, and social media for contact with contributors and users (case pp. 10–11 of the PDF).

The firm organized around three focal points rather than traditional functions — revenue, visibility, and usage — each with its own team. Debut explained the logic and, notably, the firewall between the open-source mission and revenue:

The business team is focused on revenue. The science team is focused on visibility, and the open-source team is focused on usage. In the open-source team, we’re focused on usage rather than revenue. This separation of concerns enables our open-source work to be truly free and open and does not reward putting important features behind paywalls. We, of course, collaborate with other teams, so we understand our revenue streams and business perspectives, but at the end of the day our focus is on raw usage.— Lysandre Debut, quoted in the case (case p. 11 of the PDF)

Within each focus the organization was flat, with only a few designated leaders, and employees were expected to be generalists: "We started with the idea that people are much more multifaceted than large organizations allow them to be," Delangue recalled (case p. 10 of the PDF). Nobody specialized in marketing full-time; researchers and developers were told that marketing "is your responsibility as much as everything else," including tweeting about new features and talking to journalists. No single community monitor fielded inbound messages; responding was "a distributed, shared responsibility." The firm diverged from the generalist pattern only where regulation or infrastructure demanded expertise — it kept one full-time finance person and one full-time legal person, and it ran no servers of its own, with an internal team contracting and managing cloud computing instead (case p. 11 of the PDF).

Project governance mirrored the community logic inside the firm. Employees and contributors alike could propose projects; management "viewed themselves as advisors and connectors rather than having formal power to approve or shut down projects." Buy-in mattered mainly for recognition and resources, and it could be won through asynchronous proposals (Google documents, Notion posts, Slack), with face-to-face conversation when written iteration became counterproductive. Wolf described a portfolio approach: "People come to me ten times a day with a proposal for a new project, and I say yes to maybe 1% of it. We also do a lot of small bets with maybe one or two people, to see if they can prove that it will work" (case p. 12 of the PDF). Failure was treated as information: employees who had led failed projects were more often trusted with future ones. Delangue described the balancing act:

We want to let other people lead, but to keep everyone aligned. One of the problems that we run into is that if an organization is super decentralized, people are going to go in all directions because no one is making decisions. But with feedback and communication, someone is able to look at a new proposal and say, "I’ve tried that five times in the past. I wouldn’t waste your time with it, and here’s why." That creates a system that so far has worked really well for us. We have radical transparency and knowledge sharing, while still having the involvement of people who feel ownership over their products.— Clément Delangue, co-founder and CEO, quoted in the case (case p. 12 of the PDF)

The leadership roles in 2022, as the case's Exhibit 10a describes, mapped onto the product: under CTO Chaumond sat platform work (gradio, the library behind the Spaces demos; product infrastructure, including the interface with cloud compute; and the website); under CSO Wolf sat scientific projects (large language models like BLOOM, simulation environments), open-source development, and public-policy and ethics work; and under CEO Delangue sat revenue-generating activity (sales, customer support) plus small administrative teams (communications, legal, finance). Because teams were "fluid and regularly reshuffled," the organizational chart was intended to add clarity rather than to restrict collaboration (case p. 22 of the PDF). The case summarizes the payoff in scale terms: each employee could have the impact of five or ten people by leveraging a team of volunteers (Sanseviero, case p. 12 of the PDF).

4. Growth: the numbers the case reports

Hugging Face grew fast along several dimensions the case reports as company claims. Employee count went from 38 in 2020 to more than 150 by late spring 2022 (a related passage says the team had "just over 30 employees" in 2020 — the case is internally inconsistent on the 2020 figure). By 2022 the team was split roughly 30% toward science, 40% toward open source and developer advocacy, and 30% toward business. Site-scale figures cited in the case include 30,000 datasets, 100,000 models, and 20,000 Spaces on the website; more than 100,000 daily users; and 9 million downloads per day by fall 2022. Community participation grew from 10 teams contributing monthly to Transformers at the end of 2018 to more than 30 by 2020; in 2022 the case cites 1,000 frequent contributors and 11,000 people on Hugging Face's Discord server, and its Exhibit 6 notes more than 24,000 contributors on the platform as of November 2022. As of May 2022 the Transformers library had 62,000 stars and 14,000 forks on GitHub.

5. Business model and monetization

Hugging Face was "in many ways like an open-source, research-oriented community" and simultaneously "a for-profit company backed by venture capital (VC) funding" (case p. 2 of the PDF). The company began offering paid services in 2021, leaving its core services free for general use. A small percentage of users — mostly companies using ML technologies — paid for additional MLOps services that let them employ the platform for storing, training, and deploying models, and paying users could also consult internal Hugging Face experts on how best to apply ML to their business problems (case p. 2 of the PDF). The case describes the enterprise offering as a response to demand: companies asked for help incorporating Hugging Face features into their own infrastructure "for increased security and customization," and in 2021 the firm began to charge for access to some of its experts and for special explanations of new features. Plans started at $9 per month for limited usage and were adjusted to enterprise size and needs, with pay-as-you-go and custom options for heavier assistance or larger data volumes (case pp. 9–10 of the PDF).

Delangue defended the late start in monetization:

That strategy definitely served us well, and looking back I’m really happy we did it that way rather than focusing too early on monetization.— Clément Delangue, co-founder and CEO, quoted in the case (case p. 9 of the PDF)

Charging for basic use was "out of the question," because it would deter the volunteer contributors who made Hugging Face popular. By 2022 the company had more than 1,000 paying clients, mostly companies that wanted AI and NLP tools in their products or operations but lacked in-house capacity to build or manage the technology; a large majority arrived organically through inbound requests, having heard of Hugging Face through its community. The most common enterprise applications were classification tasks on text, audio, or video: Chaumond: "The majority of use-cases in industry are classification problems—document classifications or token classification to do information extraction. We found that the most efficient way to solve a classification problem is to fine-tune a classification model" (case p. 10 of the PDF). Named paying customers in 2022 included Bloomberg, Intel, Grammarly, and Pfizer, and most clients used many models, producing long, continuous relationships.

6. Strategic tensions and open questions the case leaves open

The GPT-4chan episode. In late May 2022 a community member uploaded GPT-4chan, a language model trained on posts from 4chan's "Politically Incorrect" board over three and a half years, which reproduced the board's toxic, racist, and derogatory speech. The case prints the contributor's own rationale:

GPT-4chan is trained on anonymously posted and sparsely moderated discussions of political topics. Its intended use is to reproduce text according to the distribution of its input data. It may also be a useful tool to investigate discourse in such anonymous online communities. Lastly, it has potential applications in tasks such as toxicity detection, as initial experiments show promising zero-shot resultsg when comparing a string’s likelihood under GPT-4chan to its likelihood under GPT-J 6B.— GPT-4chan contributor, quoted in the case; the superscript g is the case's footnote marker for "zero-shot results" (case p. 14 of the PDF)

The model generated controversy because it reproduced sexist, racist, ableist, and otherwise hurtful language. Hugging Face aspired to work collaboratively with the research community and to empower its members to pursue their interests, yet the episode raised the question of whether unlimited community discretion on research topics conflicted with the company's other principles. After "some days of internal discussion," Hugging Face decided not to support the model but left the debate and model documentation in place for future reference (case p. 2 of the PDF). The case lays out both readings — openness as the ethos that made civil discussion and research possible, versus the model's demonstrated capacity for harm and the majority of the community's call-out of its limited research value — and ends the episode with Delangue wondering, in fall 2022, whether the company had made the right decision (case p. 14 of the PDF). The episode is the case's cleanest illustration that "community-driven" does not mean "responsibility-free": the platform ultimately exercised the veto it normally declines to use.

Control versus community. The firm's whole model rests on giving contributors direction over the roadmap, and Delangue was explicit that the model has a scale limit: "This is the extent that I can see in terms of scale. The way we operate is the same as when we were 40 or 50 people—it might work if we were 250, but not 1,000" (case p. 13 of the PDF). His stated aspiration for the culture was: "What I want is to get to a place where I don't have to tell anyone what to do, where people can decide for themselves what to work on, and I just formalize and spread the culture" (case p. 14 of the PDF). Whether radical decentralization survives growth, hiring, and enterprise demands is left open.

Monetization and the moat. The case reports that the company was exploring new ways to monetize, especially tabular data, which could "dramatically increase the company's earnings," even while the community found other ML topics more exciting. Wolf conceded: "We still haven't nailed the part that will be the most interesting in terms of value" (case p. 14 of the PDF). The strategic question the case leaves the reader with is what the durable competitive advantage actually is: Hugging Face's own libraries were open source, its model code lived on GitHub, and its content was contributed by volunteers who could in principle take their work elsewhere. The case's implicit answer — that the moat is the aggregation itself: the site was "extremely usable and focused on ML, making it the go-to location to search for and identify new models," and the datasets, models, and Spaces "inform and reinforce one another" (case p. 8 of the PDF) — is a network-effects argument that the case never tests. (Reading-note inference: the case suggests, but never claims, that the switching costs are the workflow — fine-tuning, hosting weights, Spaces demos — built around one destination.) A related open question is competitive: Wolf's own contrast with Google shows the frontier firms can also host and monetize models on their clouds; whether an open, community-governed hub can keep the center against them is not answered. Exhibit 12 arrays the company's 2022 priorities across platform, open-source, and scientific-research goals, each with a stated value to the company and to the community, and the tensions between the two columns of that exhibit are the strategic content of the case.

7. Position in the literature

Within the topic "Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy," this case is the descriptive anchor: it tells the institutional story that later, quantitative work on the Hub measures. The connections below are this note's mapping of the case onto the topic's papers; the case itself was written before most of them and cites none of them.

  • Laufer et al. (2025), the anatomy of the Hub. The case already performs, in miniature, what an anatomy of the Hub would quantify: its Exhibit 6 plots contributors by models uploaded against downloads (September–October 2022), reports more than 24,000 contributors, and emphasizes "significant variation in the type of contributions" across a deliberately non-representative sample of the top 75. Where Laufer et al. measure the population of models and their metadata at scale, the case supplies the qualitative account of the same object — the hub as a destination whose value depends on who uploads, who downloads, and why.
  • Longpre et al. (2025). The case documents the data side of the ecosystem: Hugging Face's Datasets library aggregated training data, and "contributors imported many of these datasets out of a desire to share resources with Hugging Face's open-source community" (case p. 8 of the PDF). Work on the provenance, licensing, and composition of the datasets that circulate through such hubs extends exactly this supply story with measurement.
  • Wu et al. (2025), "Learning From the Best." The case is full of learning as a motive and mechanism: volunteers contribute partly to "hone their skills on the technical frontier," open-source culture involves helping new participants learn, and contributors who become employees shift from making models to building the tools that make model-making easier. (Reading-note mapping: this note reads "Learning From the Best" as formalizing such learning dynamics in these ecosystems; the case supplies the micro-evidence of what participants report they learn and from whom.)
  • Yue & Nagle (2024). One of this case's co-authors (Yue) also works on the economics of open AI ecosystems, with Nagle, who has documented how hard open-source economic value is to observe (the case itself cites Greenstein & Nagle 2014). The case's puzzle — how a firm that gives away its core can still be a business, and how open ecosystems are governed — is the question that work takes into data.
  • Azoulay et al. (2024). The case's science strand — publishing at conferences, releasing BLOOM as one of the first purely open-source LLMs, the "visibility" goal of the science team, and Delangue's "pay it forward" framing — connects the firm to the economics-of-science literature on openness and cumulative knowledge production. The case shows a for-profit firm deliberately producing public knowledge goods as a reputational strategy.
  • White et al. (2024), the Model Openness Framework (MOF). The case shows that "open source" in AI comes in degrees and layers: Transformers code was released under Apache License 2.0 (which permits modification and requires sharing copies or modifications), most model code lived on GitHub, while the model weights — the artifact that makes a model usable — were hosted on Hugging Face's own website. That separation of code, weights, data, and documentation is exactly the granularity the MOF formalizes; the case anticipates it as a business-design fact.
  • O'Mahony & Karp (2022). The governance literature on open-source communities and the firms that sponsor them finds its natural subject here: a sponsor firm that describes itself as "educators and helpers of the community, less than instigators," that runs "radical transparency" internally, and whose authority over contributors is deliberately weak — until the GPT-4chan episode forces the sponsor to exercise authority anyway. (Reading-note mapping of the paper's constructs onto the case.)
  • von Hippel & von Krogh (2003), the private-collective model. The volunteers who "contributed for free, usually developing new ML models," motivated by accelerating AI, honing skills, and helping others, are a textbook private-collective population: they privately bear the costs of developing models and then freely reveal them to a collective good, gaining skills and reputation in return.
  • Boudreau (2010), granting access versus devolving control on open platforms. Hugging Face is an extreme case of the devolve-control end of Boudreau's trade-off: employees and contributors freely choose projects, management says "yes to maybe 1%" of proposals, contributor models beat employee models when they collide — and the platform still reserves, and occasionally exercises, control over access (removing GPT-4chan). The case therefore illustrates both margins of platform design in one firm.

Outside the topic library. The case's own background material reaches into the literatures this course reads alongside it. The two-sided structure — free users on one side, paying enterprises on the other, with the hub as intermediary — invites the standard platform-economics framing (e.g., Rochet & Tirole 2003 on two-sided markets; platform-strategy work on how sponsors set rules, e.g., Gawer & Cusumano). The contributor side maps onto the open-source incentives literature (e.g., Lerner & Tirole 2002 on career-concern and reputation motives: contributors "hone skills" and build visibility precisely in that spirit) and onto the firm-sponsorship strand of open-source research that O'Mahony and colleagues developed over two decades. The case's own endnotes cite the economics of open-source value that this topic's parent seminar also uses — Greenstein & Nagle (2014) on Apache as "digital dark matter," Murciano-Goroff, Zhuo, and Greenstein (2021) on veiled value creation in server software, and Iansiti & Lakhani (2020) on the "AI factory" paradigm reproduced as Exhibit 3 — and the GitHub (Microsoft, $7.5 billion, 2018) and Red Hat (IBM, 2019) acquisitions it reports are the observable market events behind the "how do you monetize open source" question. Finally, the case is an unusually clean empirical setting for questions its own authors did not ask: because the Hub logs downloads and uploads publicly (as Exhibit 6 demonstrates), the qualitative claims of this case are checkable — and have been checked — against platform data by the papers above.

8. How to read the case: evidence, limits, and use

What the case is. It is a teaching case written for class discussion, reviewed and approved by a company designate, and funded by Harvard Business School rather than by Hugging Face. Its primary material is interviews with company personnel (the many quotations attributed to Delangue, Wolf, Debut, Chaumond, Boudier, and Sanseviero), company documents (Figures and Exhibits 4, 5, 8, 9, 10), press reporting (endnotes), one survey the company ran (Exhibit 7, April 2021), third-party data (Exhibit 11 funding rounds from Crunchbase), and one casewriter computation from public platform data (Exhibit 6). It is descriptive: there are no hypotheses, no formal models, and no econometrics, and the case makes no pretense of independent verification of company claims.

Internal inconsistencies to note. The case gives two different 2020 headcounts (38 employees in the growth section; "just over 30" in the community section). It says the site grew to "more than 150 employees" and later "150 employees" by spring 2022. Exhibit numbering and header/footer text shift across pages in ways that suggest light copy-editing between the 2022 and 2023 versions. Several exhibit tables are reproduced here from the pdftotext layer and their cell text (e.g., the Exhibit 12 priorities matrix) interleaves columns; treat such exhibits as the case's scaffolding rather than as authoritative data. Exhibit 6 is explicitly "intentionally not representative of the typical contributor," since it plots only the top 75 contributors; the >24,000-contributor figure is stated in the exhibit's note. GPT-4chan is dated "late May of 2022" in the opening pages and "May 2022" in the episode section; both refer to the same event.
