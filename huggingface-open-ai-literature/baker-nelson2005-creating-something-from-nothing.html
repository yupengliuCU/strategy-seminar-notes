One-sentence summary

Baker and Nelson study 29 resource-constrained firms facing similar "penurious" environments and show that those able to create something from nothing engage in entrepreneurial bricolage — "making do by applying combinations of the resources at hand to new problems and opportunities" — refusing to enact the limitations built into dominant, objectivist definitions of resource environments; but whether bricolage leads to growth depends on the pattern, with broad "parallel bricolage" locking firms into self-reinforcing communities and stalled growth, while "selective bricolage" across few domains supports routinization and growth.

Reading guide: This paper is in the reading list of the "Hugging Face and the Open-AI Economy" topic as a supporting classic from the entrepreneurship and resource-theory side. Its value here is threefold. First, it supplies a process account of how resource-poor actors build value from inputs others discard — the theoretical vocabulary (resources at hand, testing received limitations, parallel vs. selective bricolage, bricolage capabilities) for interpreting why open-weight models, public checkpoints, and scraped datasets on a hub like Hugging Face function as a modern trove of "odds and ends" that thousands of small developers recombine. Second, it predicts heterogeneity: actors facing the same objective environment respond differently because resource environments are partly enacted, which is exactly the variation the Part I papers document among Hugging Face developers after open releases (Wu, Lin and Li 2025; Khoshbash 2026). Third, it supplies a caution: making do is not automatically a growth strategy — the pattern and discipline of bricolage (selective, domain-limited, eventually routinized) is what separates survival from growth, a distinction relevant to judging small-budget AI deployments (Atlan 2026) and behavioral-search accounts of constrained innovation (Greve 2003). While reading, track the paper's central contrast: firms that refuse to enact limitations (bricoleurs) versus firms that enact them (standard-practice firms), and its sharper internal contrast between parallel and selective bricolage.

1. Background and motivation: why "creating something from nothing" is invisible to open-systems theory

The paper opens with a fact about the population of firms: most organizational entrepreneurship takes place under severe resource constraints. The majority of new firms start with very limited resources, "generally less than $5,000 (U.S. Department of Commerce, 1992)" and either no employees or only a family member to share the work (Ruef, Aldrich, and Carter, 2003). Venture and public capital markets are not widely available to new firms, and even growing firms often cannot attract specific human, financial, or other resources when they need them — a scarcity Penrose (1959) and Aldrich and von Glinow (1992) documented "even within apparently flourishing firms." Yet some entrepreneurs "embrace or even pursue new challenges despite their inability or refusal to attract the new resources these challenges seem to demand," and their firms sometimes survive and flourish (p. 329 of the PDF; ASQ 329).

Baker and Nelson's diagnostic claim is that the dominant "open-systems" models of organization theory cannot explain this. For three decades, open-systems thinking (Boulding, 1956; Katz and Kahn; Scott) had treated organizations as dependent on continuing exchanges with their environments, and resource scarcity as a macro property of the environment — munificence in population ecology (Hannan and Freeman), selection environments in evolutionary accounts (Campbell), objectified normative rules in neo-institutional theory (Meyer and Rowan; Berger and Luckmann), and patterned scarcity of critical resources shaping power in resource dependence studies (Pfeffer and Salancik; Burt). These perspectives, the authors argue, share an underlying assumption:

Resources are what they are, and organizations either have the resources they need or they do not. (Baker & Nelson, 2005, p. 3 of the PDF; ASQ 331)

Under this objectivist view, a constrained firm faces only two rational responses: resource-seeking (attempting to ease constraints, e.g., by raising debt or equity — Berger and Udell; Lerner; Pollock, Porac, and Wade) or some form of avoidance or escape — disbanding (Sutton), downsizing (Barker and Mone; Cameron), or ignoring new opportunities (Lee, Lim, and Tan). A firm acting as if it were not constrained "would simply appear foolish from this perspective." The theoretical gap: open-systems theories built on powerful, objective resource environments "provide an inadequate basis for explaining the survival and occasional success of entrepreneurs who embrace new challenges while employing the constrained set of resources they have at hand" (pp. 3–4 of the PDF; ASQ 331–332).

The constructive alternative comes from Penrose (1959), whom the authors read as a resource-constructivist avant la lettre. Penrose did not deny the objective nature of resources, but argued "that there is a wide scope for judgment," resting her argument on the distinction between resources (physical objects and people) and services (the contributions resources can make to productive operations; a resource is "a bundle of possible services"). Because "no firm ever perceives the complete range of services available from any resource," each firm is idiosyncratic in its relation to its environment; ostensibly similar firms will differ in survival and prosperity; and, at the extreme, the same resource may be worthless to one firm (even treated as waste) but valuable to another that combines it with its own unique set of other resources and services (p. 4 of the PDF; ASQ 332).

The gap Baker and Nelson target is the missing process: Penrose focused on the growth of established manufacturing firms and "did not provide a plausible model of how firms come to construct their unique and idiosyncratic resource environments," and the resource-based view (Conner, 1991; Barney, 1986) "downplays the role of discretion and choice" (p. 4 of the PDF; ASQ 332). Several scholars had recently emphasized that RBV work "has seldom examined the processes of idiosyncratic resource combination that are argued to underlie value creation by firms" (Mosakowski, 2002; Denrell, Fang, and Winter, 2003; Ahuja and Katila, 2004; p. 1 of the PDF; ASQ 329). The paper proposes that Lévi-Strauss's (1967) concept of bricolage fills precisely this process gap.

2. Core concept: bricolage and its elements

Lévi-Strauss (1967), in The Savage Mind, "offered no specific definition of bricolage," and scholars applying his insights had not converged on one either. Drawing on a multidisciplinary literature (anthropology, biology, law, information systems, organization theory) and on their own field observations, Baker and Nelson advance an integrative definition:

[W]e developed an integrative definition of bricolage as making do by applying combinations of the resources at hand to new problems and opportunities. (Baker & Nelson, 2005, p. 5 of the PDF; ASQ 333)

Three elements compose the definition, each elaborated with roots in Lévi-Strauss and in prior applications. First, making do: Lévi-Strauss described the bricoleur's rule as "always to make do with 'whatever is at hand'" (1967: 17; quoted at p. 6 of the PDF; ASQ 334). Making do implies "a bias toward action and active engagement with problems or opportunities rather than lingering over questions of whether a workable outcome can be created from what is at hand." Second, combination of resources for new purposes: bricolage reuses resources "for different applications than those for which they were originally intended," a theme running through the information-systems work of Ciborra and Lanzara (systems built "on the ruins and with the ruins of old systems"), Garud and Karnoe's Danish wind turbine industry, and Jacob's (1977) account of evolution as tinkering that "makes a wing from a leg." Third, the resources at hand: the bricoleur accumulates a set of "odds and ends" — artifacts, skills, ideas — "on the principle that 'they may always come in handy'" (Lévi-Strauss, 1967: 18), rather than acquiring inputs for the well-defined demands of a current project. Baker and Nelson define resources at hand to include inputs "available very cheaply or for free, often because others judge them to be useless or substandard" (p. 6 of the PDF; ASQ 334).

The authors' fieldwork adds a fourth element to received definitions — a refusal to enact limitations. They consistently observed "a conscious and frequently willful tendency for firms in our sample to disregard the limitations of commonly accepted definitions of material inputs, practices, and definitions and standards, insisting instead on trying out solutions, observing, and dealing with the results." Where Weick (1979) had argued that environments are enacted when actors refuse to test limitations, the bricoleurs they observed did the opposite: "actors consciously and consistently tested conventional limitations" (p. 7 of the PDF; ASQ 335). This extension has two theoretical consequences the authors stress: the social construction of resource environments can matter as much as objective limitations, and it begins to open the black box Penrose left when she asserted that firms differ in extracting services from physical inputs.

The three elements together, not separately, produce the striking results. "Bricolage, combining all three elements, occurred much less frequently but with more interesting results" than isolated instances of reliance on resources at hand, which typically had mundane outcomes (p. 12 of the PDF; ASQ 340). The paper illustrates the convergence with Tim Grayson's farm: had his energy source not been at hand, his greenhouse would have resembled others; had he not combined existing inputs, his fish farm would have been ordinary; and "had he accepted the prevailing definitions of abandoned mines as a 'dangerous nuisance' and hydroponic growth media as an 'environment for growing plants, not animals,' without taking any action to test the limitations of these definitions, none of the unique services described above would have come to be" (p. 14 of the PDF; ASQ 342).

3. Methods and evidence: the 29-firm field study

Baker and Nelson studied small, resource-constrained firms in an economically depressed region built on a declining mining economy, a setting they chose because resource constraints, opportunities, and survival threats are common and visible there. The design is explicitly inductive and recursive, following grounded theory procedures (Glaser and Strauss, 1967; Glaser, 1978; Strauss and Corbin; Miles and Huberman; Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 1984; Denzin and Lincoln). The sample was assembled in two waves, and the data collection strategy evolved as the theory developed.

First data source. Community leaders and long-time residents helped identify independent local businesses within a 50-mile radius that had survived while others closed or were supplanted by franchises and nonlocal corporations. This produced visits to and notes on over 40 independent businesses and several hundred hours of fieldwork. As it became apparent that a subset of these firms "was able to carry on operations with even fewer resources than their hard-pressed peers," the authors reoriented toward firms "particularly good at getting by with less," then serendipitously discovered bricolage as a characterization of what they saw. Sixteen firms matching their emerging definition received special attention over about two years, five months of which was a full-time fieldwork sabbatical. As their understanding deepened, they selected for deeper study nine of the original 40 firms that showed no substantial bricolage but were otherwise similar in size, capital intensity, and industry.

Second data source. Four technology-intensive case studies, initiated in 1998 during the design of a study of employment practices in young knowledge-intensive firms, were added to provide cross-case variation in an industry domain the rural sample lacked. These firms were studied through interviews with entrepreneurs, managers, employees, and some customers, suppliers, and competitors. By the end of the study none of the four remained independent — three were sold and one was disbanded — and the authors kept in contact with key informants. These four firms supply the vignettes of intra-firm, employee-driven bricolage (e.g., the billing-system fix described below).

Data collection. The total effort: "a total of 757 hours in the field and 167 interviews" (p. 11 of the PDF; ASQ 339). The authors emphasize being on site and observing real-time activity; for many firms field hours far exceeded formal interviews. Observations and interviews were documented in field notes, research diaries, and summary reports totaling hundreds of pages. With one exception, conversations were not taped, so quoted speech in the paper is reconstructed from written notes made during conversations "rather than direct transcription" (p. 11 of the PDF; ASQ 339). Individual names and a few inconsequential details were altered.

Analytic strategy. Analysis proceeded at two levels: first, building detailed descriptions of particular new problems or opportunities that firms faced "without benefit of new resources," treating each challenge as an individual case; second, comparing cases through matrices coded on simple emergent typologies — importantly, the domains in which firms engaged in bricolage and codings of "testing limitations." Cases were then organized by firm, developing firm-level narratives. The authors circulated working papers, presented to colleagues and students, and cycled repeatedly between data, literature, and analysis. Table 2 of the paper lists each firm with size, principal activities, data-collection effort, and names mentioned in the text; the article's firm numbering (e.g., #1 Terry Starr; #3 Denny Green; #10 Jim Roscoe) is keyed to that table.

The sample in outcome terms. Of the 29 firms, nine exhibited no discernible evidence of bricolage under any working definition. Twenty firms showed substantial bricolage: nine of these were homogeneous in organization and behavior and did not grow (parallel bricolage); the remaining 11 were organizationally diverse and "accounted for eight out of ten cases of firm growth in our sample" (p. 16 of the PDF; ASQ 344). (By the study's own arithmetic, the two remaining growth cases fall among the nine non-bricolage firms.) The four technology-intensive firms all engaged in bricolage of some form, consistent with the "remaining 20" count.

4. Evidence: three contrast cases of bricolage in action

The paper builds the construct through worked examples. Three are worth reading in full; each shows a different domain and different consequence.

Jim Roscoe, electronics repair (#10): one-off recombination. When the market for electronics repair went soft, Roscoe was left with a miscellany of testing equipment, transformers, and components scavenged from televisions. Knowing that coal mines have miles of high-voltage underground power lines damaged by the harsh subterranean environment, he teamed up with a friend who had welding skills Roscoe lacked and combined scavenged components into a simple, rugged tool that let technicians test underground cables without moving or disconnecting them. He sold a few devices, then moved on to unrelated projects. The instance "provided a temporary source of income but did not alter his overall business activities in any substantial way" (p. 13 of the PDF; ASQ 341).

Jason Bond, billing manager (#28): employee bricolage under "requests and permissions." Bond worked for a startup wireless telecommunications company (about 20,000 customers) whose outmoded wire-line billing software could not process the complex billing plans the firm marketed. Clerks manually adjusted billing files. Asked by the vice president of operations to make the system require less manual work — the firm needed to save back-office money to spend on sales and marketing — Bond combined the firm's software with a "homegrown" spreadsheet-based accounts-receivable system from a small import-export firm where he used to work, using self-taught programming skills and trial and error to automate the manual adjustments. The solution let the firm avoid investing in its billing system for almost two years, saved many thousands of dollars in clerks' salaries, and permitted ever more sophisticated pricing deals (p. 13 of the PDF; ASQ 341). This vignette anchors the paper's later discussion of supervisors providing implicit "permissions" that "remove assumed limitations," sending "the message that the firm was willing to live with some shortcuts and problems in exchange for getting the basic task accomplished without the need for additional resources" (p. 22 of the PDF; ASQ 350).

Tim Grayson, farmer (#7): serial bricolage as a growth engine. Grayson's land was crisscrossed by abandoned coal mines — a collapse nuisance holding toxic methane. He and a partner drilled to a mine shaft, acquired a used diesel generator from a local factory, and crudely retrofitted it to burn methane, repeatedly getting blown off his feet when the odorless gas exploded. The generator produced electricity (sold to the local utility via scavenged switchgear) and waste heat; the heat warmed a greenhouse for hydroponic tomatoes; off-peak electricity ran lamps to speed growth; the nutrient-rich trenches then hosted tilapia, whose waste fertilized the tomatoes; finally he began selling excess methane to a natural gas company. Each step emerged from the previous one's byproducts, "with abundant methane still at hand" (pp. 13–14 of the PDF; ASQ 341–342). Grayson Hill Farms is the paper's central exemplar of bricolage that generates growth because it is used serially in one or two domains at a time.

Against these stand the non-bricoleurs. Paul Hatchett's auto shop (#13) modeled itself on the G.M. dealerships where Hatchett worked for more than twenty years — "turning wrenches for a living" — and would not estimate a repair unless confident of doing it "the right way" per shop-manual procedures (changing out rather than repairing faulty parts). Rege Cano's cleaning business (#21) ran on detailed service contracts, standard equipment, and standard dilutions, as Rege explained:

We specify exactly what we will do both verbally and in writing and stick to what we've agreed. There are very few surprises, good or bad. We won't sweep dirt under the rug, but we don't clean underneath the rug unless you specify it in the contract. If the customer specifies he wants the floor stripped and waxed monthly, that's what he gets whether the floor needs it or not. If he doesn't include stripping in the contract, it doesn't get stripped, no matter how much wax is built up. We use standard cleaning compounds at standard dilutions—no less, no more. (Rege Cano, quoted in Baker & Nelson, 2005, p. 12 of the PDF; ASQ 340)

5. Findings: testing and rejecting institutional constraints and definitions

Bricolage's distinctive mechanism is cognitive and institutional: "Making do by testing socially constructed constraints is fundamental to the process by which bricolage creates something from nothing" (p. 14 of the PDF; ASQ 342). The social construction of the resource environment "involved reframing or outright rejection of prevailing definitions of resources," and bricolage "typically appeared to involve a general awareness of existing practices and norms and a conscious willingness to abrogate them."

The extended illustration is Jim Jarvis, owner of a motorcycle repair shop (#25). A customer arrived with a front-wheel problem; Jarvis had him steady the bike on the sidewalk, asked about behavior at different speeds, spun the wheel, said "First we need to know if we have to amputate," trued the wheel by adjusting spoke tension with hand tools, presented no bill, and collected no money — because the customer owned a rare sand-cast engine block Jarvis wanted for a lucrative restoration. Asked to analyze how a dealer would have handled the encounter, Jarvis described at least ten major deviations from standard practice. "Most of these deviations involved rejecting industry practice in order to offer the customer a service unavailable elsewhere, for terms unavailable elsewhere, using a nonstandard technique or tool produced from scrap materials, so that Jim could later realize value from a scrap part to be combined with other materials at hand to produce an expensive and unique show bike" (pp. 14–15 of the PDF; ASQ 342–343). Crucially, Jarvis knew each standard practice and could implement it; he used "conventional definitions of resources and prevailing practices as a foil against which to generate unique services."

The firms that engaged in bricolage created something from nothing in five domains, which the paper's Table 3 organizes: physical inputs, labor, skills, customers/markets, and the institutional and regulatory environment. The mechanism in each domain is the same — refusing to enact the limitations embedded in dominant definitions:

The institutional domain has an openly legal-gray side, which the authors report without glossing over: Denny Green substituted a length of exhaust pipe for a fouled catalytic converter (saving the customer money, increasing air pollution, risking fines), performed air-conditioning repairs while venting freon "with abandon (also illegal)," and a customer commented that his van "ran much better after Denny disconnected the emission controls." Hatchett's and Russ Taylor's shops, by contrast, refused even to recharge a system with the smallest detectable freon leak. "This disdain for codes and standards was neither universal in parallel bricolage nor completely absent in the other firms but seemed much more commonplace and taken for granted by proprietors, employees, and even customers of firms engaged in parallel bricolage" (p. 17 of the PDF; ASQ 345).

6. Findings: parallel versus selective bricolage and firm growth

The article's central empirical payoff is the distinction between two organizational patterns of bricolage, which differ sharply in their relationship to growth. Parallel bricolage is "marked by multiple ongoing projects relying on bricolage" (p. 16 of the PDF; ASQ 343); selective bricolage uses the same resource-construction logic but deliberately, temporarily, or in few domains. The nine parallel-bricolage firms were strikingly homogeneous; the 11 selective firms "were much more diverse organizationally, and they accounted for eight out of ten cases of firm growth in our sample." None of the parallel firms grew.

6.1 Parallel bricolage: a self-reinforcing, growth-limiting pattern

Parallel bricolage firms shared reinforcing regularities across five dimensions (the paper's Table 4 columns). First, a diverse resource trove: they did "a great deal of scavenging," defined as acquiring and extracting "usage from goods that others eschew or do not intend to use" (Starr and MacMillan, 1990: 84). Denny Green's repair shop (#3), doing about the same business as Hatchett's non-bricolage shop (#13), drew most of what it needed from a trove requiring at least double the square footage of his workspace — three pole barns of used parts plus more than twenty junked vehicles. Terry Starr's business (#1) was the extreme case, with storage dominating the workshop area by at least four to one. Second, broad self-taught skills: proprietors and employees lacked formal training in their trades and applied informally acquired skills "with little regard to craft or professional boundaries" (Roscoe's electrical career began when he took a broken TV to a friend and stayed to watch). Third, nonconformity to craft standards, industry practices, and legal regulations (Section 5 above). Fourth, multiplex ties: customers became friends and vice versa; customers contributed labor, located scavenging opportunities, and brokered input acquisitions; suppliers became customers. This contrasts with arm's-length policies in other firms (Mike Mathews's "strictly business" attitude after bail bonds and defaulted loans; Homer Smith swearing off partnerships with relatives). Multiplex ties "sustained the exchange of inexpensive non-standard products and services by creating obligations on the part of firms to provide ongoing fixes and repairs and matching obligations on the part of customers to moderate their expectations about reliability." George Love (#2) estimated that about half the houses he rehabilitated and sold would otherwise have been torn down, and that many buyers "would otherwise still be renting or living with relatives": in effect these firms "created" customers from people who otherwise would not be part of the market. Fifth, a mutually reinforcing pattern: scavenging feeds the trove; the trove plus flexible standards and permissive networks encourages broad challenges; new challenges broaden self-taught skills; broader skills expand what might come in handy, which is scavenged and stored.

The same pattern explains why parallel bricolage firms did not grow. They "flitted from project to project as parts became available and interest waxed and waned," with priority depending on tie strength and customer urgency; this sustained a constant flow of work but "provided no focus on discovery or exploitation of opportunities for growth." Novel projects consumed time in learning, experimentation, and fabrication, plus the "coaxing" — ongoing attempts to extract additional service from worn, failing, or obsolete resources — that is "anathema to the establishment of reliable impersonal routines" (p. 19 of the PDF; ASQ 347). Where ties were dense enough, the pattern hardened into a community of practice (Brown and Duguid, 1991) and a bricolage identity (Albert and Whetten, 1985), in which the permissive environment became "a socially constructed fact." At Jim Jarvis's Cycle Tech (#25), members of the multiplex network converged on the shop in the hours before closing:

Jim said that during this "beer time, the place turns into a bar without stools." (Baker & Nelson, 2005, p. 20 of the PDF; ASQ 348)

The identity cut both ways: firms took pride in making do and disdained firms that could not, but "precisely this ability to call forth resources out of thin air created an organizational form and identity that isolated parallel bricolage firms from richer markets and impeded the development of organizational focus and routines that might support growth and profitability" (p. 21 of the PDF; ASQ 349). The authors connect the over-embeddedness dynamic to Uzzi (1997) and to the Scottish knitwear producers of Porac, Thomas, and Baden-Fuller (1989).

6.2 Selective bricolage: three ways to escape the trap and grow

Selective bricolage firms used the same constructivist logic but avoided self-reinforcement. The paper documents three variants.

(a) Temporary broad bricolage, then rejection. Dave Compton bought an ailing trailer park (#22) that served a "troublesome clientele" and was a mechanical and aesthetic disaster. In an environment where local codes were rarely enforced, Compton initially made do like his predecessors — stripping trailers for parts, refurbishing better units, demolishing the worst, rearranging units so larger ones clustered visibly — and built network ties by letting some clients occupy abandoned trailers for free if they repaired them. Lacking title to abandoned trailers, he found "the sheriff doesn't ask to see the title to the trailer, only the moving permit." As his cash position improved, Compton abandoned most bricolage: he implemented rental contracts "detailed and almost draconian" in their tenant-conduct terms, insisted on compliance with state and local regulations, and eventually conducted repairs with new parts and professional service firms "even when good used parts and cheaper labor were readily available." Vacancy fell, police calls fell, and Compton raised rents and pursued a modular-home dealership (p. 22 of the PDF; ASQ 350).

(b) Narrow, temporary departmental bricolage via requests and permissions. In four cases, supervisors asked employees to solve substantial problems without spending money, providing implicit or explicit "permissions" to cut corners. Three of the four helped the firm grow by freeing resources for areas senior managers viewed as strategic. But this form was "self-arresting": requests were narrow, and in two cases the solutions required so much coaxing that maintenance came to dominate the employee's job. Jason Bond's job "changed over time to the much narrower position of billing system supervisor," and Tim Reno (#26), who built an almost fault-tolerant IT architecture by bricolage with Bobby Crane, eventually quit, in large part, he said, "because I was tired of trying to make that half-a**ed system run and then trying to explain why it couldn't do everything the real [fault-tolerant] systems can do" (p. 23 of the PDF; ASQ 351).

(c) Exploratory serial bricolage confined to one or two domains. Grayson Hill Farms (#7) used bricolage serially — methane cogeneration, hydroponic tomatoes, tilapia, then methane sales — but "never became locked into a pattern of parallel bricolage, because it always limited its simultaneous attempts to create something from nothing to one or two domains," servicing customers at arm's length and buying new equipment once he had "figured out we could make money from mine methane." Biggs Dig (#4) is the fullest example: after years of mine maintenance subcontracting, he learned that reclamation standards were inconsistently enforced and that abandoned properties with environmental problems could be acquired almost for free, along with surplus mining equipment. He developed unconventional cleanup methods; at a toxic tailing pond he recovered carbon at extraordinary rates — for two years his company reported 31.2 and 23.2 tons of carbon per hour of labor versus reported state averages of 4.6 and 3.9 — then turned the restored, high-quality water into a fish farm that attracted national attention and a high-profile environmental award, secured a state contract to raise trout, acquired and reclaimed more mine lands, and founded a trucking company. Unlike the parallel bricoleurs, Dig was "cautious and scrupulous about institutional and legal regulations," and his network "did not exhibit the same degree of multiplexity or generate the same demands and permissiveness" (pp. 23–24 of the PDF; ASQ 351–352).

Selective firms created something from nothing in the physical-input domain (9 of 11) "without accumulating a diverse resource trove (only 3 of 11) and without multiplex ties (only 2 of 11)" — the authors suspect these firms exploited "neglected but plentiful undifferentiated physical inputs that did not require or benefit from storage and organization as a trove," and that the dearth of multiplex ties "helped selective bricoleurs avoid the social entanglements that constricted margins, required special deals, and prevented migration from poorer to richer client bases" (pp. 27–28 of the PDF; ASQ 355–356).

7. Toward a process model of bricolage and firm growth

Figure 1 of the paper induces a process model: a penurious environment (new challenges without new resources) presents firms with three alternatives — resource seeking, avoiding new challenges, or bricolage. Bricolage counteracts enacted limitations and generates something from nothing across the five domains, while eliciting creativity, improvisation, combinative capabilities, tolerance for ambiguity and setbacks, and social and network skills. The fork that matters for growth runs through whether bricolage becomes parallel (mutually reinforcing patterns → a permissive community of practice and bricolage identity → no growth) or selective (enacting limitations in some domains → routinization and broader, richer, more demanding markets → growth). The authors flag that some paths in the figure are "suggested by our results but call for additional research"; dashed lines denote processes not explored or only suggestive (p. 25 of the PDF; ASQ 353).

Two discussion themes deepen the model. First, the pattern of enacting versus testing limitations is the theoretical mechanism: "Our finding that the patterns of enacting or testing and counteracting limitations shape the relationship between bricolage and firm growth represents an important theoretical contribution to our understanding of entrepreneurship under resource constraints" (p. 28 of the PDF; ASQ 356). Second, firms differ in what the authors label their bricolage capabilities — "firms may differ systematically in their ability to apply bricolage skillfully." They expect such capabilities to confer survival advantages when resource stocks are low, including "in the earliest days of the emergence of most new industries" and in crowded markets when a decline in available resources drives resource-dependent competitors to exit (pp. 28–29 of the PDF; ASQ 356–357). These advantages are speculative extensions beyond the data, and the authors say so.

8. Discussion: how bricolage relates to improvisation, jobs, and value creation

The discussion situates bricolage against adjacent constructs. On improvisation, prior work had often treated the two as interchangeable, but Miner, Bassoff, and Moorman (2001) define improvisation as "the deliberate and substantive fusion of the design and execution of a novel production" and argue improvisers typically engage in bricolage because there is no temporal gap in which to seek other resources. Baker and Nelson's data add a reverse causal path: "in our study, bricolage often appeared as the cause of improvisation," because executing even a rough prior design ("often 'a mere sketch,' as Lévi-Strauss suggested") hits snags when idiosyncratic at-hand resources misbehave — Tim Grayson getting blown off his feet is the clearest example — and the surprise generates improvisation rather than a return to the drawing board. Bricolage thus appears both as a consequence and as a trigger of improvisation, and the authors candidly rate the broader claim — that bricolage elicits creativity, improvisation, and social skills — as "perhaps the weakest part of our model" (pp. 32–33 of the PDF; ASQ 360–361).

On jobs and capabilities, the requests-and-permissions process offers "a quicker route" to the job evolution Miner (1987, 1990) documented: supervisors ask employees to take on challenging tasks with no new resources; employees respond with bricolage; and jobs evolve accordingly. This is "a theoretically and practically important case of refusing to enact limitations ... in the specific domain of available skills" (p. 29 of the PDF; ASQ 357).

On value creation and appropriation, the paper makes a pointed contrast with Schumpeterian recombination. Schumpeter (1934, 1939) held that new combinations withdraw resources from their prior productive uses, and Moran and Ghoshal (1999: 393) add that "in all new combinations the loss in realizable value is always more certain and often more immediate than is any gain in potential value." Bricolage sometimes creates value without withdrawal, by combining resources "generally perceived to be worthless (or worse, a liability)" — toxic mine waste in the cases of Biggs Dig and Tim Grayson — or by calling forth hidden resources (Bond's boss neither knew about the spreadsheet system nor anticipated Bond's self-taught programming). Yet the RBV promise of appropriability mostly fails in their data:

Among the firms we studied, bricolage was much more important as a tool of value creation than as a tool of value appropriation. (Baker & Nelson, 2005, p. 34 of the PDF; ASQ 362)

Novel bricolage solutions might seem hard to replicate by rivals lacking the same resources and skills, but "most of the bricolage-based innovations we observed would have been relatively easy for other firms to copy" (p. 34 of the PDF; ASQ 362).

9. Position in the literature

Within entrepreneurship and strategy research. The paper's most direct combat is with the discovery tradition in entrepreneurship theory. Work building on Austrian economics (Eckhardt and Shane, 2003; Kirzner, 1973, 1997, 2000) and the influential Shane and Venkataraman (2000) framing portrays opportunities as preexisting, objective phenomena that are discovered, evaluated, and only then elicit resource mobilization. Baker and Nelson's firms challenge both the temporal ordering and the objectivism: "the processes of discovering opportunities and enacting resources were often one and the same, with both the resource environment and the opportunity environment idiosyncratic to the specific firm and constructed through processes of bricolage" (p. 30 of the PDF; ASQ 358). Against Stevenson and Jarillo's (1990) entrepreneurial management as "pursuit of opportunity without regard to resources controlled," bricolage offers a corrective slogan:

Entrepreneurial bricolage might well be characterized as the pursuit of opportunity through close regard to the resources at hand. (Baker & Nelson, 2005, p. 31 of the PDF; ASQ 359)

They also re-read Shane's (2000) study of the eight entrepreneurs who saw eight distinct uses for one MIT-licensed technology: those entrepreneurs, they argue, were "alert to resources as much as they were alert to opportunities," since prior knowledge in their cases was "prior knowledge about the resources at hand." Opportunity discovery could therefore be "interactive and social, rather than a process requiring an individual epiphany," as in Tim Reno and Bobby Crane's iterative, conversational construction of a fault-tolerant system — a direct counter to heroic-individualist accounts (Kirzner, 2000; Venkataraman, 1997). The paper aligns with enactment-based views of opportunities (Gartner, Bird, and Starr, 1992; Gartner, Carter, and Hills, 2003). Within strategy, it fills the process gap in Penrose (1959) and the resource-based view (Wernerfelt, 1984; Barney, 1991): RBV had stressed idiosyncratic combinations as the source of advantage but left unexplained "how these recombinations actually come about"; bricolage is that process, though — as the paper stresses — more often creating value than appropriating it.

Within organization theory. Conceptually the paper is a constructivist intervention against the objectivist core shared by resource dependence (Pfeffer and Salancik), population ecology (Hannan and Freeman), and neo-institutionalism (Meyer and Rowan): where those views give resources "a stubborn facticity," Baker and Nelson show resource environments being enacted and re-enacted. It builds on Weick's (1979) enactment but inverts his "enactment of limitations" insight, and it connects to embeddedness research (Uzzi, 1997; Portes and Sensenbrenner, 1993), showing an over-embeddedness trap symmetrical to Uzzi's under- and over-embeddedness curves, and to the identity literature (Albert and Whetten, 1985; Gioia, Schultz, and Corley, 2000). Its intellectual neighbors include the bricolage applications the authors synthesize — improvisation research (Weick, 1993a, 1993b; Moorman and Miner, 1998; Miner, Bassoff, and Moorman, 2001; Baker, Miner, and Eesley, 2003; Cunha et al., 2001), institutional and political bricolage (Campbell, 1997; Stark, 1996; Lanzara, 1998; Hull, 1991), information-systems bricolage (Ciborra, 1996, 2002; Lanzara, 1999), technology brokering (Hargadon and Sutton, 1997), Garud and Karnoe's (2003) distributed-agency account of the Danish wind industry (whose bricoleurs beat U.S. "breakthrough" competitors), evolutionary tinkering (Jacob, 1977), and evolutionary biology's parsimonious-creative "bricolage" (Duboule and Wilkins, 1998). The study also speaks to slack-resource research: Mishina, Pollock, and Porac (2004) and George (2005) ask when slack resources help or hurt, and bricolage research offers the mirror-image question of what firms do with no slack.

Connection to the Part I core on Hugging Face and the open-AI economy. Baker and Nelson never study software platforms, so the following links are interpretive extensions, not claims of the paper. (1) Behavioral foundations (Greve 2003). Greve's behavioral theory explains search as a response to performance below aspirations, in shipbuilding R&D: firms vary their search when performance signals change, and slack and problemistic search are local and path-dependent. Baker and Nelson study the resource side of the same behavioral logic — a "penurious environment" is an aspiration-relevant problem without the resource flow to solve it in the standard way — and add a third behavioral response (bricolage) to the two (seeking, avoidance) that resource-constrained actors have in rational-objectivist accounts. Both papers explain heterogeneous responses to similar objective environments through firm-specific, locally informed processes rather than through environment-as-destiny; Greve's problemistic search finds its resource-side cousin in the bricoleur's "testing received limitations" triggered by a challenge at hand. (2) Smaller developers after open releases (Wu, Lin and Li 2025, ICIS). Wu, Lin and Li document, with a difference-in-differences design around the Llama 2 open release on Hugging Face, that smaller and less-resourced developers increased downstream innovation once a powerful open artifact became available. In Baker and Nelson's vocabulary, open weights are a modern "resource at hand": abundant, cheap, and judged by others (frontier labs) as a substitute for rather than the real thing. The ICIS finding that the response is heterogeneous — and strongest for smaller developers — is what a bricolage account predicts: firms differ in "bricolage capabilities" to exploit identical at-hand inputs, and whether fine-tuning an open model leads to growth depends on whether it is used selectively (as a bounded input in a product) or as an unbounded parallel activity. (3) Small-budget deployments (Atlan 2026). Atlan's practitioner evidence on 200 B2B deployments (2022–2025), including positive ROI from small-budget projects built on open-source components, commodity infrastructure, and limited proprietary data, reads as bricolage "in the wild" — teams making do with combinations of free or cheap inputs to render services they could not afford to build from scratch. The paper's caution applies directly: "something from nothing is in many ways simply an extreme version of more from less," so a bricolage lens should help explain differential outcomes among budget-constrained AI adopters, and its warning is that undisciplined parallel bricolage stalls growth while selective, routinizable use scales (a caveat the non-peer-reviewed Atlan evidence cannot test). (4) Broader ecosystem papers. Laufer, Oderinwale and Kleinberg's (2025) anatomy of two million models on Hugging Face shows a long tail of micro-contributors recombining public checkpoints — bricolage at population scale; Greenstein et al.'s (2022) Hugging Face case shows the platform institutionalizing the "at hand" trove (a hub of reusable models, datasets, and pipelines); and the private-collective logic of von Hippel and von Krogh (2003) plus the "democratizing innovation" argument of von Hippel (2005) explain why such commons keep accumulating odds and ends anyone may use. Khoshbash (2026) and Jin et al. (2026) extend the theme to specialization and to policy-induced ecosystems, where constrained entrants bricolage with open foundation models. What Baker and Nelson uniquely contribute to this constellation is the warning that an abundant trove does not by itself produce growth — the pattern of use, the selective enactment of limitations, and the eventual routinization into arm's-length markets are what convert making do into building.

10. Limitations

Boundaries the authors state themselves. The paper is explicit that its work is "exploratory, and we relied on intensive study of a limited, non-representative sample of firms," drawn from a depressed, mining-based region plus four technology cases; "as is the case in all grounded theory development, additional work will be required to test the relationships we theorized and whether the concepts we have introduced, particularly the idea of constructing resources, are useful in other contexts" (p. 34 of the PDF; ASQ 362). Parts of Figure 1 rest on "suggestive" data only. The authors note that quotes come from field notes, not transcription; that the four technology firms all exited as independent entities (three sold, one disbanded); that the relationship between bricolage and elicited capacities (creativity, improvisation, social skills) "remains perhaps the weakest part of our model"; and that bricolage rarely produced appropriable advantage — most innovations were easy to copy. They also flag that "something from nothing" is the extreme form of a more general phenomenon — "more from less" — so generalizability beyond penurious environments remains to be shown. Claims about survival advantages in poor environments and about industry emergence are presented as expectations to test, not findings.

Independent judgment (layered and labeled):

  • Author's claim: Resource environments are socially constructed in part; bricolage — defined as making do by combining resources at hand and refusing to enact received limitations — is how constrained entrepreneurs create something from nothing; and parallel versus selective patterns of bricolage shape growth outcomes. These are explicit assertions in the text.
  • Evidence: The evidence is qualitative interpretation of field notes and reconstructed quotations across 29 firms, with the quantitative-looking Table 4 cells coding the authors' own judgments. Firm growth is asserted ("did not grow during our research") without reported revenue, employment, or margin metrics in the text I read; "ten cases of firm growth" is likewise an author-coded count. The central contrast is vivid but rests on interpretive coding in one depressed region plus four technology cases.
  • Inference: The causal reading — that parallel bricolage causes stalled growth while selectivity enables growth — is stronger than the evidence: firms that grew may simply have been able to abandon bricolage, or selectivity may proxy for unmeasured differences in managerial quality or opportunity sets, and the rural sample contains few genuinely high-growth trajectories to begin with. That bricolage and improvisation are bidirectionally causal is an inference from a handful of episodes. The translation of this framework to Hugging Face developers, open-weights fine-tuning, and small-budget AI deployments (Section 9) is entirely my extension: the paper contains no digital-platform or AI evidence.
  • Open questions: Is "selective bricolage" a deliberate capability, an emergent property of context, or a post-hoc description of firms that happened to grow? How would one measure bricolage at scale for quantitative testing (the construct's visibility in field notes does not translate easily to administrative data)? Where is the boundary between bricolage and mere improvisation or ad hoc problem solving — and between bricolage and resource-seeking when scavenging itself is a deliberate acquisition strategy? Under what conditions do troves become liabilities (the paper notes troves varied in organization and maintenance) and do multiplex ties tip from asset to trap? Does bricolage operate at the community or ecosystem level (as in Garud and Karnoe's distributed agency) rather than only the firm level — the relevant question for hub-based innovation — and how do "requests and permissions" generalize to organizations that formally reward or punish deviation from standards? These are questions the paper leaves open, and later work on bricolage and effectuation has only partly answered them.

11. Conclusion: takeaways for resource-constrained innovation

Baker and Nelson make three moves that have aged well. First, they give entrepreneurship theory a constructivist alternative to "environment-as-destiny": what looks like a hard resource constraint is partly an enacted one, and firms differ in whether they test the institutional definitions embedded in that constraint. Second, they supply the missing process story beneath Penrose's resources-versus-services distinction and the RBV's idiosyncratic combinations — bricolage as the mechanism by which firms generate heterogeneous value from ostensibly identical inputs. Third, they refuse a romantic conclusion: the same capability that lets firms "get by" can lock them into a community of practice and identity that suppresses growth. Growth requires selectivity — using bricolage in few domains, eventually enacting limitations, routinizing, and reaching broader markets. The general lesson for resource-constrained innovators — from rural repair shops to developers fine-tuning open weights on a model hub — is that making do is a capability to be managed, not an identity to be embraced: the pattern of bricolage, not its mere presence, separates survival from growth.
