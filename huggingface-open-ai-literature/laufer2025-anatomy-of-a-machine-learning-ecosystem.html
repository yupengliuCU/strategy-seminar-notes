One-sentence summary

The machine learning ecosystem is organized by adaptation chains: the 1.86 million public models on Hugging Face form "family trees" in which a comparatively small number of pre-trained base models are repeatedly fine-tuned, quantized, adapted, and merged by downstream developers, and the traits of derivative models mutate fast and in ordered, directional ways — toward permissive and copyleft licenses, English-only language support, thinner and increasingly auto-generated documentation, and downstream tasks that recapitulate the pretrain → fine-tune → align pipeline.

Reading guide: This paper is the structural or anatomical baseline for the "Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy" topic: it tells you who actually builds on whom on the largest open-model platform, at population scale. The strategy and economics readings in this topic library presuppose exactly such a map. Greenstein, Yue, Herman & Gulick (2022) describe how Hugging Face was founded and positioned as the platform "serving AI" — this paper supplies the resulting division of labor between a few base-model providers and a long tail of adapters. Wu, Yang, Xu, Wang, Le & Lo (2025, "Learning From the Best") ask which model and documentation features predict popularity on the Hub — this paper shows that documentation itself is a trait that atrophies and standardizes along adaptation chains. Longpre et al. (2025, "Economies of Open Intelligence") and White et al. (2024, Model Openness Framework, MOF) treat licenses and openness as the key institutional variables of open AI — this paper documents the license dynamics that MOF's taxonomy freezes in time (derivatives drift from restrictive commercial terms toward permissive and copyleft ones). Read it to fix in mind the empirical facts — scale, concentration of reuse, drift directions, thin documentation — that the topic's economic arguments use as premises.

1. Background and question

The paper's opening observation is that generative AI is not so much built as inherited and modified: the dominant development pattern is to take pre-trained models and adapt them to downstream tasks. The authors' point of departure is that this pattern is widely asserted but rarely measured at the level of the whole community:

Many have observed that the development and deployment of generative machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) models follow a distinctive pattern in which pre-trained models are adapted and fine-tuned for specific downstream tasks. This process is responsible for much of the powerful functionality we see in current AI systems, but there is limited empirical work that examines the actual structure of these interactions across the AI development community broadly construed.
— Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025), abstract (p. 1 of the PDF)

To study that structure, they treat Hugging Face — described as "the leading provider of open source models" and "a platform that hosts models, datasets, libraries, and other materials" — as a peer-production platform. The research question is anatomical in the literal sense: what do the "family trees" connecting derivative models to their parents look like, at what scale, in what shapes, and with what consequences for how model attributes (licenses, languages, tasks, documentation) change as models are copied and modified?

Three contributions structure the paper (pp. 3–4): (1) it builds the largest dataset to date of the open-model development ecosystem, a snapshot of all publicly available Hugging Face models with the topological relations among them (whether models "finetune, merge, or adapt from one or more other models"); (2) it tracks "genetic" traits encoded in metadata and model cards, finding that models of the same family resemble each other, but in a way that departs from standard biological models of asexual reproduction — siblings resemble each other more than parent–child pairs do, implying mutation is fast and non-random; (3) it studies the direction of trait mutations via network analysis, finding that license, language, and task mutations are "overwhelmingly acyclic," so that traits can be ordered from genetically upstream to downstream — for example, translation models sit upstream of text-generation models, and llama3 licenses sit upstream of apache-2.0 licenses.

2. Data and methods

2.1 The dataset: 1.86 million models and their metadata

The empirical object is the Hugging Face model hub, described as "the largest public repository of machine learning models, containing 1.86 million models at the time of this study." Collection proceeded in two stages (pp. 5–6): first the authors used the Hugging Face "model" API — whose lists are capped at 1,000 models per call — with pagination to iterate over every model and collect its features and relationships as JSON; second, they fetched the full text of every model's model card through individual per-model API calls, a far more data-intensive stage. All calls ran through the authors' registered Hugging Face accounts and were conducted "in consultation with employees at Hugging Face, including Hugging Face's in-house librarian." The complete dataset (with cards) uses on the order of 10 GB; the dataset without model cards is around 500 MB.

Each model's metadata JSON is the analytical atom. It records the model id (author + name), likes, trendingScore (a trait Hugging Face defines for ranking models), downloads, the pipeline tag or "task" (e.g., feature-extraction, text-generation, image-classification), the library name, createdAt, and a tags list whose structured prefixes carry much of the genetic information: tags beginning with base_model:finetune: link a fine-tuned model to its parent's model id; license: tags record the license; arxiv: tags link accompanying papers; and languages appear as ISO-639 codes. The authors treat these text snippets as the models' "DNA" because they can be compared across generations.

Model cards — the main free-text documentation on the hub — are the second source of "genetic" information (pp. 6–7). They are long, unstructured, and optional: 67.04% of models have an associated card, and the 1,247,149 available cards average 3,575.60 characters (≈ 436.06 words) with a median of 2,073.0 characters (≈ 238.0 words). The range is extreme — from a minimum of 11 characters to a maximum of 18,289,454 characters (≈ 2,813,762 words) — which the authors note means a small number of extremely verbose cards drive the average.

2.2 Building the model-adaptation graph

The relational core of the dataset is the set of documented derivative links between models. The paper represents the hub "as a set of phylogenies—branching trees rooted in base models, where nodes correspond to individual models and edges denote parent–child relationships." A single example fixes the mechanics (pp. 2–3). IBM's ibm-granite/granite-3.1 has a family tree of 147 models — the base model plus 146 derivatives contributed mostly by open-source developers:

Every black dot represents a distinct, publicly available model, and every directed edge represents a path from a parent model to a derivative model—that is, a finetune, quantization, or adapter. The base model produced by IBM spawned eighteen children, of which five are finetunes and the rest are quantizations. Only two of the children—both finetunes—spawned further generations of children.
— Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025), p. 3 of the PDF

Four kinds of derivative relation appear in the paper: finetunes, quantizations, adapters, and merges, all "documented" (logged) on the hub — the paper's own figure caption describes edges as "different forms of derivative models that are documented as having finetuned, quantized, adapter or merged existing models." The concrete detection mechanism the text describes is the metadata's base_model:finetune: tag linking a child to its parent's id. Three method points follow from this design and are stated explicitly in the text:

  • Only logged relationships are visible. The authors acknowledge as a limitation that they "only account for models that have logged fine-tuning relationships on Hugging Face"; many models may be related without such records (e.g., parameter variants Qwen/Qwen1.5-0.5B and Qwen/Qwen1.5-1.8B are separate base models, not family members).
  • Merges break the tree. A merged model has two or more parents, so graphs containing merges are not trees. The paper calls merges "a form of sexual reproduction," finds them "considerably more rare than other forms of reproduction," and sets them aside for the tree-based genetic analysis; when connected components must be treated as trees (Appendix §8), it "use[s] only the first-listed parent for merged models."
  • Fine-tuning is the branching form of reproduction. For the similarity analysis the authors keep only fine-tuning edges and omit merges, adaptations, and quantizations, "because these forms of reproduction are far less likely to branch and further support their own offspring compared to finetunes."

2.3 Measuring "genetic" similarity and mutation rates

Taking the biological metaphor operationally, the authors define genetic similarity over the models' tokenized metadata (and, in robustness checks, model-card text) using three measures: (i) normalized Levenshtein distance, a character-level edit distance; (ii) cosine similarity of bag-of-words term-frequency vectors; and (iii) cosine similarity of TF-IDF vectors — the measure reported in the body of the paper, because mutations in metadata are categorical shifts between terms rather than edits between letters (Appendix §6.2 explains why term-based measures are preferred: "the difference between the snippets 'license: mit' and 'license: gemma' should not depend on how many letters 'mit' and 'gemma' share"). All six measure × artifact combinations (three measures on metadata, three on model cards) agree in qualitative conclusions.

Because local family structures (siblings, cousins, uncles/nephews) can occur combinatorially many times in a large tree — one model with 500 children alone yields $\binom{500}{2} = 124{,}750$ sibling pairs — the authors do not enumerate every structure. Instead they specify a condition that identifies whether a node or edge lies in a given subgraph shape, count the multiplicity of pairs each node contributes, and draw a weighted sample of qualifying nodes to estimate average similarities over all subgraphs of size 2, 3, and 4 (Appendix §10 lists the eight sampled shapes, their sampling conditions, and their weights; the number of occurrences spans from 3,470,193,356,870 unordered model pairs for the random baseline down to 10,965 for the rarest four-node structure).

The mutation rate over a trait set is defined formally (Definition 7.1, Appendix §7): along a directed edge $(i,j)$ with trait groups $t_i, t_j$, the edge mutation rate is $m(i,j) = 1 - |t_i \cap t_j| / |t_i \cup t_j|$, and the rate over the whole set is the average of $m$ over edges. When each model has exactly one value in the category (as with licenses and tasks), this reduces to 0 when parent and child agree and 1 when they differ; for multi-valued traits such as languages it allows partial mutation.

3. Core descriptive findings: size, concentration, growth, and usage

The paper's descriptive picture of the hub (Figure 2, discussed pp. 6–7) is strongly skewed: a few values dominate each categorical trait, and reuse and attention are concentrated on a small set of models. Table 1 assembles the findings the text reports, category by category. The most important structural points are that reported licenses are dominated by permissive terms (Apache-2.0 and MIT alone exceed 60% of all reported licenses, out of 162 unique license types of which 98 are platform-standardized), that text generation is the modal task, that English is by far the dominant language compatibility (over 75% of models that document any language mark English; Chinese is second at 4.4%), and that reuse and downloads concentrate on individual hubs — black-forest-labs/FLUX.1-dev is the model with the most children, and Falconsai/nsfw_image_detection (an explicit-image detector, plausibly used for content moderation) is the most downloaded model in the snapshot.

Growth over time. Using the createdAt field, the paper plots the growth of the ten largest family trees over time (Figure 3). The caption reports that the growth of the ten largest connected components "reveals 'S-curve' adoption patterns, analogous to other domains with diffusion over a network." The family-tree view of growth is thus one of diffusion curves: families are adopted, expand, and saturate over the platform's lifetime. (The paper does not report per-year model counts in the extracted text; the S-curve claim is caption-level.)

Depth and shape. Appendix §8 reports on the "structural virality" of connected components (a metric taken from Goel et al., 2016, which separates broadcast-style diffusion trees from viral ones): structural virality "is quite low in many graphs which tend to be broadcast in nature, but there nonetheless exists considerable branching, with a number of models reaching depths of nearly 40 generations." So the ecosystem is simultaneously broadcast-like (one base reaches many) and deep (chains can run ~40 generations long).

4. The structure of adaptation: family resemblance, siblings, and chains

The core structural result is that proximity in the family tree predicts similarity of traits. Comparing TF-IDF cosine similarity over metadata for sampled family structures (Figure 5) against a baseline of randomly paired models, the authors find that every observed family tie raises attribute similarity above the random baseline. They summarize their findings as three heuristics (pp. 10–12):

  1. Same family. Models in the same family tree are significantly more similar than randomly paired models.
  2. Low generational divide. Pairs one generation apart (parent/child, uncle/nephew) are more similar than pairs two generations apart (grandparent/grandchild), which are in turn more similar than great-grandparent/great-grandchild pairs.
  3. Network distance. Similarity falls with the number of edges between two models — the pattern a genetic model of mutation-based asexual reproduction would predict; uncle/nephew pairs are less similar than parent/child pairs in the same subgraph structures, although generational divide generally outweighs network distance. (The one reported exception: in two measures of the four-node subgraph, a parent–child pair at network distance one shows higher similarity than a sibling pair at distance two.)

The most counter-intuitive finding in this section is about siblings. Under a model of asexual reproduction with random mutation, each child should resemble the shared parent at least as much as it resembles its siblings:

If we imagine each child model in a family inheriting the parent's genes subject to some rate of random mutation, siblings should be more related to their parent than each other, on average. We observe the opposite, suggesting that there is some directional effect of fine-tuning whereby all children tend to depart in attributes from their parents, on average, in characteristically similar ways.
— Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025), p. 10 of the PDF

In plain terms: children are more similar to each other than to their parent — not because they inherit the parent faithfully, but because fine-tuning pushes all children in the same characteristic direction, away from the base. Mutations are fast and directed, not slow and random. Figure 6 (image-only) renders this sibling-vs-parent-child comparison directly.

The granite example (Section 2.2) shows the qualitative chain structure this creates: a base model produces a mix of finetunes (which branch) and quantizations (which do not); one second-generation finetune "produced many more offspring than the other," and from that ancestor several further generations branch. As of the snapshot the granite tree spans five generations, with no structural reason for growth to stop. Why does all this matter for behavior? The paper draws an analogy to kin selection: evolutionary theory predicts altruism toward close relatives and competition with distant ones (the example given is gray wolves, from Cassidy & McIntyre, 2016). The authors observe that the open ML ecosystem mixes fierce competition with altruistic acts such as releasing weights "to enable third-party development," and suggest — as an open direction rather than a result — that family structure could predict altruistic and competitive behavior across the ecosystem.

5. Evolution of traits: directed drift and ordered transitions

Section 4 of the paper shifts from overall similarity to the evolution of individual traits — the categorical features license, language, and task, plus numerical attributes such as document length and number of languages. Across all trait categories the authors report two general empirical observations (p. 12):

  • Directedness ("drift"). For any two trait values $(i, j)$, it is typically the case that $i$ overwhelmingly mutates to $j$, or $j$ overwhelmingly mutates to $i$, rather than traffic flowing in both directions.
  • Orderedness. The orientations of these directed mutations are mutually consistent: over the oriented "typical transition" graph, the authors find orderings of trait values that account for virtually all observed drift directions. (Finding such an ordering is an NP-hard integer-programming problem in general; the authors stress that they succeed because the data's natural ordering exists, not by luck.)
Licenses counter-intuitively drift from restrictive, commercial licenses towards permissive or copyleft licenses; models evolve from multi-lingual compatibility towards English-only compatibility; and model cards reduce in length and standardize by turning, more often, to templates and automatically generated text.
— Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025), abstract (p. 1 of the PDF)

5.1 Licenses: drift from commercial and restrictive to permissive and copyleft

Licenses are single-valued, so an edge mutates if and only if parent and child differ. Over 320,065 observed inheritances the license mutation rate is 14.98%. The optimal ordering over the 20 most frequent licenses reproduces 132 of 140 drift directions (94.29%) and 84.26% of all mutations (Figure 8). The ordering runs from gemma, llama3.1, llama3.2, openrail++, creativeml-openrail-m, cc-by-nc-4.0, llama3, llama2 at the upstream end — of the first eight licenses, seven are commercial (gemma/llama varieties) or otherwise restrictive (openrail varieties) — down to permissive or public-domain terms (mit, apache-2.0, artistic-2.0, cc) and copyleft varieties (cc-by-*, gpl-3.0) at the downstream end; none of the last eight licenses in the ordering is commercial. Two concrete mutation stories illustrate the direction: the gemma license — whose terms require passing the agreement to third parties and restrict "sexually explicit content" — mutates most frequently to Apache-2.0 and MIT, which contain no such provisions; and cc-by-nc-4.0 (non-commercial) mutates to MIT and to Creative Commons variants without the non-commercial clause — "a strict relaxation of terms." (When labeling licenses permissive/restrictive/copyleft, the paper explicitly follows the taxonomy of Longpre et al., footnote 5, p. 14.)

Why would licenses weaken and relax even when doing so might constitute a violation of upstream agreement terms? The observed mutation drift suggests market and behavioral pressures toward openness outweigh the specter of legal enforcement as a motivator for AI developers.
— Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025), p. 14 of the PDF

5.2 Documentation: model cards thin and standardize

Model cards exist at a very high rate for models inside family trees, while missing cards are "far more frequent among models with no family ties"; among tied models the card is almost always present "even if it is only a few characters long." Along parent–child links with cards, the length of the card drops by roughly 5,000 characters, and "the parent's model card is roughly twice the size of the child's model card, on average." Yet even as cards get shorter, about 30% of derivative models contain the bigrams automatically generated or generated automatically; the paper reports that these markers of auto-generated cards are "uniquely observed in finetunes and adapters," suggesting they are byproducts of the packages and libraries used to create finetunes and adaptations rather than of merge or quantization tooling (Figure 9). The paper reads this as "pressures toward lean documentation and automation technologies that remove costs to document and explain models."

Model cards exist at a very high rate for models that belong to family trees. Missing model cards are far more frequent among models with no family ties. Among models with family ties, the model card is almost always available, even if it is only a few characters long.
— Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025), p. 15 of the PDF

5.3 Languages: specialization and drift toward English

Languages are multi-valued, so mutations are partial: for parent languages $(A,B,C)$ and child languages $(B,C,D)$, the mutation rate is the size of the shared set over the union (here 2/4 = 1/2), and directed mutations are logged from each dropped language to each child language. Over 115,660 observed inheritances the language mutation rate is 12.80%, and the optimal ordering — running from Polish, Swedish, Thai, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian, Dutch, German, French, Japanese, Indonesian, Vietnamese, Turkish, Russian, Spanish, Chinese, Arabic, Hindi, Korean up to English at the downstream end — explains 186 of 190 drift directions (97.89%) and 74.71% of mutations (Figure 10). Two dominant trends follow. First, specialization: large base models that anchor big family trees support many languages, while derivative models list one or a handful, so language support falls precipitously between parents and fine-tuned children. Second, drift toward English: mutations flow overwhelmingly from broad multilingual support to English-language support, which the authors interpret as "a considerable market pressure towards English-speaking products and compatibilities." Notably, although "an increasing number of Chinese models are being developed and hosted," they observe no commensurate drift toward Chinese compatibility.

5.4 Tasks: drift that recapitulates the machine learning lifecycle

Tasks are single-valued like licenses, but mutate at a much higher rate: 23.14% over 251,060 observed inheritances. The optimal ordering explains 111 of 121 drift directions (91.74%) and 95.16% of mutations (Figure 11). The ordering runs from upstream, low-level tasks — fill-mask, image-text-to-text, feature-extraction, automatic-speech-recognition, text-to-speech — through modality-translation tasks — translation, summarization, text-generation, text-to-image — down to classification and reinforcement-learning tasks (sentence-similarity, question-answering, image-classification, token-classification, reinforcement-learning, and others at the downstream end).

The results seem to suggest that tasks progress from low-level feature extraction tasks (e.g., fill-mask, feature-extraction, automatic-speech-recognition) to modality translations (e.g., translation, text-generation, summarization, text-to-image), to classification and reinforcement learning tasks.
— Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025), p. 17 of the PDF

The authors' interpretation (explicitly offered as "a possible explanatory hypothesis" needing further research) is that the task ordering "recapitulates" the machine learning training pipeline: raw inputs are tokenized and embedded; models do representation learning via masked-token prediction; tasks orient around adaptation (cross-modal inputs, low-resource languages); classification assigns discrete labels to learned representations; generative tasks produce outputs from context; and instruction tuning and reinforcement learning align outputs with human preferences. Because a "recapitulation" hypothesis in biology ("ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny") is largely discredited, the authors are careful to frame the task ordering as generative for further inquiry rather than as an established law. 6. Ecosystem dynamics: the explanatory machinery the paper offers

The paper does not estimate a formal model of ecosystem dynamics; its analytical contribution is instead a mechanism sketch built from three pieces. First, the combination of directedness and orderedness implies that trait evolution is acyclic and nearly deterministic in direction — the authors stress that directedness alone would not imply orderedness, since values could drift $i \to j \to k \to i$ in a cycle, but cycles are essentially absent from the data. Second, they connect this structure to environmental selection:

The directed and acyclic property of model traits suggests that models are evolving in response to environmental pressures, and we produce a number of hypotheses from the data about the nature of these pressures, which may come from a surrounding market or from behavioral tendencies of developers involved in open source communities.
— Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025), p. 4 of the PDF

Third, they attach specific hypotheses about which pressures dominate: that "preferences for open source outweigh existing regulatory pressures to comply with licenses"; that the English drift "suggests a formidable market for english-language products"; and that thinning, auto-generated documentation reflects automation that lowers the cost of documenting derivatives. On diffusion dynamics, the S-curve growth of the largest family trees (Figure 3, citing Foster, 1986) supplies a standard adoption-curve reading, and the low structural virality with deep branching (Appendix §8, using Goel et al., 2016) qualifies it: broadcast from hubs plus chains of repeated adaptation.

The Discussion section (pp. 18–19) adds forward-looking conjectures about how this anatomy might evolve. Merges — "essentially 'marrying families'" — are the most structurally consequential possibility:

Mergers between models could be viewed as a form of 'sexual reproduction,' contrasting with the one-to-one parent–child mappings that this paper focuses on. As merges become more popular, the Hugging Face graph may undergo a phase transition in which nearly all nodes become connected in a single, massive connected component.
— Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025), p. 19 of the PDF

They also flag the ecosystem's boundary conditions: closed models "have interesting interactions" with the open ecosystem; the analysis is a snapshot and could be extended to time series; and ecological concepts such as niche formation, competition, cooperation, kin selection, and succession could explain "how model families grow, stabilize, or die out."

7. What the anatomy implies for the AI development pattern

Read as evidence about how AI is actually produced, the paper's measurements describe a pretrain → adapt/fine-tune economy with four stylized facts that any strategy or policy account must accommodate.

  • Adaptation, not creation, is the mass activity. The overwhelming majority of models are derivatives; branching is carried almost entirely by fine-tuning, while quantizations and adapters fill out families without spawning them. The modal unit of "innovation" on the hub is a modified copy, and the copies are cheap to document (thin, templated, auto-generated cards in ~30% of derivatives).
  • Innovation is chain-like and hierarchical, not flat. Family trees run five generations in the granite example and up to nearly 40 generations deep in the dataset; the "genetic" ordering of tasks implies that capability flows from generic foundations through modality-specific intermediates to specialized, aligned endpoints. Value and capability accrue at identifiable positions in these chains.
  • Openness is self-reinforcing along the chain. Derivative-making systematically relaxes upstream restrictions (commercial → permissive/copyleft), even where that relaxes terms the upstream license imposed. Whatever the legal status of such departures, the empirical direction is unambiguous — which bears directly on the license-based governance questions raised by White et al. (2024) and Longpre et al. (2025).
  • The ecosystem is English-centric and market-driven in its mutations. Both language specialization (multilingual bases → English-only derivatives) and the popularity gradient (downloads concentrated on a few models; DOIs raising downloads 29×) point to demand-side pressures shaping which adaptations are produced.

Reading-note (inference): the paper itself stops at hypotheses about environmental pressures; items (1)–(4) above are my synthesis of its measured facts, not claims the paper makes in that wording.

What to take away. If the topic's economics papers ask whether open AI is an efficient, open, and competitive way to produce intelligence, this paper supplies the underlying production structure: a steep, hub-dominated hierarchy of pre-trained foundations and derivative specialists, whose defining dynamic — fast, directed mutation along documented adaptation chains — quietly rewrites licenses, narrows languages, thins documentation, and pushes models downstream along the machine learning lifecycle. Any account of incentives on Hugging Face must start from that anatomy.

9. Limitations and open questions

Author-stated limits (paper claims and evidence). The paper is a descriptive measurement study and stops at hypotheses about environmental pressures; the authors do not claim to identify causes of the drift patterns they document. Its adaptation graph is built from platform-visible relations — fine-tune, quantization, adapter, and merge metadata and model cards — which the platform's own design choices (metadata tags, auto-generation tooling, back-filled dates) help shape, so part of the measured structure is an artifact of what Hugging Face records. Figures and some appendix material live only in the PDF's graphics, which the text layer used for this reading note does not reproduce.

Independent judgment (reader inference). Three scope caveats follow from the design, and they are this note's inferences rather than the paper's wording: (1) single-platform scope — the Hub is the world's largest open-model registry, but it is not the whole AI production system, and closed or enterprise models are outside the measurement; (2) the "genetic"/mutation vocabulary is an interpretive frame imported from ecology and genetics, useful for description but not a demonstrated mechanism of how developers actually choose what to build; (3) downloads are treated as a popularity input, which conflates exposure, need, and quality in ways the paper does not disentangle.

Open questions the anatomy raises for the topic library. The measurements describe who builds on whom, but not the incentives, prices, or competitive returns behind that structure — which is precisely what the topic's economics papers (Wu, Lin & Li 2025; Khoshbash 2026; Yeverechyahu et al. 2024; Azoulay et al. 2024) supply at other resolutions. Whether the license "relaxation" along chains is strategic revealing, legal drift, or platform affordance — and how that interacts with the openness taxonomy of White et al. (2024) — remains an open question this paper does not resolve.
