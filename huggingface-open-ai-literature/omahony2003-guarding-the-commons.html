Guarding the Commons: How Community Managed Software Projects Protect Their Work

Author: Siobhán O'Mahony (Harvard Business School; field research conducted while a doctoral student at Stanford)
Source: Research Policy 32(7): 1179–1198, 2003 (Elsevier); doi:10.1016/S0048-7333(03)00048-9
Year / Venue: 2003, journal article
Nature: Inductive, qualitative field study of six community managed software projects; no econometrics, no formal model
Data & code status: No dataset or code was released (not applicable); evidence base = 75 semi-structured interviews, participant observation at 27 events, and online project archives (>54,000 coded lines of text). One-sentence summary

Contributors to community managed open source projects do not actually "give their work away": they release code into public circulation while retaining, pooling, and exercising rights over it, and O'Mahony documents the seven legal and normative tactics — copyleft licensing, sanctions, incorporation, copyright assignment, trademarking, trademark assignment, and brand defense — through which a bounded community keeps its work "publicly and freely available and yet, governable" (p. 1179).

Reading guide: This paper is the canonical organizational account of rights retention as commons governance, and it sits at the top of the topic library's intellectual chain. The "Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy" topic asks how a platform hosting roughly two million public models can stay open without being captured; O'Mahony supplies the template answer for the software era: openness is not the absence of property, but property deliberately configured — licenses with reciprocity clauses, foundations that hold the assets, trademarks that protect collective reputation, and community enforcement. Read it with two questions in mind. First, which of the seven tactics reappear, transformed, on today's model hubs (license headers and model cards, platform terms of use, the Hugging Face organization itself as a quasi-foundation, "open" as a defended brand)? Second, what breaks when the guarded object changes from source code (protected by copyright from day one) to model weights, datasets, and APIs, whose legal and technical status is far less settled? The paper answers a 2003 problem; the topic papers — Longpre et al. 2025, Laufer et al. 2025, White et al. 2024, Jin et al. 2026, Wu, Lin & Li 2025, Khoshbash 2026 — show that the problem is still with us, at a different scale and with weaker tools.

1. Background and motivation: the puzzle of "giving it away"

The paper opens with a contributor to the GNOME desktop project explaining what is at stake when a hacker leaves a firm:

It's a kind of cool to bring the power to where it belongs and what's really exciting about working with the hackers is that .... if you're [a] hacker and you leave [a firm] and you've done this work for hire, then you no longer own your product. Well, with open source, you own your stuff right? (Contributor, GNOME, a Graphical User Interface (GUI) Desktop Project.)

The puzzle that motivated the 1990s literature on open source was the "private provision of a public good": thousands of unpaid volunteers produce software that anyone can download and use without contributing, apparently refuting Olson's (1965) claim that rational actors will under-invest in goods whose benefits cannot be restricted. Economists answered with private incentives — career-signaling and reputation (Lerner and Tirole, 2002a), private learning and need-satisfaction (von Hippel and von Krogh, 2003), enjoyment and reciprocity (Hars and Ou, 2000; Lee and Cole, 2000; Lakhani et al., 2002; Butler et al., in press). Hann et al. (2002) found partial support for the signaling story: high-ranking contributors to Apache earned more in their regular jobs, though the authors note this could be confounded by skill. O'Mahony's objection is that the entire debate has been conducted on the wrong object. Scholars asked why contributors give their work away, and "neglected to examine what is given away (code) and what is retained (rights)" (p. 1180).

The empirical counter-observation is that community managed projects — open source or free software projects "initiated and managed by a distributed group of people who do not share a common employer" (p. 1179) — routinely use the legal instruments of copyright, trademark, licensing, and incorporation to protect their collective work. The threat they defend against is not the free rider who takes without paying; it is proprietary appropriation: a commercial vendor adding proprietary code to the community's work and privatizing it — what Lerner and Tirole (2002b) call "hijacking." If a hijack succeeds, "the future stream of benefits that would stem from the collective resource would be made unavailable to the community" (p. 1181). O'Mahony's research question is therefore institutional rather than motivational: how is the commons guarded so that a private-collective production model can survive contact with the market?

2. Core concepts: public goods, common pool resources, and where software fits

O'Mahony builds the analytical frame from two bodies of theory. Open source software has usually been characterized as a privately produced public good (Kollock, 1998, 1999; Lerner and Tirole, 2002a; Johnson, 2001; Bessen, 2001; Weber, 2000). Following Snidal (1979), a public good is non-exclusive (available to one only if available to all) and joint in supply (indivisible; one person's consumption does not diminish availability to others). Software that can be downloaded by anyone, including non-contributors, fits both criteria. But Olson's logic of collective action predicts chronic under-provision of such goods, which open source contradicts in practice.

The second frame comes from the commons literature. Hardin's (1968) "Tragedy of the Commons" predicted overuse of shared, non-renewable resources by self-interested actors. Ostrom and colleagues (Ostrom, 1990, 1999; Ostrom et al., 1994, 1999) challenged both the assumptions and the pessimism of the metaphor, showing that groups can devise durable rules-in-use to manage shared resources. Two features define a common pool resource (CPR) dilemma: difficulty of exclusion and subtractability — the opposite of joint supply, since "exploitation by one reduces the availability of the resource for others" (p. 1182). The tragedy only materializes, as O'Mahony quotes Ostrom, if the users are:

...actors using the commons [who] are "norm-free maximizers of immediate gains, who will not cooperate to overcome the common dilemmas they face" (Ostrom, 1999, p. 493, quoted at p. 1181; the pdftotext renders "norm-free" as "normfree").
— 2 form-feed page breaks precede this quote; p. 1181 of the PDF.

The paper's conceptual move is to place open source on this two-dimensional map. Open source software is privately produced and non-exclusive, which satisfies the definition of both a public good and a CPR. The ambiguous dimension is subtractability. In the present, use of software is joint in supply: "if one person downloads software for his or her personal use, the amount available for the next person is unchanged" (p. 1182). But a resource that sits unprotected in the public domain can be appropriated and closed, and then "the future stream of benefits is at risk" (p. 1182). O'Mahony concludes that open source "appear[s] to be joint in supply, but [is] in fact vulnerable to usage that would threaten its availability to all" (p. 1182): not subtractable in the conventional sense, yet sharing the CPR problem that behavior must be regulated to protect the collective resource for the future. The theoretical payoff is a reclassification: open source is "not quite a public good and not quite a common pool resource, at least, in the way these types of goods have been previously defined" (p. 1195) — it is a privately produced, non-exclusive good whose future availability depends on collective governance. That reclassification redirects attention from why individuals contribute to how the community sustains the resource they have pooled, which is exactly what the empirical study examines.

3. Methods and evidence base

The study is inductive and qualitative, using ethnographic methods appropriate for "grounded theory building" (Eisenhardt, 1989) with the three distinctive features of theoretical sampling, constant comparison, and a coding paradigm (Strauss, 1987). Three evidence sources allowed triangulation: (1) observation at project and user-group meetings, technical presentations, and conferences — over 100 hours at 27 different events between April 2000 and 2001; (2) informant interviews; and (3) project data archived online, including mission statements, charters, bylaws, meeting minutes, and mailing-list archives. Over 54,000 lines of text were coded using Atlas TI.

Seventy-five semi-structured interviews were conducted with contributors, most (77%) in person and the remainder by phone. Informant composition, per the paper's Table 1: 96% male; 63% corporate-sponsored (contributing as part of employment) versus 37% independent volunteers; 45% identified through face-to-face contact and 55% through the Internet, e-mail, or referrals (the text states 56% face-to-face — a minor internal inconsistency with Table 1's 0.45). Sampling deliberately maximized variance across volunteers, firm-sponsored contributors, and firm and nonprofit representatives.

Theoretical sampling then selected six projects for deeper examination: the GNU project, the Linux kernel, the Apache web server, the Debian Linux distribution, the GNOME desktop, and the Linux Standard Base (LSB). Selection criteria: large, technically mature, commercially attractive, and varied in their relations with firms. This is a small, purposive sample of flagship projects — a fact that matters for how far the findings generalize, discussed in Section 7 below.

4.1 Licensing terms that restrict proprietary appropriation

Five of the six projects licensed their work under the GNU General Public License (GPL), the first free-software license, devised by Richard Stallman in the mid-1980s after he abandoned the idea of placing his GNU system in the public domain. The origin story, in Stallman's own words, is the paper's clearest statement of why a commons needs a fence:

But then I thought about the question of whether people would change the software and make proprietary versions of it. And I realized that if they did that, they could defeat the whole point, they could negate the effort. Someone could make an improved proprietary version and it could displace the free version. [...] And so I decided to look for some way I could stop that from happening. And in discussions with a lawyer I worked out the idea of copyleft. (Richard Stallman, interview, 20 March 2001)
— 4 form-feed page breaks precede this quote; p. 1183 of the PDF.

Copyleft "uses copyright law, but flips it over to serve the opposite of its usual purpose: instead of a means of privatizing software, it becomes a means of keeping software free" (Stallman, 1999, p. 59, quoted at p. 1186). The engine of the flip is Section (2)(b) of the GPL, which the paper quotes verbatim:

You must cause any work that you distribute or publish, that in whole or in part contains or is derived from the Program or any part thereof, to be licensed as a whole at no charge to all to third parties under the terms of this License. (Free Software Foundation, Section (2)(b), GNU General Public License, Version 2, June 1991)
— 7 form-feed page breaks precede this quote; p. 1186 of the PDF.

The clause is self-perpetuating: any derivative work released to the public must carry the same terms, so works derived from GPL code "cannot be made proprietary" (p. 1186). With this clause, in Eben Moglen's formulation quoted in the paper, "the GPL not only establishes a commons, but a fence that protects that commons" — it assures every contributor "that source code will always be available, and that, unlike commercial software, its longevity cannot be limited by the contingencies of the marketplace or the decisions of future developers" (Moglen, 1999, quoted at p. 1187).

Two observations give the licensing tactic its analytical sharpness. First, the GPL binds almost nobody. Because agreement to the license is established by exercising the licensed freedoms, only those who modify and redistribute code — "the small five to ten percent" most likely to act with commercial intent — are bound by it (p. 1187):

Ninety to ninety-five percent of the people that use free software have never agreed to the GPL because they aren't undertaking activities governed by copyright law. They are not copying, they are not modifying, they are not redistributing. They grabbed it once from a Website, put in on their computer, and they are just using [it]. They have no obligations under GPL whatsoever. (Informant, Non-Profit Foundation)
— 8 form-feed page breaks precede this quote; p. 1187 of the PDF.

Second, the problem the GPL solves is not the free-rider problem of non-contribution, but "the inability to prevent the proprietary appropriation of work in the public domain" (p. 1187). Informants chose the GPL "explicitly with the protections offered by Section (2)(b) in mind"; a GNOME founder explained that "GNOME was GPL'd to enforce, to protect software that gets developed from [getting] proprietary features. The license has to be used strategically" (Table 3, p. 1186). Lerner and Tirole (2002b) counted 70% of 25,792 SourceForge projects under the GPL, and Lee (1999) argued that without intellectual property protections there would be "no legal way to prevent the closing of source code" (p. 1187). In short: the license targets would-be appropriators, not end users — the opposite targeting of conventional copyright.

4.2 Legal and normative sanctions

A license with no enforcement is decoration, so O'Mahony separates adoption of terms (tactic 1) from compliance work (tactic 2). Enforcement is differentiated by the violator's identity. A community violation — improper license use, removal of copyright notices, or co-mingling GPL and proprietary code by a community member — is handled informally on mailing lists. A commercial violation, typically a vendor distributing software without honoring the obligation to supply source code, is more serious because it involves distribution of the offending product. Customers often act as the community's monitors, reporting infringements to copyright holders; one informant described "three people writing to us, saying ... we discovered that it looks like it is based on [your software], and darn it they didn't give us the source code" (p. 1187). The Free Software Foundation, "the institution founded primarily to enforce the GNU GPL," asks those who suspect infringement to "report it ... check the facts as best you can. Then tell the publisher or copyright holder of the specific GPL-covered program" (FSF, 2001, quoted at pp. 1187–1188). One foundation informant received two to five violation reports a week, of which only about one every two weeks proved valid (p. 1188).

Formal escalation mirrors corporate practice — lawyers draft "cease and desist"-style letters — but the objective is compliance, not damages: violators are asked to release source code, to remove GPL code from proprietary bases, or to re-license offending code under the GPL, and then to appoint "a GPL compliance officer within your organization who is at a high enough level that when we find a GPL problem in the future we can call them up and have a line of communication" (pp. 1188–1189). The GPL's Section 4 backs this with a rarely used power: any attempt to copy, modify, or distribute in violation "is void, and will automatically terminate your rights under this License" (quoted at p. 1188), letting copyright holders halt non-compliant distribution until the violation is resolved. As of writing, no known court case had tested the GPL; the Epson Kowa Corporation, after coming into compliance, publicly announced on its website: "We have temporally closed the services due to the fact that our products were against [the] GPL, however the problem has been settled" (p. 1189). [The GPL has been litigated since 2003 — an editorial aside beyond this paper's scope.]

Informal enforcement runs through public online forums, and here O'Mahony makes a cultural argument. The GPL "codifies a strong norm of reciprocity that has long been an important part of the programming culture" (p. 1188); the founder of GNU and the FSF described the mechanism as pressure to join the commons rather than legal prohibition: "The idea of the GPL is that if you want to include our code in your program, your program must also be free software. It is supposed to put pressure on you to release your program in a way that makes it part of our community" (p. 1188). There is "no limit on what one can take from the commons, but one is expected at some time to contribute back to the commons to the best of one's abilities" (p. 1188). Because postings on project mailing lists are public and not anonymous, forums make both norms and information about others' contributions visible, which prior work suggests produces interdependent, mutually reinforcing contribution (Gould, 1993; Guttman, 1987). A Debian contributor summed up the hierarchy of enforcement: "So far no one has had to litigate ... they fight it out in the court of public opinion" (p. 1189). One informant's line is the paper's thesis in miniature: "I don't want to give the legal system too much credit. It [the GPL] also works because there is a whole community ready to accept it. There is a norm of good behavior" (Independent Private Legal Counsel, p. 1189).

4.3 Incorporate

Five of the six projects created a legal entity — a non-profit foundation — to hold their intellectual property and to shield volunteer contributors from personal liability. A project that exists only as mailing lists and web pages "does not have any legal rights. It cannot sign contracts or hold property" (p. 1190). Incorporation gives the community the same protections and privileges as a corporation: volunteers are protected from individual liability, the project can enter agreements collectively, and its code, trademarks, licenses, and brand can be owned. The choice of the non-profit form is meaningful: because their non-distribution constraint prescribes distributing net income, nonprofits "signal trustworthiness" (DiMaggio and Anheier, 1990; Hansmann, 1980, 1996), which is appealing "when certain goods are seen as inappropriate for market exchange or requiring special protection from corruption by the profit motive" (DiMaggio and Anheier, 1990, p. 144, quoted at p. 1190). A Debian founder's rationale was protective and practical: "We want to make sure that Debian has an umbrella over it, so that people who are working on this potentially as a hobby are not exposing themselves to risk" (Table 3, p. 1186). The Linux kernel project is the conspicuous abstainer: unincorporated, and the user of the fewest legal tactics (four of seven).

4.4 Transfer individual property rights to a foundation

Rights held by hundreds of individuals are hard to coordinate, so several projects asked contributors to assign their copyrights to the foundation. "Intellectual property rights are better defined and more defensible when owned by a single legal entity" (p. 1190); assignment also converts individual liability exposure into institutional liability and improves the ability to pursue enforcement. Yet this was "the least popular among the projects and the most controversial" (p. 1190): contributors on four of six projects transferred copyrights, but no project made assignment a condition of contribution, and only two — GNU and Apache — actively asked. The Linux kernel founder rejected the practice outright:

[Copyright] transfer? No, I think that is amoral. Plus, I hate paperwork. So everybody keeps their own copyright. It makes people comfortable about the fact that there is no single copyright holder. [...] Well in practice, there are not that many people who have even bothered to put their own copyrights on code. There are a couple hundred or something, so to actually change the license would be practically impossible, which is what makes people feel comfortable with it. (Founder and Project Leader, Linux kernel project)
— 12 form-feed page breaks precede this quote; p. 1191 of the PDF.

The kernel's fragmentation is itself a guard: with "a couple hundred" scattered copyright holders, no single actor could unilaterally re-license the code, so the absence of centralized ownership protects the commons by making capture practically impossible. The controversy runs deeper, against the "hacker culture" in which "individual merit, autonomy, and problem solving ability are paramount" and information sharing is "an ethical duty" (Raymond, 1999, p. 236, quoted at p. 1191). Assigning one's copyright to a nonprofit run by project leaders compromises that ethic more than any other tactic. Apache contributors live with the tension, giving two answers to the question of who owns the code — first a legal answer, then a normative one: "Legally, the foundation owns the code. However, having said that, the people who write the code own the code .... If you have ever gotten a piece of code into Apache, you own that code, its yours and you should feel proud of it" (Sponsored Contributor, Apache, p. 1191). When one contributor claimed too much individual ownership, the collective checked him:

And you know this was kind of the shock. He says "this isn't your code, this is our code. If you think it is [your code], I will take your code out of this tree, and put this [other] code back in [...]. " And somebody else basically said to him, "you cannot take his code out because it isn't your code any more than it isn't his code." It's one of those things, checks and balances. (Sponsored Contributor, Apache Project)
— 13 form-feed page breaks precede this quote; p. 1192 of the PDF.

Projects with active assignment policies also worried about work-for-hire agreements: firms typically require employees to transfer ownership of works created with company resources, so the Free Software Foundation asks contributing authors to demonstrate that they are free from such agreements (Moglen, 2001). Several informants revised their employment agreements to be able to contribute — evidence that firms' legal claims over employee code were, even in 2001, a live governance problem for the commons (p. 1192). One foundation informant articulated the paradox that runs through the whole paper:

So, we are in this odd situation as people who want as few legal restrictions as possible on software, but we are pragmatic enough to realize that without some legal protection we would loose the community we built. [...] Our goal was to build freedom in the system we had, this flawed system where ... there was a decision made that copyright law would cover software. We have to work within the decision that exists and it is the default belief in the industrialized world at least. So, we have to build legal systems within copyright laws to defend freedom. (Informant, Non-Profit Foundation)
— 13 form-feed page breaks precede this quote; p. 1192 of the PDF. ("loose" appears in the original.)

4.5–4.7 Trademarks, trademark assignment, and brand defense

The last three tactics protect the collective identity rather than the code. All six projects developed distinct brands or logos and filed trademarks with the US Patent and Trademark Office, motivated "to ensure that the project would be uniquely distinguished and to prevent others from confusing a project's software with other related work" (p. 1192), and because an undefended mark can lapse into the public domain. Five of six projects assigned their trademark rights to the supporting foundation — the Apache Software Foundation's assignment form transfers "Member's entire right, title, and interest" in the marks (pp. 1192–1193) — with governance varying by project (Apache's foundation manages its mark; Debian's members, not its foundation board, manage theirs). The Linux kernel again diverges: its trademark is held by an individual — the project leader — because, as a Linux International informant explained, "We were afraid of corporations taking over who might do nasty things. [The project leader] was the only one that was trusted" (p. 1193). Linux International had earlier marshaled funds to hire an attorney when "the Linux trademark was under attack in 1997," to "wrest it away from the guy who was trying to use it as a weapon" (Table 3, p. 1186).

Brand defense is most intense where firms repackage community work. Contributors preferred what marketers would call co-branding — recognition of both the firm's product and the project's contribution (Kotler, 2002) — over anonymous absorption. The Debian example shows why. One distributor shipped broken CDs, and "people would write to Debian and it would not be [our code]," so the project began producing official CD masters and attached conditions to its mark:

So Debian started making official CDs. And I attached it to the Debian trademark, which thank goodness is a real trademark, and said you may call your product official Debian if it is made from our CD masters and if you include the source CD and the binary CD. (Former Volunteer Contributor and Project Leader, Debian Linux Distribution Project)
— 14 form-feed page breaks precede this quote; p. 1193 of the PDF.

The theoretical observation that closes the findings is about which rights these tools protect. Contributors cared about recognition for the project, not themselves; the Debian contributor's complaint was that a firm's manual mentioned Debian only once, with "never a Debian logo, never a Debian anything" (p. 1193). O'Mahony argues that while theorists emphasized individual reputation as a motivator (Raymond, 1999; Weber, 2000; Lerner and Tirole, 2002b), "few have recognized the value of the collective reputation": "the collective reputation of the group may matter as much, if not more, to volunteer contributors than individual reputation," and projects "value and protect their identity as a community of developers as much as they do the product of their collective efforts" (p. 1194).

5. Findings summarized: unbundled rights as governance

Across the six projects, three used all seven tactics (GNU, Debian, LSB) and every project used at least four; every project used sanctions, logos and trademark filing, and active brand defense, and projects varied most in their willingness to centralize ownership through copyright assignment. The pattern O'Mahony draws from this is that community managed projects take legal instruments designed to restrict access and redeploy them to preserve access: "the rights retained by the licensors ... are not the rights for which these tools are traditionally designed, but those most closely aligned with the licensor's values" — less concerned with restricting access, more with preserving public access (p. 1194). The GPL's grant of use, modification, and distribution rights appears to transfer power from licensor to licensee, "but these rights are not unbound": licensees may sell the community's work for profit only in ways that acknowledge the community's contribution, so "a subtle rebalancing of rights is achieved, by transferring power to the licensee, but by making this power conditional on cooperation with community norms" (p. 1194). In the discussion, O'Mahony connects the tactics back to Ostrom's design principles. CPR research finds that groups manage shared resources sustainably when they can identify one another, draw on trust, reciprocity, and reputation, monitor and coordinate, and have "some autonomy to make and enforce their own rules and they highly value the future sustainability of the resource" (Ostrom et al., 1999, p. 280, quoted at p. 1195). The six projects fit: contributors "envisioned a long future working with the software" (Debian, for example, had run over nine years, survived six leaders, and grown beyond 1,000 registered contributing members), and the online forum context makes contributions, norms, and information highly visible. The difference from natural-resource CPRs is that open source "is always publicly available" — resources do not need scheduled redistribution — so the problem is not allocation of a scarce flow but protection of a future stream: without the legal tactics, the code "might be in danger of becoming a subtractable good" (p. 1195). Moglen's phrase, quoted approvingly, is that GPL §2(b) creates a commons "to which anyone may add but from which no one may subtract" (p. 1195).

The discussion also generalizes beyond software. Drawing on Stefik's (1997a, b) notion of trusted systems — digital systems in which property rights are respected while transport, rendering, and derivative-work rights "can be disaggregated and managed under different terms" (p. 1196) — O'Mahony notes the irony that the most sophisticated technology users are rebalancing power between producers and users through legal and normative means rather than technology. With a broad definition of digital rights management, "this is precisely what the community managed software projects in this study do: manage, exercise and defend rights to digital intellectual property" (p. 1196). The key architectural insight is that legal mechanisms let communities "decouple the ability to govern their work from its circulation and possession" (p. 1196): the code circulates freely while governance stays with the community — a configuration the commercial sector's rights vocabulary was not built to describe, and one that copyright law's "untapped elasticity" makes possible in the digital era (p. 1195). Finally, echoing Lessig (1999c), governance choices matter for regulators: when ownership of code is firmly established, governmental authority over it is more easily assured; when it is ambiguous, so is the ability to regulate (pp. 1196–1197).

6. Position in the literature

6.1 In its own research stream

O'Mahony 2003 belongs to a specific confluence of three streams and became a hinge between them. The first stream is the commons and collective-action literature of Hardin (1968), Olson (1965), and above all Ostrom (1990; Ostrom et al., 1994, 1999), from which it imports the vocabulary of exclusion, subtractability, and rules-in-use, and to which it contributes the observation that a digital, non-subtractable commons can nonetheless face an appropriation hazard that calls for institutional guarding. The second is the economics of open source: it answers Lerner and Tirole's (2002a, b) question of what sustains the private provision of a public good by shifting the object of analysis from contribution incentives to the governance of the contributed stock, and it complements the companion paper in this library, von Hippel and von Krogh's (2003) private-collective innovation model: von Hippel and von Krogh explain why private investment and collective benefit can coexist; O'Mahony explains how the resulting collective resource is kept collective. The third stream is legal scholarship on free software — Moglen (1999) on the GPL's self-perpetuating commons, Lee (1999) and McGowan (2001) on the license's enforceability, Lessig (1999a–e) on code and the commons, Stallman (1999) — from which the paper borrows the mechanics of copyleft and into which it injects systematic organizational evidence that communities deliberately manage those mechanics as strategy, not ideology.

Within organizational research, the paper is the empirical foundation of a program O'Mahony continued: O'Mahony and Ferraro (2007), also in this library's Part II set, study the same kinds of projects (Debian in particular) to show how governance structures emerge in the absence of authority; O'Mahony and Karp (2022) generalize the arc from proprietary to collective governance for platforms deciding how far to open participation. The paper's rights-retention framing also connects to the "selective revealing" literature (Henkel, 2006, this library), which asks the mirror question from the firm side — what firms disclose when they join open communities — and to later work on firms opening proprietary IP (Wen et al., 2016), on open source as a productivity and entrepreneurship input (Nagle, 2019; Wright et al., 2023), and on firms signaling through community engagement (Conti et al., 2025). If von Hippel and von Krogh established that the private-collective model is an efficient mode of innovation, O'Mahony established that its sustainability is an organizational achievement — communities do governance work with lawyers, foundations, and trademarks, not only with code.

6.2 Connection to the Hugging Face core and the open-AI economy

Read against the topic's Part I core, this 2003 paper functions as the theoretical ancestor for a cluster of claims about today's model ecosystems, and its seven tactics provide a checklist against which the platform era can be audited.

  • Laufer et al. 2025 ("Anatomy of a Machine Learning Ecosystem: 2 Million Models on Hugging Face"). The hub is a commons of unprecedented scale. Laufer et al. map who produces and consumes models; O'Mahony supplies the question their anatomy cannot answer from graph structure alone — what keeps two million public models from being appropriated or silently privatized. On the hub, the guard is distributed across license headers, dataset cards, and platform terms rather than the foundations and mailing lists of 2003, and O'Mahony's framework predicts that hub-level governance will be weakest exactly where ownership is ambiguous (models whose weights derive from mixed sources) — an empirical conjecture this deep dive flags as an inference, not a claim of either paper.
  • Longpre et al. 2025 ("Economies of Open Intelligence: Tracing Power & Participation in the Model Ecosystem"). Longpre et al. trace power and participation through the open-weight ecosystem, asking who contributes, who controls, and who benefits — the same questions O'Mahony answered for software communities with interviews and archives, now answered at population scale with model metadata. Where O'Mahony found communities deliberately bundling legal instruments (licenses, foundations, marks) to retain rights over freely circulating goods, Longpre et al. document a hub economy in which firms, not volunteer communities, are the dominant contributors — a shift from the bounded community of O'Mahony's six projects to a corporate ecosystem that makes the "bounded" part of her definition the central open question.
  • White et al. 2024 (Model Openness Framework). The MOF decomposes "open model" into seventeen elements (weights, code, data, documentation, and their licensing terms). This is a direct methodological descendant of O'Mahony's unbundling insight: she showed that openness is not a single state but a configuration of separable rights — use, modify, redistribute, appropriate — that communities assemble tactically. The MOF extends the same decomposition to the AI stack, where the GPL-style reciprocity clause has no settled analogue for weights, and permissive licensing (Apache-2.0, MIT) dominates — precisely the configuration O'Mahony's sample treated as the risky one (only Apache, whose license "does not prevent proprietary appropriation," chose it, and Apache compensated with foundation ownership and strong trademarks).
  • Wu, Lin & Li 2025 (ICIS) and Khoshbash 2026. Both use the Hugging Face platform as a data environment to estimate how openness shocks reshape downstream innovation — Wu, Lin & Li with a difference-in-differences around the Llama 2 release, Khoshbash with a longitudinal panel of model families. They operationalize "openness" largely through model licensing and hub distribution choices; O'Mahony's lesson is that a license is only one of several coupled guards, and that measurement of openness should follow her lead in asking what rights are retained and by whom, not only what is downloadable.
  • Jin et al. 2026 and Yue & Nagle 2024. Jin et al. argue that U.S. export policies unintentionally accelerated China's open AI ecosystems. Read through O'Mahony, the mechanism is licensing geography: permissively licensed models (Apache-2.0-style) circulate globally with no reciprocity clause, so a commons can form without any of the guarding structures — foundations, copyright pooling, community enforcement — that made the software commons governable. Yue & Nagle's PyTorch study is the mirror image on the supply side: a firm deliberately relinquishing control over core technology, the corporate analog of O'Mahony's communities choosing how much legal control to exercise. Azoulay et al. 2024 ("Old Moats for New Models") frames the same tension as openness versus control in generative AI; O'Mahony's contribution is to show that openness and control are not opposites — the open source projects she studied are among the most legally controlled artifacts in software, controlled in the service of openness.
  • Greenstein et al. 2022 (Hugging Face case) and Fleischmann & Haeussler 2026. The HBS case documents a platform that deliberately positioned itself as the neutral host for a community that feared capture by any single firm; O'Mahony explains why such fears are rational and what institutional work assuages them. Fleischmann and Haeussler's study of "degrees of openness" in open-source AI is the most direct empirical heir: it measures how license choice conditions downstream innovation, effectively testing at scale whether the 2003 finding — that restrictive licensing can protect rather than deter — survives in the model economy.

The connecting thread is that O'Mahony reframed a debate about motivation ("why give code away?") into a debate about governance ("how is the commons guarded?"), and every Part I paper that treats licenses, hub governance, or openness shocks as explanatory variables is standing on that reframing — whether its authors cite her or not.

7. Limitations

Boundaries the author states or implies. The paper is explicit that it studies a deliberately chosen set of six large, technically mature, commercially attractive projects — the flagships of the movement — which "varied in their relations with firms" but cannot represent the long tail of projects (the SourceForge population in footnote 5 already exceeded 500,000 registered users). Its claims about tactics are descriptive and inductive; the frequency counts in Table 3 are counts within a purposive sample, not population statistics. The author also notes that the GPL had not been tested in court as of writing, that evidence on firm-side compliance is "suggestive," and that future research should examine governance "especially when [work] is distributed in commercial markets or becomes the basis for de facto standards" (p. 1196).

Independent judgment (layered and labeled):

  • Author's claim: contributors to community managed projects "maintain and exercise rights over their work"; the seven tactics let a project's intellectual property be "publicly and freely available and yet, governable" (pp. 1179–1180, 1194). This is the paper's core assertion, supported by convergent interview, documentary, and observational evidence.
  • Evidence: the evidence is qualitative and interpretive — 75 interviews (96% male informants; 63% corporate-sponsored), archived documents, and the researcher's coding of over 54,000 lines of text. It is strong on mechanism and actor interpretation, weak on prevalence: nothing in the design can estimate how widespread each tactic is across the population of open source projects, or how effective the tactics are against determined adversaries (no litigation had occurred).
  • Inference (mine, not stated in the paper): the paper implies that the tactics are causally important for commons survival — that without licensing and foundations, appropriation would in fact have occurred. The evidence is consistent with that claim but does not test it against a counterfactual; the Linux kernel, the least legally guarded project in the sample, survived fine, suggesting that norms and code characteristics (thousands of scattered copyright holders, developer trust) can substitute for formal structure — a substitution the paper documents in passing but does not theorize. A further inference: the framework should extend to any digital commons whose value lies in a future stream of improvements, which is exactly how I connect it to model ecosystems in Section 6.2.
  • Open questions: (1) whether guarding tactics that deter proprietary appropriation also deter commercial adoption and investment — the paper notes the argument that the GPL "inhibits commercial adoption and innovation" (p. 1187) but does not resolve it, and the Apache case (permissive license plus heavy non-license guarding) suggests firms and communities may trade off along different margins; (2) whether collective reputation, so central to brand defense, survives in ecosystems where contributions come from thousands of firms rather than a bounded community; (3) how governance scales when the guarded object is a model whose provenance, weights, and training data have no clean copyright baseline; (4) the internal inconsistency between the text's 56% and Table 1's 45% for face-to-face identification, and the era-bound assumption (no court tests of the GPL) — both should be checked before citing precise figures.

8. Conclusion and takeaways

O'Mahony's contribution is to dissolve a false dichotomy. The literature had framed open source as a puzzle of altruism versus self-interest in the private provision of a public good; her field evidence shows that contributors treat their collective work as property they both share and guard. Community managed projects use copyright, copyleft, incorporation, and trademark not because they are hostile to openness but because they are committed to a specific kind of openness — one that survives contact with the market. The seven tactics are, in effect, a governance technology that decouples the circulation of a good from control over its future: the code flows freely; the rights stay pooled in the community.

For the "Hugging Face and the Open-AI Economy" topic, three takeaways carry forward. First, treat openness as a configuration of separable rights, chosen tactically, not as a binary state — the analytical habit that White et al. 2024 systematize and that Wu, Lin & Li 2025 and Khoshbash 2026 must respect when coding "openness" from licenses. Second, expect the guard to shift from legal instruments to platform governance: where 2003 communities needed foundations and mailing lists because no platform existed, today's hub supplies terms of use, moderation, and brand at scale — but platforms are commercial actors, and O'Mahony's framework predicts the friction will reappear wherever a platform's interest in appropriation diverges from the community's interest in a guarded commons (the dynamic behind Greenstein et al. 2022 and the cautionary readings of Jin et al. 2026). Third, watch the weakest link: the software commons was governable because copyright law unambiguously covered code and because foundations created clean ownership; model weights, training data, and derivative model rights have neither property, so the model commons is guarded by terms of service and permissive licenses — a fence with, in many cases, no legal estate behind it. Whether that is enough is the open question this 2003 paper lets us ask precisely.
