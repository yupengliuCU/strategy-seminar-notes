One-sentence summary

von Hippel and von Krogh argue that open source software development is an exemplar of a private-collective innovation model that occupies the neglected middle ground between the private investment model (innovation financed privately, returns appropriated through intellectual property) and the collective action model (innovation as a public good, plagued by free riding): contributors privately fund and develop code, freely reveal it as a public good, and still capture private benefits — use value, learning, enjoyment, ownership, and community standing — that free riders cannot obtain, which can deliver society “the best of both worlds” under many conditions.

This is the organizing theory paper of the topic: it supplies the canonical answer to the question that every Hugging Face study inherits — why do thousands of capable developers contribute valuable work (models, code, datasets) to a shared hub without being paid for it? Carry four of its claims into the Part I readings as hypotheses, not facts: ① the identity of the typical innovator (users, not manufacturers — check who uploads to the Hub in Laufer et al. 2025 and Longpre et al. 2025); ② free revealing is individually rational when rivalry with adopters is low and diffusion costs are near zero (posting a model card and weights to Hugging Face is nearly costless — the mechanism Wu, Lin & Li 2025 study through the Llama 2 shock); ③ private benefits are self-awarded selective incentives, so contributions respond to changes in the private cost-benefit of contributing — the channel that Yeverechyahu et al. 2024 (Copilot) tests when a tool cheapens contribution; ④ free riders are an asset because adoption raises a project’s market share and standard-setting power — a claim to weigh against the downloading-but-never-contributing majority documented on the Hub. The 2003 paper predates platforms, so the mapping from its open source communities to model hubs is this note’s interpretive bridge, not a claim the paper makes.

1. Background and motivation: two models of innovation that cannot accommodate open source

The paper opens with a diagnosis of a gap in organization science. Two models of innovation dominate the field. Under the private investment model, innovation is supported by private investment, and private returns are appropriated from that investment; society grants innovators limited rights through patents, copyrights, and trade secrets to make appropriation feasible (citing Demsetz 1967; Arrow 1962; Liebeskind 1996; Dam 1995). The model implies that free revealing — an uncompensated spillover of privately developed knowledge — reduces the innovator’s profit and will therefore be avoided to the extent possible, even though society pays for the resulting restricted access with a deadweight loss. Under the collective action model, by contrast, innovation is the provision of a public good, defined by nonexcludability and nonrivalry (Olson 1967); because beneficiaries can wait and free ride on others’ contributions, the central problem becomes motivating contributors, typically through recruiting, norms, reputation systems, or selective incentives.

Open source software fits neither template. Its developers voluntarily collaborate over the Internet to build software that they or their organizations need; they write code at their own expense and then post it where anyone can use, study, and modify it, without selling it and without appropriating private returns from the software itself. The result is a phenomenon the two received models cannot jointly explain, and it is precisely this anomaly the paper targets. Two further properties make the phenomenon attractive to organization scientists: it offers an alternative to conventional models of how innovations should be developed and how organizations should form, and it generates complete, time-stamped, publicly available logs of community interaction and output, offering “an unprecedented clear look into their detailed inner workings” (p. 5 of the PDF; journal p. 212). The programmatic thesis is stated in the abstract:

In this paper, we propose that open source software development is an exemplar of a compound “private-collective” model of innovation that contains elements of both the private investment and the collective action models and can offer society the “best of both worlds” under many conditions.
— p. 2 of the PDF (journal p. 209, Organization Science 14(2))

2. Background material: the history and characteristics of open source software

Section 1 of the paper performs a service function for organization scholars: it explains what open source software is and how its development projects typically work, with two brief case histories (Apache and Fetchmail) that the rest of the argument reuses as illustrations.

2.1 The product: how “free” code became “open source”

Freely exchanging and building on each other’s code was normal practice among scientists and engineers in the 1960s and 1970s, and it became a central feature of “hacker culture” (the paper notes that among programmers, “hacker” is a positive term for a talented and dedicated programmer). The ARPANET, established in 1969 by the U.S. Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA), let hackers exchange code widely, cheaply, and spread their norms of behavior. The communal culture was strong at MIT’s Artificial Intelligence Laboratory in the 1960s and 1970s (Levy 1984), and it received a jolt in the 1980s when MIT licensed some hacker-written code to a commercial firm that promptly restricted access to its source code — the human-readable instructions programmers write and document, which are compiled into hard-to-read binary machine code. Richard Stallman, offended by the loss of access to communally developed code, founded the Free Software Foundation in 1985 and used copyright law in reverse: a license (the General Public License, or GPL, also called “copyleft”) that grants all users the rights to use the software at no cost, to study its source code, to modify it, and to redistribute modified or unmodified versions. In 1998 Bruce Perens and Eric Raymond founded the “open source” software movement, which kept the same licensing practices but emphasized practical benefits over Stallman’s moral framing of software freedom; “open source” is the term scholars now use for both traditions.

The scale claims of the paper are worth fixing precisely because they describe the state of the world in 2002–2003, before GitHub. A single infrastructure provider, Sourceforge.net, then listed in excess of 50,000 projects and more than 500,000 registered users; the number of users of the software produced ranges “from few to many millions,” with GNU/Linux, Apache server software, and Perl as the canonical examples (p. 3 of the PDF; journal p. 210). Commercial firms were already releasing software under open source licenses, and contributors were actively concerned with protecting user rights to use, improve, and learn from the code.

2.2 The process: how a typical open source development project works

The paper stresses that “open source” denotes the license under which software is made available, not how it was developed. But free access has generated typical development practices that differ sharply from commercial practice. A project is typically initiated by an individual or small group who want the software for their own intellectual, personal, or business reasons; the founders usually become the project “owners” or “maintainers.” The initiators develop a first rough version, post it on a project website, and set up mailing lists. In successful projects, others download, use, and modify the code; most post their modifications back to the project site, where maintainers decide what is of sufficient quality and general interest to add to the “authorized” version. The privilege of adding to the authorized code is often restricted to a few trusted “developers,” who act as gatekeepers for contributions from others (citing von Krogh et al. 2003). The paper is explicit about the participation distribution that later Hub studies rediscover:

Most who download open source software are free riders—only a relatively small proportion contribute to a project by developing code or in other ways. Open source projects do not pay participants for their services, and the motivations and characteristics of contributors vary. Most are strongly motivated by a personal or business use for the code that they develop—they are code “users” in our terminology. Other major sources of motivation include intrinsic rewards such as personal learning and enjoyment from programming.
— p. 4 of the PDF (journal p. 211)

The Apache case history: server software written by Rob McCool at the National Center for Supercomputing Applications (NCSA) was posted on the Web; when McCool departed in mid-1994, a small group of adopting webmasters took over development, and “a core group of eight users” consolidated documentation and bug fixes into a patch that evolved into Apache; Apache 1.0 was released on December 1, 1995; within four years Apache became the most popular Web server software, used by roughly 60% of the millions of websites worldwide despite competition from Microsoft and Netscape, with new releases coordinated by a central group of about 30 volunteers (pp. 4–5 of the PDF; journal pp. 211–212). The Fetchmail case history: Eric Raymond, personally dissatisfied with existing e-mail delivery solutions, searched the open source world for an existing utility he could improve, took over maintenance of “popclient,” cultivated an active “beta list” of users who reported and fixed bugs, and evolved the program (renamed fetchmail) into standard software used by millions of servers (p. 5 of the PDF; journal p. 212).

3. Core concepts: the two received models and the case for a compound model

Section 2 builds the paper’s central argument in four steps: state the two canonical models; show where open source practice deviates from each; and then propose the private-collective model that occupies the excluded middle ground.

3.1 The private investment model and open source’s two deviations

The private investment model places no constraint on who innovates, but convention treats manufacturers as the most logical private developers, because a manufacturer can sell what it develops to an entire marketplace while spreading development costs over many units, whereas an individual user-innovator can expect to benefit financially only from internal use of the innovation. Open source inverts this: the bulk of contributions, and therefore of private innovation-related investment, comes from users — individual users or user firms — rather than from software manufacturers. The fundamental reason: users profit by using the code they improve, while there is no commercial market for open source software precisely because developers make it freely available as a public good. This eliminates the manufacturer’s direct appropriation path; manufacturers may still contribute through indirect paths, as when IBM improves GNU/Linux because better Linux raises demand for complementary proprietary software or hardware that IBM sells (p. 7 of the PDF; journal p. 214).

The second deviation concerns revealing. Users in open source communities freely reveal their innovations by posting code where anyone can view and download it — behavior the private investment model says a budget-constrained innovator should avoid, since revealing without compensation forgoes potential private returns. The paper reviews the emerging argument (Harhoff et al. 2003; Lakhani and Wolf 2001) that free revealing is individually rational when two cost components are small: the loss of proprietary rights to intellectual property is small when the innovator has low rivalry with potential adopters (many contributors are students with no commercial rivalry), and the cost of diffusion is nearly zero because posting and retrieving code is almost costless. Under these conditions “even a low level of reward can be sufficient” to induce revealing, with rewards arriving as reputation, expected reciprocity, and the value of building a community (Lerner and Tirole 2000; von Krogh 2002) (pp. 7–8 of the PDF; journal pp. 214–215). The two deviations are stated compactly:

Open source software development practice involves two major deviations from the private investment model of innovation as it is conventionally viewed. First, software users rather than software manufacturers are the typical innovators in open source. Second, open source innovators freely reveal the proprietary software that they have developed at their private expense.
— p. 6 of the PDF (journal p. 213)

3.2 The collective action model and open source’s deviations from its prescriptions

The collective action model is a response to market failure in providing public goods, where nonexcludability creates the free-rider dilemma: if non-contributors benefit on equal terms with contributors, how are contributors motivated at all? The collective action literature answers with recruiting (specifying goals; building direct social relationships between recruiters and potential participants), with appeals to long-term cooperation (Axelrod 1984) and “group fate” (Schwartz and Paul 1992), and above all with selective incentives — benefits available only to contributors, such as credentials, reputation, and privileged social access (Oliver 1980; Friedman and McAdam 1992). Because selective incentives can be carefully tailored and monitoring is easier face to face, small groups should be most successful at collective action (Olson 1967; Fireman and Gamson 1979; Ostrom 1998).

Successful open source projects violate every one of these prescriptions. Their goal statements vary from technical to ideological and from precise to vague and emergent; they engage in no active recruiting beyond posting their goals on a public website; they succeed with large groups of perhaps thousands of contributors; and they expend no effort encouraging contribution over free riding — anyone may download code or seek help without moral pressure to reciprocate. The paper’s proposed explanation for these deviations is the seed of the private-collective model: “the private rewards to those who contribute to open source software collective action projects are considerably stronger than those available to free riders” (p. 8 of the PDF; journal p. 215).

3.3 The private-collective model: an incentive structure on the excluded middle ground

The central claim is that the two canonical models, in an effort to stay clean, excluded “a very rich and fertile middle ground where incentives for private investment and collective action can coexist.” Contributions to open source are not pure public goods — “they have significant private elements even after the contribution has been freely revealed.” The model is built by eliminating one assumption from each parent model. From the private investment model it drops the assumption that free revealing of privately funded innovations represents a loss of private profit; under common conditions it may involve no loss, and under some conditions a net gain (for example through network effects that raise diffusion). From the collective action model it drops the assumption that a free rider obtains benefits equal to those of a contributor; contributors inherently obtain private benefits tied to the process of developing the good, which free riders cannot obtain, and which therefore function as a form of selective incentive “that need not be managed by collective action project personnel.” The compound is introduced as follows:

In the case of open source software development projects, we see an interesting compound of the private investment and collective action models of innovation. We term this compound the “private-collective” innovation model. In this model, participants in open source software projects use their own resources to privately invest in creating novel software code. In principle, these innovators could then claim proprietary rights over their code, but instead they choose to freely reveal it as a public good. Clearly, the net result of this behavior appears to offer society the best of both worlds—new knowledge is created by private funding and then offered freely to all.
— p. 6 of the PDF (journal p. 213)

The private benefits that survive free revealing are catalogued with supporting citations. Contributors must solve problems to create novel code, and the process itself yields learning and enjoyment and a sense of ownership and control over the work product — benefits contributors report valuing in open source work but not in programming carried out for hire, where firms monitor how programmers work (Lakhani and Wolf 2001; Lakhani and von Hippel 2000; Moon and Sproull 2000). Code is information and therefore nonrival: any number of people can use it simultaneously, so a developer can contribute code as a public good and simultaneously use it for private, somewhat different purposes; modules are regularly created by individual users for their own needs and then revealed. Some private benefits can only be obtained because the code is revealed — the private learning from critiques and corrections supplied by others. Community participation itself yields benefits unavailable to free riders, and intense, sustainable cooperation might outweigh individual user rewards; the authors quote John Elster on the psychology of cooperation and note that Rabin (1993) and Fehr and Schmidt (1999) show that a prisoner’s dilemma in material payoffs can become a coordination game with cooperation as an equilibrium once social motivations are included:

As John Elster remarks (1986, p. 132): “Cooperation reflects a transformation of individual psychology so as to include the feeling of solidarity, altruism, fairness, and the like. Collective action ceases to become a prisoner’s dilemma because members cease to regard participation as costly: It becomes a benefit in itself, over and above the public good it is intended to produce.”
— p. 9 of the PDF (journal p. 216)

Because these benefits are applied and “awarded” by the contributor herself, they are selective incentives in the collective action sense that do not require project managers to provide or monitor them — the load-bearing mechanism of the private-collective model.

Putting the two observations together — the interlinked public and private aspects of contributions, and the low competitive advantage of keeping privately generated code private — yields the paper’s answer to the question Lerner and Tirole (2000) posed, and the one-sentence theory of the paper:

If we combine our observations regarding the interlinked public and private aspects of contributions made to open source software projects with our earlier observation regarding the generally low competitive advantage associated with keeping privately generated code private, then we have a reasonable and reasonably likely answer to this question: Programmers contribute freely to the provision of a public good because they garner private benefits from doing so.
— p. 10 of the PDF (journal p. 217)

The authors are careful to say that open source does not undermine either parent model; it demonstrates the utility of combining them into a private-collective incentive model for settings with interlinked private and collective incentives, “and perhaps elsewhere as well.”

4. Evidence and methods

This is a theory-construction paper, and its evidence base is correspondingly heterogeneous; the authors do not present new data. The load-bearing materials are four. First, two illustrative case histories (Apache, Fetchmail) drawn from Raymond’s (1999a) account and project records, used to fix the typical project anatomy (initiator-maintainers, gatekeepers, authorized code, free-riding majority). Second, survey evidence on contributor motivation cited from prior work — most prominently the Boston Consulting Group survey of open source participants by Lakhani and Wolf (2001), plus Hermann, Hertel and Niedner’s (2000) Linux study and Lakhani and von Hippel (2000) on user-to-user assistance — establishing that contributors rate personal learning, enjoyment, and use value as central motivators and value ownership and control over their work. Third, the empirical regularities of collective action and user innovation drawn from the cited literature (Olson 1967; Ostrom 1998; Allen 1983 on collective invention; Franke and Shah 2001 on sports equipment communities; Morrison, Roberts and von Hippel 2000; Harhoff et al. 2000 on voluntary information spillovers). Fourth, the paper’s own field-level observations — that open source projects do no active recruiting and apply no anti-free-rider pressure — presented as “informal observation,” i.e., stylized facts offered without systematic measurement.

Because the target audience is organization scholars unfamiliar with the setting, §3 includes an unusually practical methodological section that doubles as a map of the data that would later power the empirical literature on open collaboration. The paper names the main access points: SourceForge project pages (code listings, mailing lists, logs); the Concurrent Versioning System (CVS) logs that record what was committed to the official code, by whom — with commit privileges often restricted to a few trusted gatekeepers; public mailing-list archives organized into time-stamped threads (e.g., Geocrawler); and real-time or private channels (Internet Relay Chat, private e-mail, direct communication) whose absence from public logs can bias studies of motives and coordination. It cites exemplar studies using each source, and ends by recommending that researchers construct an “intellectual genealogy” of a project — a map of major changes in architecture and of the decisions and contributions that shaped the software. 5. Findings: what the private-collective model buys organization science

Because the paper proposes rather than tests, its “findings” are the analytical consequences it draws in §3, each of which is a candidate hypothesis for the empirical study of voluntary contribution:

  • Self-provision of selective incentives. The selective incentives that matter most — private functional value from the code, learning, and enjoyment — are applied and “awarded” by the contributor to herself. This may largely obviate the need for project managers to provide or monitor selective incentives within a project, and it weakens the standard argument that large groups require central governance to discourage free riding.
  • Free riders are an asset, not a threat. Because contributors self-reward, their concern about free riders diminishes. The paper’s informal observation is that contributors regard free riders positively: adopters raise the project’s market share and may help set de facto standards, and many “free riders” of code still contribute non-code inputs such as bug reports; the more users and usage, the faster bugs are identified and fixed (Raymond 1999a):
The “self-rewarding” of important private benefits by participants in open source software projects that are not also available to free riders may considerably diminish participants’ concern about free riders. Indeed, informal observation in the field of open source software development suggests that contributors actually regard free riders as an asset. Free riders that adopt open source code without contributing to it nonetheless increase the “market share” and importance of the project and may help set de facto standards.
— p. 11 of the PDF (journal p. 218)
  • Governance questions are reopened, not closed. The immediate nature of private rewards implies that active recruiting matters less, but also that contributors may have weaker incentives to stay with a project long term than if rewards were allocated gradually by managers. The paper raises open questions about how members join and leave projects, the emergence of social categories (such as core-developer status that entrepreneurs may make valuable, rare, and restricted), the nature of social integration, and the role of leadership — noting the hacker norm that work cannot be mandated by a leader, who has no monetary incentives or legal basis to enforce cooperation, so leadership in distributed innovation may resemble that of “a playing coach.”
  • Boundary conditions on generality. Free revealing of privately developed innovations is not unique to open source: it has been observed in industrial equipment, in closed-source software developed by firms, and in sporting equipment innovations by individual end users (Allen 1983; Morrison et al. 2000; Harhoff et al. 2000; Franke and Shah 2001). What may be unique to knowledge and information products is that users carry out the entire innovation process — design, distribution, field support, improvement — because software can be produced and distributed essentially for free on the Web; physical products still require manufacturers because production and distribution exhibit economies of scale (von Hippel 2001). Even physical products have an information-design stage where CAD models can be freely revealed as readily as software code, and contributors to physical public goods (an architect on a public bridge project) gain private learning, enjoyment, and reputation that free riders cannot match. The authors’ judgment is that the model “can apply quite broadly,” with the full-function community form reserved for information goods.
  • A research agenda for organization science. The paper calls for distinguishing incentives facing firms that assign employees to contribute from incentives facing individual contributors; for studying the social integration of contributors and the value of social categories; and for testing how far the model travels to other fields. It closes by observing that answering these questions might lead to rethinking the very concept of “organization for innovation” (pp. 10–12 of the PDF; journal pp. 217–219).

6. Position in the literature

The paper sits at the confluence of three streams and is best read as their synthesis.

Its own research stream: user innovation and free revealing. The first author’s program runs from The Sources of Innovation (von Hippel 1988) — users, not manufacturers, innovate when they hold the relevant sticky information — through the economics of user innovation (von Hippel 1998) to the claim that free revealing can be privately rational (Harhoff, Henkel and von Hippel, cited as 2003; Allen 1983; Morrison et al. 2000; Franke and Shah 2001). The paper extends this program from individual user innovators to user innovation communities that jointly produce and distribute a public good, and it supplies the incentive-theoretic bridge to the collective action tradition. The second author’s knowledge-management and community work (von Krogh 1998, 2002; von Krogh, Spaeth and Lakhani 2003, on joining and specialization) contributes the community-side mechanisms. The paper is also a direct answer to Lerner and Tirole (2000), whose “simple economics of open source” posed the canonical question — “Why should thousands of top-notch programmers contribute freely to the provision of a public good?” — and whose career-concern account of contributor motivation the private-collective model complements rather than replaces: the paper adds the self-awarded, process-based private benefits that do not require an audience to observe one’s performance.

Competing and adjacent accounts of open source organization. Within organization science, the paper’s incentive account is one half of a two-part founding statement. The companion half is the governance account in O’Mahony (2003), “Guarding the Commons” (in this topic’s Part II library), which asks how community-managed projects protect their work through licenses and legal structures; where von Hippel and von Krogh explain why people contribute, O’Mahony explains how communities keep the resulting commons from being expropriated — the two papers together map the 2003 research frontier of open source. The empirical sharpening came in Henkel (2006), “Selective Revealing in Open Innovation Processes: The Case of Embedded Linux”: studying 268 firms contributing to embedded Linux, Henkel showed that firms do reveal, but selectively — keeping strategically important code proprietary — which converts the private-collective model’s binary choice (keep secret vs. freely reveal) into a continuum and extends it from individual users to profit-seeking firms. The book-length generalization is von Hippel (2005), Democratizing Innovation, which folds the private-collective logic into the broader claim that user-centered innovation processes (lead users, innovation toolkits, innovation communities) are socially desirable and increasingly feasible. Later governance work in this library continues the trajectory: O’Mahony and Ferraro (2007) study the emergence of governance in an open source community; O’Mahony and Karp (2022) ask how platform participation strategies evolve “from proprietary to collective governance,” transplanting the private-collective question to platform ecosystems.

Relation to the Part I core papers on Hugging Face. This 2003 paper is the organizing theory for the topic’s empirical question — why developers voluntarily contribute to a shared AI platform. Greenstein et al. (2022), the Hugging Face teaching case, documents a platform that is in effect a private-collective experiment: model developers privately fund training and fine-tuning, then freely reveal models, weights, and code on the Hub, capturing private benefits (usable artifacts for downstream tasks, reputation, community standing, and access to others’ models) that non-contributors cannot obtain. Yeverechyahu et al. (2024), on GitHub Copilot and open source contributions, is a direct test of the model’s comparative statics: if contributions respond to the private cost-benefit of contributing, then a tool that lowers the private cost of producing code should raise contribution volume, holding the free-rider option unchanged. Wu, Lin and Li (2025), the Llama 2 difference-in-differences on Hugging Face developers, tests the complement: an open-weight release raises the private value of building on the shared base (and lowers the cost of producing a contribution), and developers respond heterogeneously — high-experience developers retreat from commoditized niches while low-experience developers explore — a pattern the private-collective lens reads as developers re-optimizing their privately capturable benefits around the new public good. The participation skew that Laufer et al. (2025) measure across two million Hub models — a small core of active contributors atop a vast downloading majority — restates in platform data the paper’s 2003 observation that most downloaders free ride and only a small proportion contribute; and the paper’s claim that free riders are an asset (adoption, market share, bug reports) is the natural prior for interpreting Hub download counts as both usage and contribution of a kind. Longpre et al. (2025), tracing participation and power in the open-weight ecosystem, extends the private-collective model’s open questions about who captures private benefits and whether influence concentrates among a few sponsors and gatekeepers. Finally, the paper frames the normative stakes that Azoulay et al. (2024) and White et al. (2024) revisit for AI: if privately funded innovation freely revealed as a public good yields “the best of both worlds,” then open-weight AI inherits that presumption — but the 2003 model, built for code whose misuse risk was modest, says nothing about safety externalities, competitive dynamics among platform sponsors, or the licensing granularity (open weights are not open source) that the Model Openness Framework now formalizes.

7. Limitations

Boundaries the authors themselves state. The paper repeatedly flags that its empirical claims are provisional: the answer to the Lerner–Tirole puzzle is “reasonable and reasonably likely” rather than demonstrated; the evidence that contributors regard free riders as an asset is presented as “informal observation”; the key motivational claims rest on surveys conducted by others; and the authors state that “much of the research needed to answer that puzzle is yet to be done.” The generalization to other fields is explicitly hedged as a belief (“We think that it is likely that it can apply quite broadly”), and the full-function community form is confined to information goods because physical production and distribution have scale economies. The authors also acknowledge, in the form of research questions, the limits of their account of membership dynamics, social integration, and leadership — all left open.

My independent assessment (layered and labeled, distinguishing the paper’s claims from the reader’s extensions):

  • Author claims (paper claims). Open source contributions are not pure public goods; contributors capture private benefits (use value of the code, learning, enjoyment, ownership and control, community standing) unavailable to free riders; free revealing is individually rational when rivalry is low and diffusion is cheap; these private benefits act as self-awarded selective incentives, reducing the need for managed incentives and for concern about free riding. All four are asserted as the paper’s explanation of observed practice.
  • Evidence. The supporting evidence is real but light by later standards: two illustrative case histories; secondary survey results about stated motives; and the authors’ informal field observations about recruiting and free riding. Motivation claims rest on self-reports that are vulnerable to post-hoc rationalization — a contributor may say she codes for learning and enjoyment while also, or instead, building career capital or expecting reciprocity, channels the paper mentions but does not weigh. No evidence is offered on magnitudes: the model predicts that contributions rise when private benefits rise or revealing costs fall, but the paper tests no such comparative statics.
  • Inferences (my extensions, not the paper’s statements). Several readings of the paper travel beyond its text: that self-awarded private benefits are sufficient to sustain contribution at scale (the paper argues they explain observed practice, not that they dominate in all projects); that free riders are an asset in general (the paper’s observation is about adoption-dependent software projects; for goods where contributions are rival in maintainers’ attention, or where security vulnerabilities and liability matter, a large passive user base can be a cost); and that the model transfers to model hubs on Hugging Face (the paper predates platforms, and Hub contribution differs from code contribution — dataset curation, fine-tune uploads, and evaluations involve different cost structures, private benefits, and misuse externalities). These transfers are hypotheses, not findings of the paper.
  • Open questions the paper itself leaves. How do incentives differ for firms that assign employees to contribute versus individual contributors? How and why do members join and leave projects, and what sustains long-term participation when immediate process rewards dominate? What are the functions of leadership in distributed innovation, and how do leaders emerge without authority? How far does the model extend beyond information goods? The later literature — including the Part I Hugging Face papers — can be read as a sustained attempt to answer the first and second of these with platform data.

A contextual caveat for citation: the paper is a theory-proposal essay in an organization science journal, published in 2003, the same year as O’Mahony’s “Guarding the Commons” and Harhoff–Henkel–von Hippel’s formalization of free revealing; claims about “today” (SourceForge’s 50,000 projects, Apache’s 60% share) describe the 2002–2003 world and should not be cited as current facts.

8. Conclusion and takeaways

The paper’s contribution is a recombination: take the private investment model’s insight that people innovate when they privately benefit, and the collective action model’s insight that public goods suffer free riding, drop the one assumption in each that makes open source paradoxical, and obtain a model in which privately funded innovation is freely revealed because contributors privately capture benefits that free riders cannot. The one-sentence theory is the answer the authors give to Lerner and Tirole: programmers contribute freely to a public good because they garner private benefits from doing so. The empirical corollaries — self-awarded selective incentives, benign free riding, large-group viability, and the information-goods boundary of full-function user communities — define the research agenda the paper hands to organization science.

Three takeaways travel best to the Hugging Face topic. First, the phenomenon of volunteer contribution does not require altruism or irrationality; the analytical task is to identify the private benefits that contributors capture and that free riders cannot — use value, learning, reputation, community — and to measure how contributions respond when those benefits change. Second, the theory’s comparative statics are testable with platform data: cheapening contribution (Copilot), enriching the shared base (Llama 2), or changing the rivalry structure of adopters should shift contribution volume and composition in predictable directions, which is precisely what the Part I difference-in-differences papers attempt. Third, free riding and skewed participation are features of the private-collective equilibrium, not evidence of failure: a hub where most users download and few contribute may still be an efficient arrangement for producing and diffusing the public good — a benchmark against which the “participation crisis” readings of the Hub data should be evaluated.
