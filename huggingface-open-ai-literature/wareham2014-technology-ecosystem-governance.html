One-sentence summary

Through a case study of a multinational ERP software vendor and its ecosystem of implementation and solution partners, Wareham, Fox and Cano Giner argue that technology ecosystem governance is a design problem of managing three paradoxical tensions — standard–variety in outputs, control–autonomy among actors, and collective–individual in identifications — using governance mechanisms that simultaneously increase desirable variance and decrease undesirable variance, and they show how latent, complementary tensions ("dualities") can be triggered by plurality, change, and scarcity into salient, contradictory tensions ("dualisms") that call for quasi-regulatory governance responses.

Reading guide (tied to "Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy"): This paper is the canonical qualitative template for studying governance inside a technology ecosystem, and it supplies the vocabulary that the Hugging Face papers in this library repeatedly need. The three tensions translate almost one-to-one onto the model-hub setting: standard–variety — the Hub's shared interfaces (the Transformers library, safetensors, model-card and dataset conventions) versus a long tail of heterogeneous models and derivative fine-tunes (Laufer et al. 2025 count roughly two million models); control–autonomy — who decides what may be uploaded, licensed, gated, or removed, and how much self-selection developers retain (compare Yue & Nagle 2024 on devolving control of PyTorch, and Azoulay et al. 2024 on openness versus control in generative AI); and collective–individual — individual builders pursuing private returns while the Hub depends on shared social goods (reusable code, datasets, documentation, community moderation) in the sense of Longpre et al. 2025 on power and participation. Where the Part I papers give us large-scale measurement of one ecosystem (Laufer et al. 2025; Khoshbash 2026; Longpre et al. 2025), Wareham et al. give the mechanism-level theory of how an ecosystem's centre deliberately engineers variance — and of why excessive variety without control can poison the ecosystem. Read it before the Hub papers as the "governance design grammar"; read Jacobides et al. 2018 for the structural definition of ecosystems and O'Mahony & Ferraro 2007 for how such governance can emerge rather than be designed.

1. Background and motivation: governance as the frontier of platform strategy

Wareham et al. open from a well-established premise in the platform literature: a platform strategy lets a core firm orchestrate a rich portfolio of contributions made by many independent actors who form an ecosystem of heterogeneous complementors around a stable core. This form of organizing has succeeded in smartphones, gaming, commercial software, and industrial sectors. The distinctively governance question arises because ecosystems must satisfy two opposing requirements at once. The paper's abstract states the problem in one compressed sentence, which also serves as the paper's abstract-quote anchor:

Through an extensive case study of a business software ecosystem consisting of a major multinational manufacturer of enterprise resource planning software at the core and a heterogeneous system of independent implementation partners and solution developers on the periphery, our research identifies three salient tensions that characterize the ecosystem: standard–variety, control–autonomy, and collective–individual.
— Wareham, Fox & Cano Giner (2014), abstract (p. 2 of the PDF; journal p. 1195)

Technology ecosystems need stability and homogeneity so that participants can amortize common investments in standard components, and variability and heterogeneity so that evolving market demand can be met. The authors call this the stability–evolvability problem and, following Tilson et al. (2010, p. 6), the "paradox of change." Their central claims are that (i) this general paradox manifests across a small number of underlying tensions; (ii) governing an ecosystem is essentially a design problem (Simons 1995) in which specific governance levers can direct tensions toward a sustainable equilibrium or, if mis-implemented, toward "a degenerative evolution of the ecosystem"; and (iii) the existing literature, which had addressed stability–evolvability conceptually (Tilson et al. 2010; Tiwana et al. 2010; Yoo et al. 2010), lacked empirical and theoretical insight into how the tension is manifested and governed. This motivates two research questions:

Research Question 1: How are the main tensions in technology ecosystems addressed in technology ecosystem governance?
Research Question 2: Are contradictory and complementary logics present in technology ecosystems? If so, how are they governed?

The empirical setting is enterprise resource planning (ERP) software, a sector in which providers such as SAP and Oracle have long relied on regional implementers for country-specific modifications, sector-specific add-ons, and company-specific customizations. ERP is chosen deliberately: it is one of the few empirical examples of a technology platform functioning in a for-profit, business-to-business context, and its extreme heterogeneity — one standard suite must serve sectors as diffuse as paper processing, healthcare, financial services, manufacturing, and education, across more than 100 legal and accounting regimes — makes it "an extreme form of technology ecosystem" whose governance of salient tensions is "likely to be one of the more illustrative and subtle exemplars."

2. Core concepts and argument (following the paper's own sections)

2.1 The ecosystem concept and the stability–evolvability equilibrium

The paper defines technology ecosystems as product platforms made of core components produced by the platform owner and complements produced by autonomous firms on the periphery. Two primary characteristics are required: the platform should perform an important function within a "system of use" or solve an important technical problem in an industry, and it should be easy to connect to or build on the core so that the system of use can expand in new and even unanticipated directions. The ecological metaphor is taken seriously: the central concept borrowed from biology is resilience — the ability of an ecosystem to maintain its own inertia while adapting to exogenous shocks with an innate generative ability to evolve endogenously. Excessive evolvability without stability and inertia renders a system fragmented in use, financially unsustainable, and irrelevant; excessive stability without evolvability blocks adaptation to market and technology change. The paper therefore frames governance as the management of desirable variance (which should be increased) versus undesirable variance (which should be decreased):

Without evolvability, the platform cannot adequately adjust to changes in customer requirements, market shifts, and technology evolution. Without demonstrated stability, complementors and customers would have little assurance that their substantial financial and human resource investments can yield long-term returns.
— Wareham, Fox & Cano Giner (2014), p. 3 of the PDF (journal p. 1196)

Outputs are produced by complementors — autonomous actors who channel entrepreneurial instinct toward client needs — so the trade-off between stability and evolvability is ultimately borne by three related objects: outputs, actors, and identifications. Each maps onto one tension (Table 1 below).

2.2 Outputs: standard–variety

The economic logic of platforms is that high standardization of the core enables economies of scale and amortization of fixed costs, while on the periphery economies of scope are realized through specialized complements and constant experimentation (Baldwin & Clark 2000). Stability is thus leveraged by design to maximize variety. But variety is not confined to the periphery: core components also evolve, and what remains stable through platform evolution are the interfaces — Baldwin's "thin crossing points" — that govern interaction between layers. These are the architectural control points (Woodard 2008) where the platform operator can, via property and other legal rights of exclusion, grant or deny actors access to the system (Boudreau & Hagiu 2009; Jacobides et al. 2006; Rochet & Tirole 2003). Interface coupling is the key design lever: loosely coupled ecosystems with thin interfaces enable generativity at many layers but risk fragmentation, inefficiency, and overcrowding; tightly coupled architectures protect lock-in and integration but can stifle innovation and constrain evolvability. Governance must therefore run variance-decreasing and variance-increasing mechanisms simultaneously across different dimensions to reach an optimal aggregate effect — an early statement of what the paper later formalizes as "simultaneity."

2.3 Actors: control–autonomy

Complementors act as entrepreneurs, invoking the speed of market mechanisms while focusing their own portfolio of domain expertise, sector knowledge, and relational capital. The risk is the mirror image: unchecked variance in quality creates agency costs in which the platform core bears the negative consequences of poor complements. The paper cites Atari as the canonical cautionary tale — "Hagiu and Halaburda (2010) found that Atari’s inability to control the overcrowding of low-quality games and subsequent downward price pressure in their ecosystem was central to the platform’s demise." The normative conclusion is compressed into a memorable contrast:

In other words, 200 well-designed flowers can be more beneficial to the sustainability of the ecosystem than 1,000 of a variable quality.
— Wareham, Fox & Cano Giner (2014), p. 5 of the PDF (journal p. 1198)

Control levers therefore cannot focus on output quality alone; they must also address complement quantity, the distribution of complementor efforts across market niches, the frequency of complement releases, and the other managerial decisions that shape complement supply. The ideal is what the authors, citing Faraj et al. (2011), call constrained serendipity: variance-reducing mechanisms to control poor quality, ensure professional conduct, and constrain excess supply, running alongside variance-increasing mechanisms that leverage autonomy for innovative responses.

2.4 Identifications: collective–individual

Individual complementors work for financial compensation, career advancement, and other extrinsic motivations; yet the ecosystem needs cohesion, a canon of best practice, and a portfolio of reusable knowledge that enables scalable exploitation of common innovations. The alignment problem is worse in industrial ecosystems because complementors often deliver in consortia, generating "joint action interdependence" (Caglio & Ditillo 2008, p. 877) — team production in which nonsequential, complementary processes dominate and direct lines of accountability are hard to define, let alone enforce (Miller et al. 2008). Governance must therefore cultivate identification with multiple foci at once — the immediate task/client, the organizational group (partner consortia), and the entire ecosystem — using collective identifications to reduce undesirable variance (toward reusable knowledge, positive externalities, and social goods) and individual identifications to increase desirable variance (creative, explorative, entrepreneurial responses).

2.5 Framing paradox: dualism versus duality, and the triggers of contradiction

The paper's second conceptual move borrows from the paradox literature (Smith & Lewis 2011; Farjoun 2010) a distinction between two ways a tension can be framed. In a dualism framing, tensions are mutually exclusive trade-offs — the classic "exploration versus exploitation" zero-sum view (March 1991). In a duality framing, the poles are complementary, mutually enabling, and constituent of one another — a positive-sum logic in which exploration needs the economic sustainability provided by exploitation and vice versa. Effective ecosystem governance, the authors argue, should frame tensions toward complementary, not contradictory, outcomes:

…can function as complementary and mutually enabling attractors in a holistic system, as a duality (Farjoun 2010).
— Wareham, Fox & Cano Giner (2014), p. 3 of the PDF (journal p. 1196)

Drawing on Smith and Lewis (2011), the paper argues that paradoxical tensions remain latent and complementary while dormant, and become salient and contradictory under three triggers: plurality (many similar options, overpopulation by complements, paralyzing choice), change (evolution of base technologies or market conditions), and scarcity (elevated competition for few clients, discouraging cooperation and best-practice sharing). 3. Evidence and methods: a grounded-theory case study of one ERP ecosystem

The research design is inductive and grounded-theory based (Glaser & Strauss 1967), consistent with the exploratory nature of the questions. Data collection ran in two main phases, corresponding to the two research questions, and was decomposed into eight specific stages (documented in the appendix's Table A.1 with exemplary questions, thematic codes, and outcomes). The empirical base:

  • Interviews: 31 semi-structured interviews between November 2007 and June 2010, selected by theoretical sampling for similarities as well as differences: software vendor employees (N = 16) across functional areas including channel management, development management, and R&D; independent software development and implementation partners (N = 10); and customers (N = 5) in distinct industry sectors. Interviews ended at theoretical saturation, were attended by a minimum of two researchers, and a third researcher validated transcriptions.
  • Ethnographic elements: attendance at two three-day international partner conventions, including presentations and workshops designed for implementation partners.
  • Archival data: the software vendor's partner-program guidelines and other archival materials, triangulated with interviews and with industry literature on competing software platforms.
  • Validation: five staged validation incidents cross-checked information provided by partners and customers against the vendor's representatives and vice versa (three in phase 1, two in phase 2).
  • Analysis: open coding to identify general concepts, axial coding to organize concepts into categories with properties and relationships, and selective coding to theorize the ecosystem as an aggregate phenomenon; categories were triangulated across four sources — partner-program guidelines (archival), vendor interviews, partner and customer interviews, and the literature of §2.

Case background. The vendor sells an ERP software suite to organizations of roughly 50–5,000 users, so small and medium enterprises are the majority of clients, and it operates in more than 45 countries across Europe, Asia, and North and Latin America. Because clients prefer to change software to fit existing business structures rather than change their processes, the vendor's core code is open to its partners, who configure, customize, and build add-ons on it. Licence revenues for core products are shared between vendor and partners; partners own licences for their own solutions. The sales-and-implementation process runs through three activity categories — product and channel development, early-stage sales, and implementation and late-stage sales (the paper's Figure 1) — with customer training and support largely delegated to partners. Partners are heterogeneous: the paper profiles three archetypes, summarized in Table 3. This heterogeneity, in the vendor's own words, "often leads to excessive variance in the quality of both complementors and complements."

4. Findings

4.1 RQ1: the mechanisms of technology ecosystem governance

Outputs (standard–variety). Entry as a registered partner is not particularly difficult, requiring approval and validation by the vendor. To enrich creative response, the vendor offers code libraries, technical support, integrated development environments with a proprietary coding language, financing facilities, centralised sales and marketing support, and a community-based platform for sharing and recombining tested code, modules, and vertical applications. Undesirable variance in process and output quality is reduced through an advanced regime of certification — of employees, of partners, of complements and solutions — plus development and implementation templates, third-party verification of solution compatibility, and required use of an implementation tool/process (Table 4 of the paper). A recurring theme is that mechanisms are embedded and dual-function: "the integrated software development tool serves to increase complementor productivity, functional exploration, and creative problem solving. At the same time, it constrains specific technological attributes that ensure quality and compatibility." Variance-increasing and variance-decreasing functions are thus often built into the same instrument.

Actors (control–autonomy). The central mechanism for negotiating this tension is self-selection. Partners enter the ecosystem with very high autonomy and then choose how much control to accept, based on the perceived value of higher control levels:

Via self-selection, they choose the level and nature of control that is appropriate for their business portfolio based on the perceived benefits realised by adopting higher control levels.
— Wareham, Fox & Cano Giner (2014), p. 10 of the PDF (journal p. 1203)

Control is operationalized as a graduated regime of five partner certification levels (the paper's Table 5). Entry begins at level 1, where partners may remain perpetually; advancement is elective and, with limited exceptions, inclusive (level 3 is a small-business specialist), with higher levels building on lower ones. Advancement to levels 4 and 5 requires accumulating internal points, awarded for achievements such as personnel certifications, specialist competency designations, validated customer references, technical testing of applications, participation in customer satisfaction surveys, and the volume of core licences or revenue sold — and valid for a limited time. Higher levels of certification for both solutions and partners require more stringent control (e.g., testing by a third party, documented industry-level implementations) yet simultaneously offer value to the periphery partners (higher-quality solutions and services, co-branding with the vendor) and the core (an expanded catalogue of high-quality partner solutions, greater scale and scope of the ecosystem, additional revenue). The key design insight is that a value proposition is subsumed in the control infrastructure: each level of control imparts value to both the periphery and the core, so submitting to control resembles a market transaction in which something is exchanged for expected value and status. The vendor's Figure 2 renders this with a solar-system metaphor: partners of different types reach similar control levels through diverse means. Because the pdftotext copy scrambles the columns of the paper's Table 5, this note does not reproduce the full level-by-level matrix; the qualitative description above follows the paper's prose (§4.1.2, pp. 10–12 of the PDF; journal pp. 1203–1205).

Identifications (collective–individual). Because participation in a for-profit ecosystem is premised on profit-seeking, individual identifications predominate; governance must cultivate collective ones concurrently. The vendor's main partner web portal links several hundred independently maintained websites offering discussion forums, blogs, white papers, rated solution listings, job and event listings, code samples, bug reports, tutorials, and best-practice exchange. Deeper interdependence is cultivated through a partner-finding platform — a directory of several hundred thousand partners publishing practice and technical profiles, thousands of registered add-on applications, and collaboration opportunities — which supports multi-partner projects and geographic or functional expansion. Collective identification also requires informal governance that communicates legitimacy and status: drawing on Stinchcombe (1965) (liability of newness), Akerlof (1970) (quality uncertainty), and Podolny (1994) (status as a quality signal), the authors describe status as a currency that the core awards to the periphery by merit-based affiliation (Castellucci & Ertug 2010). The graduated certification levels and graduated co-branding are the clearest instruments: higher levels confer greater legitimacy, and the authors found several partner applications "virtually indistinguishable from the core applications" — a visible convergence of peripheral identity toward the core.

4.2 RQ2: transitions between complementary and contradictory logics

The second phase of the study analyses three illustrative cases, one per primary tension, in which complementary logics slid toward contradictory ones.

Example 1 — Complement generativity (standard–variety). The vendor's brand demonstrably helps partners sell ("[Software vendor]'s brand is helping partners to sell [software application]… Now partners have more opportunities, especially access to big deals" — implementation partner). But liberal governance of complements generates two pathologies: partners face uncertainty about the evolution of core products (new versions can absorb their add-ons, making compatibility expensive), and an overpopulation of similar verticals produces redundancy with no common amortization — several partners "competing in the same accounts with similar solutions." Paradoxically, some partners asked for tighter governance (vendor "seal of approval" on a handful of vertical solutions per segment) to lower clients' total cost of ownership, even at the price of less differentiation. The vendor refrains from heavy regulation, accepting that locally embedded partners best handle heterogeneity while acknowledging the residual agency problem: "officially [software vendor] does not have any commitment to the customer. However, it always comes back to [software vendor] anyway." The trigger here is plurality combined with change — uncertainty about platform evolution on top of an overcrowded complement market.

Example 2 — Standardised implementation strategies in heterogeneous markets (control–autonomy). Localization — translation, legal requirements, and local business practices — is the single largest cost borne by the ecosystem collectively. The vendor therefore developed a standardised implementation method, recommended for all and required at higher certification levels, embedding flexibility through filters and project profiles ("Partners can use the templates more or less as they like"). Yet the one-size-fits-all ideal is admittedly utopian: partners complain the method is not easy enough to handle customizations, risks over-automation, and cannot accommodate the differences between large and small partners, so they resort to pragmatic workarounds, “use what they need and ignore what they don’t.” The vendor's response is to leverage market mechanisms in place of control:

You cannot test for the business functionality of the product. It is totally impossible. This is why we require the customer references—let the market speak. This is a very risky strategy, of course, because as mentioned, at the end of the day it all comes back to [software vendor].
— Software vendor channel manager, quoted in Wareham, Fox & Cano Giner (2014), p. 15 of the PDF (journal p. 1208)

Here the contradictory framing is driven by plurality — the extreme heterogeneity of the ERP market — plus some emotional resistance to centralised control.

Example 3 — Competitive balance between partners (collective–individual). There are no barriers to becoming a partner beyond certification, and the vendor does not cap partner numbers for antitrust reasons. The result is intense partner-on-partner competition: “If you ask a partner who their biggest competitor is, they’ll say, ‘Another partner.’ … If there are three partners competing for the same work, [software vendor] always wins.” Training centres that also compete as developers create conflicts of interest; partners perceive the vendor as focused on licence sales with "tremendous pressure" to report results monthly; and country-level modifications that should be shared and amortized are kept private — each partner runs its own "standard plus" version as a point of differentiation, with no incentive to share with other partners or the vendor. The triggers are plurality and scarcity: many partners concentrated in some markets, scarce coverage in others.

Summary of RQ2 findings. The case confirms Smith and Lewis's position that tensions can be framed as either complementary or contradictory, and that plurality, change, and scarcity render them salient. Plurality proved the dominant trigger across the cases; scarcity played a strong secondary role; and — notably — change was not a major factor in the authors' data, possibly because technology ecosystems are designed for evolvability and participants are accustomed to it. Examples 1 and 3 "resemble a classic 'market failure' from the economics literature" (Bator 1958), so the suggested managerial responses are analogous to public-policy responses — a form of quasi-regulation: limit the number of complement types in defined functional areas and increase coordination to reduce redundancy (Example 1); legitimize variance from standard methods and build feedback into future versions (Example 2); elect market winners in overcapacity regions and seed underserved markets (Example 3). 4.3 Simultaneity as the core governance requirement

The paper's second major theoretical contribution (beyond the three tensions) is the concept of simultaneity: governance mechanisms repeatedly serve variance-increasing and variance-decreasing objectives at the same time — (i) within a single tension (increasing variance on characteristic alpha while decreasing it on beta, e.g., development tools that raise functional variety while constraining quality and compatibility standards), (ii) across multiple tensions (application certification simultaneously serves actor autonomy and collective reuse), and (iii) across multiple constituents (self-selection matches complementary interests, giving value to both partners and core). Table 8 of the paper formalizes these three forms. Because plurality proved the main trigger of contradictory framing, the authors infer that heterogeneity itself may be the binding constraint on ecosystem growth: "it may well be that a main constraint of ecosystem growth is its degree of heterogeneity," and "there may be a pragmatic limit to how much heterogeneity any single governance regime might accommodate." As the ecosystem expands, plurality reframes latent tensions as contradictory; the actors' decisions become adverse to ecosystem health; and "the same network effects that have enabled its growth ultimately facilitate its demise."

5. Position in the literature

Within its own research stream, the paper positions itself as an empirical and theoretical deepening of work that had treated the stability–evolvability tension conceptually. Its direct interlocutors are Tilson et al. (2010), Tiwana et al. (2010), and Yoo et al. (2010) on digital infrastructure and platform evolution, and it answers Tiwana et al.'s loose-versus-tight framing of platform governance by showing that governance "must do far more than simply mitigate agency conflicts," operating through three distinct tensions and simultaneous variance mechanisms. On the platform-economics side it builds on Gawer & Cusumano (2002, 2008), Baldwin & Clark (2000), Baldwin & Woodard (2008) and Woodard (2008) on architecture and control points, Boudreau & Hagiu (2009), Rochet & Tirole (2003) and Parker & Van Alstyne (2005) on platform rules and network effects, and Jacobides et al. (2006) on industry architectures. Its paradox vocabulary comes from Farjoun (2010), Smith & Lewis (2011), and March (1991). Empirically it sits alongside the ERP-platform studies of Ceccagnoli et al. (2012) and Sarker et al. (2012), and it explicitly engages Boudreau's (2010, 2012) results on open platform strategies and complementor populations — including Boudreau's argument that complementor churn and heterogeneity shape innovation, which the authors take up in their future-research section. It also converses with the ecosystem-strategy canon: Jacobides et al. (2018) later supply the structural definition of ecosystems (interdependence, modularity, complementarities) that Wareham et al. take as given, while Adner & Kapoor (2010) treat value creation under technological interdependence — Wareham et al. supply the governance machinery inside such interdependence; Adner (2017) calls for ecosystems to be treated as structure, which this paper implicitly does by anchoring governance to outputs, actors, and identifications as structural objects.

Relation to the open-source and commons literature. The governance problem the paper dissects sits between two literatures it does not itself foreground but which this topic library makes salient. O'Mahony & Ferraro (2007) document how governance emerges in a community-managed software project (Debian) through legitimate, distributed authority; Wareham et al. show the opposite pole — governance designed by a for-profit core over a periphery. O'Mahony & Karp (2022) then ask how platform participation strategies evolve from proprietary to collective governance, which is precisely the transition Wareham et al.'s static design view cannot handle (they concede a cross-sectional perspective). von Hippel & von Krogh (2003) supply the private-collective model — individual benefit combined with free revealing — that underlies the paper's collective–individual tension, and Henkel (2006) documents selective revealing in embedded Linux, the firm-side analogue of the vendor's decision to open its core code while keeping partner solutions' source protected. Boudreau (2010) offers the sharpest adjacent contrast: he separates granting access from devolving control as two distinct open-platform strategies with different innovation effects, and Wareham et al.'s control–autonomy tension can be read as the governance-theoretic account of why a platform would mix the two rather than choose one.

6. Limitations

Author-stated limitations (paper claims). The authors are explicit that their design is inductive and exploratory, anchored to a single, anonymized ERP vendor, and that their view is cross-sectional: "we acknowledge that generativity may result in undesirable variance, our view is admittedly a cross-sectional one." They do not directly address ecosystem evolution through time, leaving to future research the question of how complementor churn, complementor maturity, and ecosystem maturity interact with governance. They also concede that governance may need to oscillate between archetypes as the ecosystem matures — loose control in seeding phases, stricter control at intermediate stages — citing Thrane (2007). Finally, they acknowledge that normative guidance on architecture is easier to infer than the elicitation of complementary versus contradictory logics, which "may be a question of trial and error, as was the case for our case ecosystem."

Evidence and its limits. The empirical core is 31 interviews plus archival partner-program material and two partner conventions, with five staged validation incidents. The interview counts are small, the vendor is anonymized, and complementor respondents were partly identified through initial recommendations from vendor management, which could bias toward cooperative partners. The evidence is largely perceptual — how partners and vendor managers frame tensions — triangulated against program guidelines but not against outcome data such as complement quality, sales, or churn. The paper's mechanism tables (its Tables 4–6) are presented as the vendor's own design catalogue; the authors classify mechanisms into variance-increasing and variance-decreasing categories, but the classification itself is interpretive and some instruments (training, community platforms, certification) plausibly serve both functions at once, which the paper acknowledges as "embedded" dual function.

Independent judgment (inferences by this reader). Several extensions in this note go beyond what the paper claims. First, the mapping of the three tensions onto Hugging Face (Table 5) is an analogy: model hubs differ from ERP ecosystems in that the "complementors" are largely unpaid, the core is a nonprofit-leaning intermediary rather than a licence-selling firm, and output quality is policed by community norms and benchmarks more than by certification. Second, the claim that heterogeneity is a binding constraint on ecosystem growth — "there may be a pragmatic limit to how much heterogeneity any single governance regime might accommodate" — is offered as an inference from the plurality findings and is not directly tested. Third, the paper treats governance as largely a design problem in which the core is the designer; it does not theorize cases in which governance is contested, emerges from below, or is co-created with complementors (the territory of O'Mahony & Ferraro 2007 and O'Mahony & Karp 2022). Fourth, the "duality versus dualism" framing is applied from the paradox literature rather than derived from the case; the authors use the case to illustrate the framing, not to test it.

Open questions left by the paper. What counts as "desirable" versus "undesirable" variance, and who decides, is left to the core firm's judgment; the paper does not resolve how an ecosystem should weigh the private losses of individual complementors against ecosystem-level social goods. Whether quasi-regulatory interventions (entry limits, seeding) are feasible or legal in for-profit settings — the vendor already refuses to cap partners for antitrust reasons — is unresolved. And the boundary conditions for the framework — young versus mature ecosystems, for-profit versus open-source (which the authors note may justify skewed weightings), and ecosystems whose core does not own the complements — remain open.

7. Conclusion and takeaways

The paper's contribution is a vocabulary and a mechanism map for ecosystem governance. Its takeaways for the Hugging Face and open-AI literature are threefold. First, openness and control are not opposites to be traded once, but tensions to be governed continuously: the Hub's challenge is not choosing between open and closed but calibrating variance across outputs, actors, and identifications with mechanisms that increase and decrease variance simultaneously. Second, excess variety is a governance failure, not a success signal: the "thousand flowers" logic that motivates open hubs carries the seed of their degeneration through overcrowding, redundancy, and quality variance — the exact pathologies the ERP case documents among complements. Third, plurality is the trigger to watch: as an ecosystem's population of complements and complementors grows, latent complementary tensions are reframed as contradictory, and the same network effects that built the ecosystem can unmoor it. The design question for any hub — and for the intermediaries, firms, and regulators studied in Longpre et al. (2025), Azoulay et al. (2024), and Jin et al. (2026) — is how to institutionalize variance governance before plurality does its damage.

In the extreme, the unconstrained growth of low-quality innovations can kill a platform. Effective governance is necessary. — Wareham, Fox & Cano Giner (2014), p. 19 of the PDF (journal p. 1212)
