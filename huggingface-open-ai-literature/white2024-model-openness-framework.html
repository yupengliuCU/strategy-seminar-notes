One-sentence take

Open weights are not an open model: under the Model Openness Framework a release may only be called “open” when every component required for its class is released under an appropriate open license, and the framework operationalizes that test across 17 components of the model development lifecycle organized into three ascending classes — Class III “Open Model,” Class II “Open Tooling,” Class I “Open Science.”

Reading guide: this paper is best read as the vocabulary document of the “Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy” topic. Nearly every other reading in the topic wants to talk about “open” AI — as a business model, a competitive threat, a policy object, or an empirical variable — and the MOF is the taxonomy that lets them say precisely which artifact is open, under which license, and which class of completeness the release reaches. Keep three distinctions from this note in mind when reading the rest of the topic: (i) open weights (downloadable parameters) is not open model (the full, license-compliant artifact set); (ii) openness (permissive licensing) and completeness (how many lifecycle components are released) are separate axes; (iii) MOF classes are cumulative, so “open” is a ladder, not a binary — although at each rung the license test is still all-or-nothing. The MOF vocabulary implicitly underlies or is extended by the topic readings: Azoulay, Krieger & Nagaraj (2024) on openness, control, and competition in generative AI; Yue & Nagle (2024) on opening access while keeping control rights in open collaboration; Longpre et al. (2025) on open-weight versus truly open models in the evolution of the open-AI ecosystem; and Fleischmann & Haeussler (2026), whose empirical study of “degrees of openness” across 22,137 Hugging Face base models cites White et al. (2024) explicitly as its conceptual anchor.

1. Motivation: why “open-source AI” claims mislead

The paper opens with a diagnosis familiar from the frontier-model debate: the most capable systems are black boxes — hard to audit, hard to explain, hard to reproduce — and although “the number of publicly available models has been growing, many of these models are falsely being promoted as ‘open-source,’ a practice that has been characterized as ‘openwashing’” (p. 1 of the PDF). The abstract states the core complaint directly:

Most models lack the necessary components for full understanding, auditing, and reproducibility, and some model producers use restrictive licenses whilst claiming that their models are “open source.” (Abstract, p. 1 of the PDF)

The paper names three interlocking problems behind the misleading claims (Section 2.2, pp. 2–3):

  • License mismatch. Open-source licenses were “designed to cover conventional software code and are not appropriate for the intricacies of ML models” (p. 3). They cover the model architecture, which is expressed in code; but parameters are data, and the paper argues they are “more aptly governed by open-data licenses than by open-source licenses” (p. 3). Producers nonetheless release weights under Apache 2.0 or MIT (see §5.2). Some even add conditions — e.g., outputs may not be used to train subsequent models, or trigger conditions that force a consumer to renegotiate the license — and fine-tuned derivatives of restrictively licensed models are sometimes re-released under Apache 2.0 “even though altering the original license is not legally permitted” (p. 3).
  • Missing components. Most releases contain only architecture plus final trained weights. Pretraining data and human-feedback data are almost never released, and evaluation results often cannot be reproduced because their disclosure is lacking (p. 3).
  • Widespread unlicensed sharing. Citing Osborne, Ding & Kirk’s quantitative study of Hugging Face Hub, the paper reports that 64.67% of models and 72.13% of datasets on the platform are shared without any license (p. 2).

Section 2.1 frames the background debate: openness has documented benefits (distributed auditing, adaptation to diverse domains and languages, scientific advance) and risks (disinformation, illegal content, security vulnerabilities). The paper relays the claim — from Kapoor et al. (2024) — that open foundation models have five distinctive properties that cut both ways: broader access, greater customizability, local adaptation and inference ability, the inability to rescind model access, and the inability to monitor or moderate model usage (p. 2). A systematic review it cites concludes the benefits outweigh the costs in the short, medium, and long terms (p. 2). The MOF is positioned as the response to the measurement gap left by this debate:

There is not yet a formally agreed-upon definition of “open source AI.” (Section 2.3, p. 3 of the PDF)

2. Related concepts the MOF builds on and pushes back against

Section 3 walks through the vocabulary of the “open knowledge” movement — open science, open access, open collaboration and community, open source software (OSS), source-available, open data, and open licenses — and Section 2.2 contrasts the MOF with adjacent concepts. Only the paper’s own definitions are reproduced here.

Openness versus completeness is the paper’s key conceptual move. “Openness” has become a vague umbrella term covering licensing, availability, and thoroughness at once; the paper splits it: completeness measures which artifacts are released, openness measures under what license. Section 3.2:

…we use the term “completeness” to measure the availability of components that are released with models (with the goal of full completeness) and the term “openness” to describe the usage of permissive licenses for components. (Section 3.2, p. 4 of the PDF)

Open source software (OSS) (Section 3.7): software published under licenses allowing inspection, modification, and redistribution; OSI-approved licenses such as MIT, Apache 2.0, and 3-Clause BSD are the standard. The paper notes OSS is estimated to be used in 96% of global codebases and to constitute up to 90% of software stacks (p. 5), and it treats open-source AI as building on this tradition while outgrowing it.

Source-available (Section 3.8, p. 6) “should not be confused with open source”: the code or model is accessible, but the license imposes restrictions consumers must understand before use. “Most open-washed projects are examples of source available due to their restrictive licensing.”

Open data and open content (Section 3.9–3.10): for datasets the appropriate instruments are open-data and open-content licenses — CC0, CC BY, CC BY-SA, the Linux Foundation’s Community Data License Agreement (CDLA-Permissive), and Open Data Commons licenses (PDDL, ODC-By). For papers and documentation, Creative Commons licenses (CC BY, CC BY-SA) are the norm. Crucially, “alternative licenses, such as the Llama 2 license, OpenRAIL, and AI2 ImpACT licenses, are not considered open-source licenses due to their restrictions on usage” (p. 6).

Open weights (Section 2.2): models whose “weights are made publicly-available for download and downstream use” are “falsely promoted as ‘open-source’… Such models may more accurately be described as ‘open-weight models’” (p. 2). Downloadability of parameters is thus the minimum, not the test, of openness.

Adjacent transparency instruments the MOF absorbs (Sections 1–2.3): reproducibility checklists (the NeurIPS 2019 reproducibility program of Pineau et al.; the AAAI Reproducibility Checklist), model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019 — “model cards for model reporting”), data cards following the datasheets-for-datasets tradition (Gebru et al., 2021) and the Data Nutrition Project, ethics guidelines and review boards, and government efforts the paper names: NIST and NTIA in the USA and the AI Safety Institute in the UK, plus the OSI’s then-draft Open Source AI Definition. Its complaint against all prior instruments is that none “evaluate both the completeness and openness of models” (p. 3).

It also explicitly distances itself from a rival classification: the gradient approach to model openness of Solaiman (2023), which treats openness as a continuum and labels BLOOM and GPT-J as open. The MOF instead says:

We would classify GPT-J as open because it was released under the OSI-approved Apache 2.0 license, while BLOOM is source-available due to its restrictive, non-OSI-approved OpenRAIL license. (Section 2.3, p. 3 of the PDF)

3. The framework: three classes of completeness

The MOF (Section 4) is a “three-tier ranked classification system” over the artifacts of a model’s development lifecycle. Each class builds on the previous one: “Class III being the least complete and Class I being the most complete,” and “the higher the class indicates a more complete distribution that promotes more transparency and enables reproducibility, auditing, and downstream use” (Section 4.1, p. 7). Qualifying for a class requires every listed component of that class, each under an acceptable open license (“Each component must be released using an appropriate open license from Table 2 to qualify the entire project at the specified class level,” p. 7).

On hybrid releases (Section 4.3), the MOF deliberately borrows open source’s binary license logic, applied rung by rung. “When any component is not released using an open license as described in Table 1, that component is not deemed open and does not qualify for an MOF class” (p. 8). A producer may drop a component and claim a lesser class if everything remaining is open — but projects using modified open licenses with added restrictions or acceptable-use clauses cannot qualify even for Class III. The authors warn: “The reader should not confuse the classification system as being a gradient measure of openness” (a dig at Solaiman’s gradient) “but rather a measurement of the completeness of a release in adherence with open science principles” (Section 4.3, p. 8).

4. The 17 components of the model development lifecycle

Section 5 defines the components one by one, each tagged with its class membership (e.g., “Model Architecture (III.1)”), and the definitions use the RFC 2119 keywords (MUST, SHOULD, MAY, OPTIONAL). The paper counts 17 components (“The MOF has 17 components to fulfil completeness of model artifacts, which cover the code, data, and documentation that are part of the model development lifecycle,” p. 7), and adds an eighteenth element, the MOF configuration file, which “MUST be included in any distribution” and records which components the release contains and under which licenses (p. 12). Components may be combined in a single artifact — e.g., evaluation results may live inside the technical report or model card — “as long as the combined release is covered by the appropriate LICENSE file” (my paraphrase of Section 5’s combining rule, p. 8). Counting caveat (inference): the paper’s own Table 2 and the MOT interface instead speak of “16 components,” because they merge the two parameter components (final and intermediate checkpoints) into one row and treat the configuration file separately; the text’s “17” counts final and intermediate parameters as two components. The mismatch is the paper’s, not an error of this note.

Read by lifecycle layer, the components are:

  • Model layer — Model Architecture (III.1): the algorithms, network layout, connectivity, activations; MUST be distributed as open source code under an OSI-approved license and SHOULD be documented in the paper/report/model card. Model Parameters — Final Checkpoints (III.2) and — Intermediate Checkpoints (I.5): trained parameters, optimizer state, in a common framework format (TensorFlow, Keras, PyTorch, ONNX); final parameters MUST be open, and for Class I the intermediate checkpoints and log files MUST also be released.
  • Data layer — Datasets (I.3): “data is the lifeblood of ML models and is the most often held back element in the release of a model” (p. 11); covers pretraining, fine-tuning, alignment data, validation/testing/benchmark data, tokenized datasets and metadata. Data Preprocessing Code (I.4): cleaning, formatting, feature engineering, augmentation, tokenization, RLHF transforms; MUST be OSI-approved. Data Card (III.6): summary statistics and composition following the Data Nutrition Project; MUST be open (CC BY-4.0 preferred).
  • Tooling / code layer — Training, Validation, and Testing Code (II.2), including fine-tuning, reinforcement-learning and adapter code; Inference Code (II.3), which MUST be enough to replicate the model’s benchmark results; Evaluation Code (II.4); Evaluation Data (II.5); Supporting Libraries & Tools (II.6) — an enumerated list covering tokenizers, hyperparameter-search code, compute infrastructure, monitoring code, containerization files, frontends, deployment orchestration, SDKs and interactive demos. Code components MUST use OSI-approved licenses; evaluation data uses open-data/open-content licenses.
  • Documentation layer — Technical Report (III.3) or Research Paper (I.2), both under CC BY-4.0/CC0 and linked from the model card; Evaluation Results (III.4) reporting benchmark names and versions (or full custom benchmarks); Model Card (III.5) per Mitchell et al.’s reporting standard; Sample Model Outputs (III.7, optional), shared without copyright restriction where legally permitted; Model Metadata (I.6, optional) under an open data license.

The single most consequential licensing argument of the paper sits in §5.2. Because weights are data, not code, slapping an OSS license on them is a legal mismatch:

Since model parameters are in fact data, model parameters SHOULD be distributed under an open data license, like CDLA-Permissive-2.0. (Section 5.2, p. 9 of the PDF)

The paragraph warns of an “untested legal gray area”: most permissive software licenses do not refer to data directly and do not clearly grant rights to modify and redistribute parameters, which “could result in a legal obligation to any model consumer if the model producer were to implement royalties after widespread adoption of their model” (p. 9).

5. Acceptable licenses (Section 6)

Table 2 of the paper (“Model Openness Framework Components and Licenses,” p. 13 of the PDF) is the compliance table of the whole system: for every component it fixes the domain (Data, Model, or both), the content type (data, code, or documentation), and the accepted open licenses. The logic, which the paper stresses in Section 3.10 and Section 6, is content-appropriate licensing: code → OSI-approved software licenses; data → open-data/open-content licenses (CDLA-Permissive-2.0, CC0, CC BY); documentation → Creative Commons licenses (CC BY-4.0 preferred). Datasets are the only component where even unlicensed release is acceptable (“any including unlicensed”), because Class I demands the data be present regardless of its license (see the Table 3 entries above for per-component detail). Licenses named as not open for MOF purposes: the Llama 2 license, OpenRAIL, and the AI2 ImpACT license (p. 6). MOF.JSON license fields must use valid SPDX identifiers (p. 14).

6. Applying the MOF: process, files, tool, badging, scoring

The seven-step process (Section 7.1, pp. 12–13). Adoption is not prescriptive about how to train — it is an evaluation procedure: (1) inventory all artifacts (data, code, documentation) with names, locations, versions, licenses; (2) map inventory items onto the MOF components (the paper says “the 16 components defined in Section 5” here — the counting inconsistency flagged above); (3) verify licenses against Table 2 — “if licenses are incompatible, the model cannot be classified”; (4) determine completeness — classify at the highest tier whose required components all carry open licenses, with Class III as the minimum; (5) generate MOF.JSON; (6) self-assert the class; (7) badging and validation, with the community filing disputes over mislabeled models.

Distribution files (Section 7.2–7.3). Every distribution needs a LICENSE file (either all applicable licenses in one file, with headings, or per-component LICENSE files in each component’s base directory) and an MOF.JSON file: a root “MOF” object with three required nested objects — Framework (name, version, date), Release (name, version, date, type, architecture, treatment, origin, producer, contact, mof_class), and Components (per component: description, location, license as SPDX identifier(s), license_path). A component that is released under a non-open or custom license “should not be included in the MOF.JSON file” — the file references only openly licensed components (p. 13).

Class assignment and the Model Openness Tool (Sections 7.4–7.5). Classification “relies on self-reporting and projects are not classified by a central authority” (p. 15). The MOT — the reference implementation at isitopen.ai — offers three functions: view registered models in a catalogue (each with a component-wise breakdown into “released with valid licenses / released with invalid licenses / unreleased,” plus a copyable badge), evaluate your own model (license dropdowns for each MOF component, empty fields counted as unreleased), and submit a model to the catalogue. For every model the MOT “assesses each criterion from the MOF and generates an openness score based on the degree to which each criterion is fulfilled” and returns a classification of 1–3 (p. 15). Scoring guidance in one line (inference, but directly supported): the class integer is not a continuous score of goodness — it is the highest tier whose full component set clears the open-license bar, so the MOT’s “score” is a completeness-class result plus a per-criterion openness tally.

Badging and disputes (Sections 7.6–7.7). Modeled on “the OpenSSF Best Practices Badge Program” (p. 16), the Generative AI Commons badge lets producers display their class, get license recommendations, generate MOF.JSON, and track their standing on the MOF scoreboard; consumers can validate classes and submit disputes. The authors are candid that everything rests on honesty: “Misrepresentations will only harm the reputation of the model producer” (p. 16).

8. Benefits and limitations as the paper states them

Benefits claimed (Section 8, pp. 16–17): clarity (what is included, under which license), a push toward more complete and open releases, reproducibility, transparency and explainability, data provenance (tracing bias and PII leakage), accountability and fairness via public auditing, continuous improvement, collaboration, education, and regulation (openness making models “more amenable to oversight and governance”).

Known limitations acknowledged (Section 9.1, p. 17): the framework is designed for deep-learning artifacts and “does not translate entirely to all aspects of reinforcement learning”; it depends on producer honesty; it must persuade producers reluctant to share without restrictions; openness must be balanced against privacy, IP, institutional policy, and commercialization; classification ignores model functionality, and bias, safety, and other harms remain concerns (though open artifacts enable external audits); a simple rubric may miss nuances; and the framework does not address the use of copyrighted material in training data, which the authors say is being handled through courts and legislation. Out of scope (Section 9.2, p. 17): AI safety (bias, fairness, trustworthiness), performance testing, red-teaming, security and privacy, model-serving components, and model provenance.

9. Position in the literature

Within the paper’s own citation network. The MOF is a standards-and-governance intervention as much as a research artifact. Its most direct intellectual debts and targets are: the open source licensing literature and institutions — the Open Source Initiative’s Open Source Definition and OSI-approved license list, the draft Open Source AI Definition, and the argument (Lerner & Tirole; von Krogh et al.; the paper’s refs. 55–59, 76) that license choice shapes contribution and adoption; the documentation-of-AI literature — Mitchell et al.’s (2019) model cards and Gebru et al.’s (2021) datasheets-for-datasets, which the MOF turns from recommendations into class-gated requirements; the reproducibility movement — the NeurIPS 2019 reproducibility program (Pineau et al.) and the AAAI Reproducibility Checklist; the “openwashing” critique — Widder, West & Whittaker (2023) on the political economy of open AI, Liesenfeld et al. and Liesenfeld & Dingemanse on tracking openness in instruction-tuned models, Maffulli’s OSI blog post on the Llama 2 license, and Nolan’s IEEE Spectrum piece; the gradient approach of Solaiman (2023), from which it explicitly differentiates itself; the open model movement it wants to codify — EleutherAI, BigScience/BLOOM, BigCode; and policy bodies it engages — NIST and NTIA (US), the UK AI Safety Institute, the EU AI Act debates, and the OSI process (two OSI staff are thanked as reviewers). Empirical anchors it cites include Osborne, Ding & Kirk’s Hugging Face Hub analysis and the EleutherAI “science in the open” program.

Within this topic’s library. The MOF sits upstream of the topic readings as the shared measurement language for “how open is that model, really?”:

  • Azoulay, Krieger & Nagaraj (2024), “Old Moats for New Models: Openness, Control, and Competition in Generative AI” (NBER): asks whether the “open-source AI ecosystem’s green shoots” devalue proprietary moats. The MOF’s distinction between downloadable weights and fully open, license-clean releases is precisely the granularity needed before that question can be answered empirically.
  • Yue & Nagle (2024), “Igniting Innovation” (PyTorch / open collaboration): their analytic split between access rights (opening use of the technology) and control rights (retaining governance over its direction) is a sibling vocabulary to the MOF’s openness/completeness split — openness is about the rights granted, completeness about what is handed over (interpretive link; the paper does not cite the MOF).
  • Longpre et al. (2025), “Economies of Open Intelligence”: distinguishes “open weight” releases from “truly open source models” and tracks the transparency of training-data disclosure — the empirical analogue of the MOF’s completeness ladder applied to the Hugging Face ecosystem.
  • Fleischmann & Haeussler (2026), “Degrees of Openness in Open-Source AI”: cites White et al. The widespread adoption of the MOF promises to establish completeness and openness as core tenets of responsible AI, ultimately promoting a more transparent and trustworthy advancement of AI R&D. (White et al., 2024, Conclusion, p. 18 of the PDF)

10. What to keep

The MOF’s contribution is not a new finding about models — it is a measurement convention, and measurement conventions are what let the empirical papers of this topic count anything at all. Three ideas travel furthest: (1) licensing is content-specific — code needs OSI-approved software licenses, parameters are data needing open-data licenses, documentation needs Creative Commons; (2) openness and completeness are separate axes — a release can be perfectly open and thin (Class III) or rich but closed (not open at all); (3) the class ladder is cumulative and license-absolute — higher classes demand more components, but at any level a single restricted component disqualifies the claim. When a Hugging Face model card, a paper, or a policy document says “open,” the MOF-trained reader should ask: open weights, open code, open data, or open science — and under which licenses?
