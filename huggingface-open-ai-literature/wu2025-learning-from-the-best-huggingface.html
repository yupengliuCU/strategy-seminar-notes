One-sentence summary

This registered report pre-commits to a protocol that will ask which observable features of a Hugging Face model repository — organized into four complementary dimensions (Documentation, Models, Platforms, Techniques, 32 proposed features) — distinguish highly popular models (top 10% by likes or downloads) from the long tail, using non-parametric significance tests, classifier-based popularity prediction, permutation-importance feature ranking, and cross-domain / cross-affiliation generalization checks, so that practitioners receive evidence-based guidelines for raising their models' visibility.

Reading guide: This note belongs to the topic "Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy". Read it as a methodological artifact rather than as a results paper: it shows how a software-engineering research community plans to measure "success" inside an open model hub. The parts that matter for the topic are ① the two popularity indicators (likes, downloads) and the claim that platform search ranks by them — popularity literally buys visibility, an attention-allocation mechanism with feedback effects; ② the four-dimension feature framework, which is a catalogue of the observable "publishing practices" (documentation hygiene, SafeTensors, Spaces demos, pipeline tags, framework support) that platform theory would call complements or signals; and ③ the pre-registration design itself, which contrasts with the retrospective, hypothesis-after-the-fact mining typical of platform data studies. Keep in mind throughout: the abstract's "Method" is a plan, and every "we will" in the text is a promise the authors have not yet executed in this copy.

1. Background and motivation: popularity as visibility on the Hub

Pre-trained models (PTMs) have become the shared artefacts of machine learning work across NLP, computer vision, speech, and code generation, and Hugging Face has become a central open-source platform for sharing and reusing them. The authors open from the practitioner's dilemma: new models are continuously uploaded into a growing and diverse ecosystem, and a sharing-extension-sharing cycle raises a practical requirement — how do model developers make their models popular enough that users see them?

Popularity matters, in the authors' framing, because of a visibility mechanism that is specific to platforms like Hugging Face. Models with high popularity tend to appear frequently in search results and platform interfaces; Hugging Face itself offers "rank by number of likes (descendingly)" and "rank by number of downloads (descendingly)" options for searching models, so that models with more likes or downloads are more visible, more likely to be reused and extended, and more attractive as benchmark subjects for empirical machine-learning and software-engineering studies (p. 1 of the PDF). The gap they claim to fill is empirical: prior Hugging Face research has examined model reuse, usability, and documentation quality, but "has not discovered what makes a model more popular in this ecosystem" (p. 1–2 of the PDF).

2. What kind of paper this is: a registered report, and what that implies

Before summarizing the design, one textual fact needs to be stated plainly: the text read for this note is a registered-report protocol, not a results paper. The abstract is written in the registered-report format (Background / Aim / Method), the method sections describe planned rather than executed work, and the document ends with "Threats to Validity" and references — there is no results, evaluation, or discussion section. A reader's handwritten margin note in the annotated copy at the end of the text ("我去 没结果", roughly "ugh, no results") confirms that whoever annotated the copy expected results and found none. A registered report is a publishing format in which the introduction and protocol are peer-reviewed and accepted in principle before data collection (stage 1), and the eventual outcomes are published afterwards regardless of whether the hypotheses hold — the design is meant to blunt hindsight bias, p-hacking, and selective reporting. The abstract's Method paragraph is the most compact statement of the whole protocol:

Method: We plan to extract features from models’ metadata across four complementary dimensions: Documentation, Models, Platforms, and Techniques. Based on these features, we propose to build prediction models to identify the most important features and assess their effectiveness in popularity prediction.
(p. 1 of the PDF)

The intended contributions are likewise announced as expected rather than achieved: "Empirical Insights" (how features relate to downloads and likes), "Practical Recommendations" (guidelines for raising visibility), and a "Research Foundation" for future work on AI model development and adoption (p. 2 of the PDF).

3. Core constructs: two popularity indicators, analysed separately

The protocol measures popularity with the two indicators the platform itself exposes, and deliberately analyses them as distinct outcomes rather than combining them into one score. Downloads are treated as a sign of recent usage and community engagement, likes as closer to GitHub stars — community endorsement from registered users:

To measure the popularity of models on Hugging Face, we consider two indicators provided by the platform: the number of likes and the number of downloads.
(p. 2 of the PDF)

The text adds that downloads indicate recent usage (the protocol describes downloads as usage "in the past 30 days"; p. 5 of the PDF), and the plan includes computing Spearman's rank correlation between the two metrics to check whether they capture different aspects of popularity — with 0.3 and 0.7 as the thresholds for calling a correlation weak, moderate, or strong. A weak-to-moderate correlation between the two would justify keeping both as separate outcomes; the authors note they are departing from earlier single-indicator studies in the GitHub literature.

4. Core framework: 32 proposed features in four dimensions

The central analytical move is to treat each Hugging Face model as a Git repository whose observable content — README text, file structure, metadata, platform configuration — can be converted into features. Following the GitHub-popularity literature (above all Fan et al.'s study of academic AI repositories), the authors initially propose a set of 32 features grouped into four dimensions (p. 2–3 of the PDF):

We extract 32 features across four dimensions, including Documentation, Models, Platforms, and Techniques. Our goal is to investigate which of these features are most correlated with model popularity, as measured by likes and downloads on Hugging Face.
(p. 3 of the PDF)

They stress that Hugging Face models, though hosted as Git repositories, plausibly have features with no analogue in general software repositories — model files, configuration, quantization, platform services — which motivates a feature set designed for model hubs. To the best of their knowledge this is "the first exploratory study that aims to systematically analyze how model features influence visibility and engagement" (p. 3 of the PDF).

4.1 Documentation dimension

Hugging Face automatically renders a documentation page (a "model card") when a repository contains a README.md, and the README consists of a Markdown description part plus a YAML metadata part. The plan analyses the Markdown part with the Mistune parser, reusing and extending Fan et al.'s features to obtain 14 documentation features (p. 3 of the PDF): character counts of the YAML and Markdown parts (length-yaml, length-doc); counts of lists and tables; counts of code blocks and inline code (clear run-instructions matter because unclear code instructions are a documented source of user issues in AI repositories); counts of static and animated images and the presence of video; counts of GitHub, Hugging Face, and arXiv links; and two binary flags for whether the metadata carries license information and a BibTeX entry.

4.2 Models dimension

The model-specific features concern the repository as a model artefact: whether a configuration file is present (has-config, needed for loading), the total size of the model files (model-size, relevant to deployment feasibility), the number of files in the root directory, the number of modules (subdirectories, a proxy for how the project is organized), the number of model-weight files (.bin / .safetensors), whether dataset information is specified and how many datasets are linked and whether they are hosted on Hugging Face (transparency and credibility signals), whether evaluation results are reported in the metadata, and whether the model is quantized — the text notes quantization "often reflects real-world efficiency or consumption concerns" (p. 4 of the PDF). The authors flag an intention to later expand the model dimension toward real usage signals (e.g., being loaded through downstream tools such as BertScore or RAG libraries).

4.3 Platforms dimension

The platform dimension captures whether developers use Hugging Face's own affordances. The most distinctive rationale concerns file format security. Loading weights from formats such as .pt or .bin relies on pickle, the default Python serialization library, "which poses the risk of malicious code injection and execution"; Hugging Face introduced SafeTensors as a secure alternative, and the authors argue security signalling can feed popularity:

Hugging Face introduces SafeTensors for storing and loading model weights securely. Users may consider models using SafeTensors to be more secure and trustworthy, which can enhance the model’s popularity.
(p. 4 of the PDF)

Two further platform features are interactive demonstration affordances: Spaces, which lets developers host, share, and run a model without configuring servers, so users can try it through the website ("which can enhance the model’s visibility and accessibility"; p. 4 of the PDF), and the inference Widget, which lets users run the model with default example inputs from the model's page (has-space, has-widgets).

4.4 Techniques dimension

The techniques dimension records implementation and framework choices. has-pipeline captures whether metadata specifies a named pipeline via the pipeline_tag field; pipelines are sets of APIs that simplify inference (e.g., text classification, named entity recognition), and models that declare one can be discovered more easily by users searching for a task. has-impl-lib captures the primary implementation library via the library_name field. has-supported-lib indicates whether the model uses a library Hugging Face officially supports (the text names transformers and diffusers), which makes the model loadable with minimal code. has-additional-fw captures whether the model supports additional frameworks (pytorch, tensorflow, jax) beyond the primary one, on the argument that multi-framework flexibility raises attractiveness. Feature extraction is to be validated by a manual sanity check on a statistically representative subset of models (p. 4–5 of the PDF).

5. Evidence and methods: data collection plan

The data plan (Section III of the paper) has three filters worth noting, because they define the population the future findings will speak about. First, the authors will call the official Hugging Face Hub APIs to take "a timely and comprehensive snapshot" of public model repositories. Second, they exclude models added within the 180 days before the collection date, on the argument that recently added models have not had time to accumulate downloads and likes — an exclusion "widely adopted in previous studies". Third, because repositories without model-weight files (e.g., .bin or .safetensors) are often small and outside the study scope, they will filter out repositories smaller than 300 MB (all p. 3 of the PDF).

A fourth element anticipates a specific bias: "highly reused base models" (e.g., BERT, CodeBERT) may have artificially inflated download counts because other models are built on them. The plan combines automated detection (the Hugging Face model-tree metadata that records which models are referenced as base models; a heuristic for names ending in "-base"; README self-descriptions) with manual review of every identified base model, followed by a robustness check that repeats the research questions with and without base models (p. 3 of the PDF).

6. Evidence and methods: analysis plan by research question

The analysis is organized around three research questions. For each, the outcome labels are the same: ranking models by likes and by downloads separately, then treating the top 10% as "popular" and the bottom 80% as "unpopular", dropping the middle 10% as a buffer zone to sharpen the contrast (p. 5 of the PDF).

RQ1 — are popular and unpopular models different? (p. 5 of the PDF). For continuous features the plan first checks normality (Shapiro-Wilk) and, anticipating non-normal data, applies the Mann-Whitney U test; binary and categorical features are tested with the chi-square test of independence. Significance is set at p < 0.05 with Holm-Bonferroni or false-discovery-rate correction for multiple comparisons, and effect sizes (Cliff's delta with 0.147 / 0.33 / 0.474 thresholds; Phi or Cramér's V for chi-square) are computed to gauge practical significance. The same comparison is run separately for likes and downloads.

RQ2 — which features matter most, and how well can they predict popularity? (p. 5–6 of the PDF). This is the prediction stage. The plan first refines the feature set: pairwise Spearman correlations identify collinear pairs, one member of each pair above a threshold (e.g., |ω| > 0.7) is dropped, and mutual information ranks the remaining features so the top-k set can be tuned by cross-validation. Then five classifiers are trained with grid-searched hyperparameters — decision trees, random forests, support vector machines, naive Bayes, and k-nearest neighbours — under 10-fold cross-validation or bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations, reporting mean AUC as the primary metric. The acceptability criterion is explicit:

A classifier’s performance will be considered acceptable if it significantly exceeds a random baseline or the mean AUC is larger than 0.7
(p. 6 of the PDF)

Finally, the best-performing classifier(s) are interpreted with permutation importance — shuffling each feature's values one at a time in a test set and measuring the drop in predictive performance — repeated over bootstrap iterations to obtain stable rankings (p. 6 of the PDF).

RQ3 — do the features generalize across domains and affiliations? (p. 6 of the PDF). The best classifier from RQ2 is transferred across Hugging Face subgroups. Domain labels come from each model's pipeline_tag (e.g., text-classification, image-to-text) mapped to six higher-level categories — Audio, Computer Vision, Multimodal, NLP, Tabular, and Reinforcement Learning — and affiliation labels are parsed from the model owner's Hugging Face profile page into eight categories: company, university, nonprofit, organization or individual, community, unknown, classroom, and government. Training on one domain or affiliation group and testing on another reveals whether popularity drivers are consistent or context-dependent.

7. Findings: none are reported in the text read

This version of the paper contains no findings. There is no results section, no reported dataset statistics, no classifier performance numbers, no feature-importance results, and no answer to any of the three research questions. Everything that looks like a finding in the sections above is a plan: the authors describe what they will test (differences between popular and unpopular models), what they will build (classifiers), and what they will interpret (permutation-importance rankings). The only empirical-sounding numbers in the text are design parameters (180 days, 300 MB, top 10% / bottom 80%, p < 0.05, AUC > 0.7, |ω| > 0.7) and prior results cited from other papers (e.g., Jones et al.'s finding that the top 1000 most popular models scored higher on a documentation-quality scale than the bottom 1000; Fan et al.'s finding that README image and link counts predict stars of academic AI repositories — both cited on p. 2 of the PDF). Those belong to the cited literature, not to this study.

If the registered-report process runs to completion, the future stage-2 paper is expected to report, per the protocol: which features differ significantly between popular and unpopular models (RQ1); which features are most important in the best classifiers and whether acceptable AUC (> 0.7) is reached (RQ2); and whether feature effectiveness transfers across domains and affiliations (RQ3). None of that is knowable from this copy, and nothing below should be read as a result of this study.

8. Position in the literature

Within its own field (mining software repositories), the paper positions itself at the junction of two lines. The first is OSS popularity research, almost all of it on GitHub: Borges et al.'s large-scale analysis showing documentation quality and maintenance activity drive star growth; Zhu et al.'s finding that standard folders (docs, tests, examples) correlate with forks; Wang et al.'s study of README features; and above all Fan et al.'s study of popular academic AI repositories, which extracted 21 features in three dimensions (documentation, code, reproducibility), applied random-forest classifiers, and found that links to other repositories and image counts in the README matter for stars. The second line is Hugging Face research on reuse, usability, and documentation: Jiang et al. on model reuse, versioning and adaptation and on naming practices; Pepe et al. and Liang et al. on documentation practices, transparency, and model cards (Liang et al. systematically analyse tens of thousands of model cards); and Jones et al.'s 0-to-5 documentation-quality scale whose top-1000 popular models outscore the bottom 1000. The registered report's claim to novelty is narrow but clear: those literatures study projects and documentation; none systematically studies which model-level features drive popularity on a model hub, and none pre-registers that question.

9. Limitations

Limitations the authors themselves state (Section VI, "Threats to Validity", p. 6 of the PDF). Internal validity: feature extraction and data filtering may introduce bias, some important features may be uncaptured, and the chosen tests and classifiers carry assumptions. External validity: the study is confined to Hugging Face and may not generalize to GitHub or domain-specific registries such as PyTorch Hub, and popularity dynamics may drift as community practices and interface algorithms change. Construct validity — the most candid passage concerns the outcome measures themselves:

Likes can be driven by interface exposure or social dynamics, and automated pipelines may inflate download counts.
(p. 6 of the PDF)

The authors add that permutation importance does not indicate directionality; that correlation/mutual-information filtering may overlook individual feature effects; that filtering on repository size may still leave low-quality or inactive repositories in the sample; and that the metrics may not reflect long-term impact or actual usefulness — they rely on using both indicators to triangulate.

Protocol-level limitations that follow from the text itself. Most fundamentally, there are no results in this version; the protocol's promises (e.g., "we plan to explore additional features in future iterations"; hyperparameters such as the top-k to be tuned; affiliation categories to be parsed from owner pages) are underspecified in ways that will be resolved only during execution, so the eventual stage-2 paper may differ from this design.

Independent judgment (note-taker's assessment, clearly labelled).

  • Author's claims vs evidence: the claims in this text are claims about a plan (that the feature set is comprehensive, that the tests and classifiers are appropriate). The empirical claims of the study — that certain features significantly differ, predict, or transfer — do not yet exist. No factual result from this paper should be cited anywhere until the stage-2 report appears.
  • Inference about the object of study: because Hugging Face ranks search results by likes and downloads, popularity is plausibly self-reinforcing — visibility buys attention, attention buys likes. If that feedback is strong, features that predict popularity may partly capture the platform's own allocation dynamics rather than intrinsic model quality. The authors acknowledge the interface-exposure mechanism in their construct-validity discussion but the design is a cross-sectional snapshot, so this remains an open interpretive issue for the future findings.
  • Inference about method: the practical-recommendations goal ("clear directions for improving model popularity") invites a causal reading, but the planned evidence is correlational and predictive. Directionality of any discovered feature-popularity association cannot be settled by permutation importance (the authors concede this).
  • Textual caveats: ① the source is a pdftotext conversion of a two-column annotated copy; marginal reader notes in Chinese are interleaved with the body text and were ignored for this note, but on some pages (notably p. 5) two text streams interleave, so page attributions and verbatim spans should be checked against the PDF; ② the prose states 32 features while the extracted Table I shows 31 named rows — the discrepancy is attributed to a row lost in extraction, not verified against the PDF; ③ page numbers here are counted by form-feed characters in the txt and are approximate at page boundaries; ④ the library catalogue entry lists arXiv:2310.01642 for this paper, but that identifier belongs to Jiang et al.'s "Naming practices of pre-trained models in Hugging Face" (2023), which is reference [18] inside this paper's bibliography — the identifier appears to be a cataloguing error, and the registered report itself carries no arXiv number in the text read (public listings associate it with the ICSME 2025 Registered Reports track).
  • Open questions: will documentation and signalling features that matter for today's PTMs survive the shift toward very large foundation models with different publishing practices? Do popularity drivers differ between genuinely high-quality models and merely well-packaged ones? How sensitive are the answers to the 180-day / 300 MB / base-model filters? Whether the 0.7 AUC bar is met — and whether likes and downloads turn out to be weakly correlated as the design expects — is exactly what the registration was built to reveal.

10. Conclusion: takeaways for the Open-AI Economy topic

Read as a protocol, the paper's value to this topic is threefold. First, it formalizes popularity as a measurable, two-dimensional outcome (likes, downloads) on the central hub of open AI, and it documents a concrete visibility mechanism — rank-by-likes/downloads search — through which popularity becomes an economic resource for model owners. Second, its four-dimension feature framework is a checklist of the publishing practices an open-model commons rewards or fails to reward: documentation completeness, secure weight formats, demo-ability (Spaces, widgets), task discoverability (pipeline tags), ecosystem compatibility (official libraries, multi-framework support). Those are hypotheses to be tested, but they are the right kind of hypotheses for platform strategy — they concern complements and signals that developers control. Third, the registered-report format itself is the methodological takeaway: in a literature full of ex-post mining of platform data, this design commits in advance to its tests and thresholds, which is precisely the discipline that empirical platform research needs if its "insights for practitioners" are to be trusted.

The expected contributions announced in the introduction — empirical insights into what drives model success on Hugging Face, actionable recommendations for visibility, and a research foundation for AI model adoption studies — will materialize only if the protocol is executed and the stage-2 results published. For the reading list, this page should therefore be treated as documentation of a study in progress, useful for its framework and its questions, and not as a source of empirical findings.
