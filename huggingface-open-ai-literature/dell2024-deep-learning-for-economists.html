One-sentence summary

Dell shows economists how to use deep learning as a measurement technology: pre-trained neural networks are adapted — by fine-tuning a classifier head, contrastively tuning embeddings, or prompting generative models — to convert massive unstructured corpora (scans, news text, satellite imagery) into the low-dimensional, structured variables that then enter causal or descriptive analyses, with sample-efficient open tools making the pipeline cheap enough for academic budgets.

Reading guide: This paper is the measurement-and-methods backbone of the "Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy" topic. Almost every empirical paper in this library takes for granted the workflow Dell codifies: download a pre-trained model from the Hugging Face hub, fine-tune it on a modest number of hand-labeled examples, and deploy it over millions of documents. Laufer et al. (2025) and Longpre et al. (2025) describe the hub's ecosystem that Dell treats as infrastructure; Wu, Lin & Li (2025) and Khoshbash (2026) estimate how releases such as Llama 2 shift what this fine-tuning activity looks like; Azoulay et al. (2024) study whether open weights erode the proprietary advantage of frontier builders — while Dell's review shows, from the researcher's side, why openness (reproducible open backbones versus black-box commercial APIs) is a methodological virtue, not just an ideological one. Read it as the economist's user manual for the models whose governance the rest of the topic studies.

1. Background and motivation: unstructured data as an economic measurement problem

The review opens with a division of labor between two literatures. Economists increasingly want to measure activity in settings where the raw evidence is text or images: the presence of economic activity in satellite images, the topics or entities mentioned in social media, the congressional record, or firm filings, or the content of millions of historical newspaper scans. The review's stated aim is narrow and disciplined — it focuses on imputing low-dimensional structured data from unstructured texts or images, in contexts where the ground truth is uncontroversial but extraction needs to be automated due to the massive scale of the problem. That is, deep learning is treated as a scaling technology for tasks humans could in principle do by hand, and the resulting structured variables then serve as an outcome, an endogenous variable, an instrument, or a control in a downstream analysis. Dell is explicit that the review is a successor to the "text as data" survey of Gentzkow, Kelly and Taddy (2019), updated for methods developed since that article, and equally explicit about what it deliberately leaves out: language-model tools that raise economists' own productivity (covered by Korinek, 2023), machine learning on structured data using shallower networks (Athey and Imbens, 2019), neural approximation of combinatorial optimization and DSGE solutions, and a small literature that puts deep networks directly inside causal frameworks (e.g., Lynn et al., 2020).

Three properties of deep learning justify the investment. First, transfer learning: networks pre-trained on colossal corpora already encode the basic structure of language and vision, so adapting them to a new task needs only a few hundred to a few thousand labeled examples rather than training from scratch. Second, contextualized representations: a word or pixel is mapped to a vector that depends on its neighbors, which is necessary to interpret meaning (the review's running example is why "I don't hate this article" cannot be scored by summing word-level sentiments, as bag-of-words methods must). Third, computational scale: continuous vectors can be compared with highly optimized tools — Silcock et al. (2023) perform 1014 exact vector similarity computations on a single mid-range GPU in about three hours — which lets the analysis run over millions or billions of observations. Against this stands the alternative Dell repeatedly benchmarks against: human-engineered features such as keywords, rules for detecting table structure, or hand-crafted string distances, which she argues are brittle in the face of noise (OCR errors, typos, historical printing) and complexity (many ways to say the same thing), and which do not transfer across datasets. Her verdict is that the deep learning revolution "has illustrated over and over—across many different tasks—that learning from empirical examples greatly outperforms human-engineered feature extraction in processing unstructured data."

For example, a researcher who needed to train a topic classifier could go to Hugging Face—the central hub for NLP (Section III)—and download a pretrained state-of-the-art language model made publicly available by entities such as Google, Meta, and Microsoft. The language model was trained on a colossal corpus to predict tokens (words or sub-words) randomly masked from text. From this training, it learned to produce meaningful contextualized vector representations of texts. The researcher could add a classifier layer to the pre-trained language model and fine-tune the resulting neural network on their classification task using a relatively modest amount of labeled data. (Dell, 2024, p. 7 of the PDF)

The passage states the review's core workflow in miniature — pre-training by Google, Meta, or Microsoft, distribution through a public hub, and local fine-tuning by a researcher with modest labels — and it is worth holding onto while reading the rest of this library, because the platform economics studied elsewhere (who controls the hub, whether weights stay open, how fine-tuning activity is distributed) is precisely the economics of this sentence.

2. Core concepts: architectures and how the network learns

The paper's own structure runs from an overview (Section I) through foundational architectures (Section II) to data (Section III), bias and uncertainty (Section IV), reproducibility (Section V), then applications (Sections VI–VIII) and a comparison of alternative methods (Section IX). The technical exposition is deliberately compressed — Dell's recurring "Legos" metaphor is that neural network components can be reconfigured and, crucially, swapped for newer ones as the literature advances, so the review emphasizes frameworks that survive model replacement rather than the details of any single architecture. Technical specifics are pushed to the EconDL companion. Only the pieces needed to understand the workflows appear in the article.

What a network is. A neural network is layers of interconnected neurons; each neuron's value is a non-linear function (an activation such as the rectified linear unit, ReLU) of weighted inputs from the previous layer, and the millions to billions of weights are estimated by minimizing a loss that compares predictions to ground truth, using backpropagation (the chain rule applied layer by layer) and gradient descent. Three families dominate. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) apply small learnable filters across an image, sharing weights across spatial locations; this gives translation invariance, hierarchical features (edges in early layers, structures later), and far fewer parameters than fully connected networks, at the price of fixed-size inputs. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), notably the bi-directional LSTM, process variable-length sequences by maintaining a hidden state, but suffer from a locality bias — information is lost as the state is passed along — and are now superseded for language. The transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) feeds all tokens in parallel and lets each token attend to every other token, solving the RNN's locality problem, and parallel training made much larger models feasible. Self-attention is quadratic, which is why context windows are limited — a typical open-source model window is 512 tokens, adequate when texts can be chunked.

Transformer language models come in two flavors that matter for workflows. Masked (encoder) models such as BERT and RoBERTa are bidirectional — each token's representation can see both sides of the text — and are trained by predicting masked tokens; they are the natural backbone when the goal is a representation of an entire text. Generative (decoder) models such as GPT and Llama predict the next token with causal attention and are the backbone of generative AI. The same encoder backbone can serve many tasks by attaching different heads (the "same pre-trained language model as the backbone" logic of Figure 3): a classifier head on the summary (cls) representation classifies whole sequences; heads on each token do token tagging (named entity recognition); two texts embedded jointly around a separator token let a head classify their relationship. In vision, the same transformer architecture works on image patches (vision transformers), though Dell's practical guidance is that a modernized lightweight CNN is often competitive and cheaper, and audio can even be handled by applying a vision transformer to spectrograms — a striking illustration of transfer across modalities, especially because performance was maximized by pre-training on ImageNet.

Two practical lessons close the architecture section and recur throughout. When performance is unexpectedly poor, "it is most often due to either a poorly chosen learning rate or incorrectly formatted input data." And researchers new to the field should train on cloud GPU services (the EconDL tutorials use Google Colab) rather than resolving local dependency stacks.

3. Training data, measurement discipline, and the economist's error budget

Dell's treatment of data (Section III) is where the review is most explicitly written for economists, because imputation errors feed downstream estimation. Learning can be supervised (labeled examples), self-supervised (labels gleaned automatically, as in masked-token pre-training), or unsupervised (no labels; discovering structure). Economists mostly need supervised extraction from unlabeled corpora, but three data practices deserve emphasis. First, continued pre-training: when the application domain shifts from the web corpus a model saw (18th-century legal texts, historical scans), one can first keep pre-training the language model on the target domain by predicting masked tokens, imparting domain vocabulary before any supervised tuning. Second, representative evaluation: labeled data must be split into training, validation (hyperparameters and prompts), and test sets, and the test set must be representative of the unlabeled population; with severe class imbalance — her example is a topic appearing once in every ten thousand web texts — random sampling to find positives is infeasible. Third, the labeling-quality trap: keyword-based sampling for annotation, the social-science default, "by construction, this fails to place positive sampling probability on all instances," so whole regions of the unlabeled data have no support in the labels; the resulting prediction bias can be systematically correlated with the error term of the downstream causal equation, since semantics and omitted variables both vary across space and time. Embedding models offer a metric for stratified sampling that avoids this, and congruence labeling — two annotators labeling the same points, with discrepancies resolved by hand — is the quality gate that made the review's own benchmarks usable.

Section IV concedes the limits of the whole enterprise: the review only claims competence for features "that humans are likely to agree on." Subjective judgments are dangerous — annotators' own political leanings affect political-sentiment labels, and the sentiment categories deep learning was built around (laptop, restaurant, movie reviews validated by stars) do not transfer to implicit sentiment in media or corporate reports. On uncertainty, Dell points economists to their comparative advantage: conformal inference gives prediction sets with marginal coverage guarantees, and "prediction powered inference" — debiasing model predictions using a high-quality auxiliary sample of ground-truth labels — is shown to be equivalent to the debiased machine learning of Chernozhukov et al. (2018). Her framing: causal inference and deep-learning imputation are both instances of imputing missing data.

4. Route A: classifiers versus generative AI

The applications part of the review opens with a decision rule that the mermaid diagram in Section 7 below reproduces: when are the classes enumerated ex ante, and how many are there? A classifier computes a score for each class from the last layer of a pre-trained model, so it can only be estimated when the classes are specified and seen in training. If classes are not specified ex ante, or are too numerous to estimate (record linkage, where each unique entity is a class), the researcher must work with embeddings directly. If classes are specified and modest in number, the choice is between a custom-trained classifier and generative AI — prompting a decoder model such as GPT to classify as text generation.

Generative AI for classification. Prompting a generative model is less straightforward than gradient-descent tuning, because the space of discrete prompts is infinite; the review distills the useful rules: tune prompts on a validation set, never the test set; simple prompts beat lengthy ones, and complex tasks should be broken into steps (chain-of-thought); prefix prompts ("What's the sentiment of this review?") suit autoregressive models better than cloze prompts. The paper's own experiments, run on historical newspaper articles as of June 2024 with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o, found GPT-4 and GPT-4o similar and edging out Turbo, with no systematic gains from newer releases — and two Anthropic models (Claude Haiku and Opus) performed 10–40 F1 points worse, largely because Claude's "Constitutional AI" refused to output classifications for some historical content and did not always return the requested Yes/No format. The advantages of generative AI are low start-up cost and zero-shot use; the disadvantages are fine-grained control, interpretability (a classifier's errors can be diagnosed and fixed by adding training data, whereas GenAI "can feel more like a black box"), reproducibility (a deprecated commercial API ends reproducibility, unless an open model such as Llama is used, which forfeits the ease-of-use advantage), and deployment cost over millions of texts.

Sequence classification: the benchmark evidence. To illustrate, the review trains 19 binary topic classifiers on massive-scale databases of historical news (Dell et al., 2023; Silcock et al., 2024), comparing them against generative AI. Binary classifiers were used because annotators could not hold 19 topic definitions in mind, and prompts for generative AI also favored binary classes. Labels came from congruence-labeled annotation, which surfaced definitional disputes — for crime, annotators disagreed about Watergate, and a zero-shot model produced a spurious spike in crime coverage in 1974 that the tuned classifier quickly learned to avoid with a modest number of labels. The classifiers used a consistent recipe — a DistilRoBERTa (82M parameters) or RoBERTa large (335M) backbone, a learning rate of 1e-6 or 1e-5, batch size eight. In most cases—across a diversity of topics—the tuned classifier tends to outperform or equal the performance of GPT, though for the more straightforward tasks, GPT’s performance can be very good, particularly in the case of GPT-4o (GPT-4 performed similarly). (Dell, 2024, p. 22 of the PDF)

The pattern behind the numbers: generative AI performs best on straightforward topics heavily represented in its pre-training (horoscopes, obituaries, polio vaccine are "nearly perfect"), and deteriorates as the domain shifts away from modern web texts — politics, drawn from the late 19th and early 20th centuries and including local and national coverage, is hard, and World War I scores in the low 70s for both GPT models while the Vietnam War does much better, presumably because it has greater representation in the training corpus. "Yet with minimal labels, the RoBERTa classifiers can adjust to this domain shift." Costs point the same direction: training such a classifier took minutes on a $9.99/month Google Colab plan at the time of writing, and deployment over millions of articles takes hours on cloud CPUs or a mid-range GPU, while GPT on a large corpus was "likely to be well beyond the budget of most social science researchers." One hybrid stands out: training a classifier on labels predicted by GPT works when GPT's labels are very high quality, but "where GPT performs less well, training on noisy data can magnify errors" — the paper's own example is the bible topic, where a classifier trained on GPT-3.5 labels falls to an F1 of 0.10 against 0.85–0.87 for the human-labeled classifiers. The same benchmarking logic extends to token classification (named entity recognition with BIO labeling over 27 million entities in historical newswire) and to paired-text problems, where the trade-off is architectural: a cross-encoder lets terms in two texts attend to each other (higher accuracy) but costs M×N forward passes, whereas a bi-encoder embeds each text once (M+N) — so the standard pattern is bi-encoder retrieval followed by cross-encoder re-ranking of the top candidates.

5. Route B: embedding models, record linkage, and data exploration

Embedding models become necessary when classes are not known ex ante, when new classes must be added without retraining, or when the number of classes makes the classifier's softmax intractable — "vector similarity calculations are highly optimized, allowing for problems at the scale of millions or even billions of classes." The catch is that raw pre-trained transformer spaces are not metric-friendly: they are anisotropic, low-frequency words are pushed to the edges of the hypersphere, and raw distances correlate with lexical rather than semantic similarity. Contrastive learning fixes this by training a bi-encoder so that semantically similar inputs map close together and dissimilar inputs far apart (losses include contrastive, cosine, triplet, InfoNCE, and supervised contrastive variants). Negatives matter: "easy" negatives teach little; "hard" negatives can be mined from prior knowledge or pre-trained similarity (Wikipedia disambiguation pages supply confusable names); but mining hard negatives from unlabeled data risks selecting positives, and random negatives need large batch sizes that strain academic budgets. Off-the-shelf embedding models exist (Sentence-BERT, affordable OpenAI sentence embeddings), and the review's empirical message is that fine-tuning a lightweight model on target data beats zero-shot embeddings from larger models: in the comparative-agendas example, tuning accentuates the topic dimension so that within-topic similarities separate starkly from across-topic similarities, and the tuned model still generalizes to UK Acts of Parliament despite domain shift.

Record linkage. Traditional linkage by string distance (Levenshtein edit distance, Jaccard n-gram similarity) remains dominant in social science despite a growing transformer literature, because "ABC Corporation," "ABC Co.," and "ABCC" are semantically near but string-distant. The review's own contribution is LinkTransformer (Arora and Dell, 2024), an open-source package that encodes records with a transformer language model and finds nearest neighbors with the FAISS similarity-search backend, supporting 1-1, 1-many, and many-many merges and no-match thresholds, multiple noisy fields serialized with a separator token, and any Hugging Face backbone. Across 20 diverse linking tasks, "models custom trained on a modest number of labels tend to perform best, followed by off-the-shelf embeddings from OpenAI, and then off-the-shelf Sentence Transformer models." Applications include 1940 Mexican tariff schedules, 1950 Japanese firm-level records linked on noisy fields (name, location, products, shareholders, banks), and modern firms and products across six languages — "A multilingual model can link products across languages without the need for translation." Vision enters too: OCR-free linkage on image crops of firm names works well (even across horizontal and vertical Japanese scripts), and a multimodal model built by fine-tuning a Japanese CLIP — contrastively pre-trained on 400 million image-caption pairs — produces far more accurate supply-chain networks than string distances, which "would likely lead to biased downstream economic analyses." Even where string methods stay preferable, deep learning can extend them cheaply: Yang et al. (2023) learn character-similarity ("homoglyph") spaces from augmented fonts and feed cosine distances into Levenshtein edit distance, reviving a lineage of sound- and shape-based matching that goes back to the 1918 Soundex patent.

Linking unstructured text to knowledge bases. Entity mentions in raw text can be disambiguated to Wikipedia and coreferenced across documents with a contrastively trained bi-encoder (Arora et al., 2024), trained on over 190 million entity pairs from Wikipedia, with hard negatives mined from disambiguation pages and family members from Wikidata, plus random negatives to avoid catastrophic forgetting. Disambiguating 100 years of newswire (1878–1977) yields stylized facts that validate the method: entity mentions correlate strongly with Wikipedia Qrank, the most-mentioned entity is Dwight Eisenhower (edging out Hitler, Nixon, and Truman), and only 4.7% of disambiguated entities are women — a stark, measurable fact about whose actions historical media recorded.

Exploration with unknown categories. When the researcher cannot enumerate classes, embeddings plus clustering substitute for classification. Reproduced-content detection — finding that "by the 1910s and 1920s, most of the articles that Americans read in their local papers had either been bought or sold on the national news market" (quoting media historian Julia Guarneri) — is solved by contrastively training a bi-encoder so that noisy, abridged copies of the same wire article map together, then clustering with single-linkage plus community detection; neural methods beat n-gram and hashing baselines "by a wide margin," and a lightweight CNN trained largely on synthetically augmented images handles reproduced images analogously. In Dell et al. (2023), the same machinery asks which were the biggest news stories of each year from 1885–1920 without specifying them ex ante: strikes and labor conflict are the single biggest story in eight of the thirty-six years (from the 1886 Southwest Railroad Strike and the 1892 Homestead Steel Strike through anthracite coal strikes in 1902 and rail strikes in 1910 and 1920), alongside wars, tariff politics, and the Treaty of Versailles — evidence that motivates studying labor movements before any classifier is built. The News Déjà Vu tool (Franklin et al., 2024) extends this to semantic search over history with named entities masked out.

6. OCR, layout, and regression: digitization as the enabling application

Document digitization gets unusually serious treatment, because it is the front door to most historical and administrative data. Off-the-shelf OCR fails away from high-resource commercial settings: on printed Japanese documents from the 1950s, "the best-performing existing OCR mis-predicts over half of the characters," and even in English, an OCR error in a number (hallucinating a leading "1") can severely bias quantitative analysis, whereas the same error in prose is often inconsequential. Scale compounds the problem — the U.S. National Archives holds approximately 13.28 billion pages of textual records. The review's answer is EffOCR (Carlson, Bryan and Dell, 2024), which reframes OCR as image retrieval rather than sequence-to-sequence translation: object detection localizes characters or words, a vision model contrastively trained on augmented digital fonts maps crops of the same character together, and transcription is nearest-neighbor retrieval against a digital-font index. New characters can be added after training — essential for the idiosyncratic symbols of historical documents — and inference parallelizes across characters.

EffOCR runs approximately 50 times faster than TrOCR, the only open-source model comparable in accuracy once fully trained. We created the opensource American Stories dataset consisting of over 430 million historical news articles with a $60,000 budget using EffOCR, and could not have realistically generated any substantial additional funds, let alone 50 times more funds. (Dell, 2024, p. 40 of the PDF)

The architectural comparison makes the budget point concrete: TrOCR, the seq2seq alternative, was trained on 684 million synthetic English text lines using 32 32GB V100 GPUs — "a very costly setup that no academic researcher can replicate" — and sample-efficiency curves show EffOCR reaching roughly 4% character error rate on only 5% of its training data (99 labeled Japanese table cells and 21 labeled U.S. newspaper rows, plus digital fonts), where seq2seq architectures trained on identical data "remain unusable." The same logic recurs for entity disambiguation: masked-language-model approaches (LUKE) are restricted to the top 50K Wikipedia entries and cannot mark out-of-knowledge-base entities, and the seq2seq translator (GENRE) runs roughly 60 times slower than a bi-encoder — "a sequence-to-sequence architecture is costly to run at scale and doesn't necessarily offer performance advantages." Regression, the review's shortest application section, appears mainly as object detection: predicting the four coordinates of a bounding box plus a class, used to localize document layout objects (articles, headlines, table headers, cells) so that scans become structured text; LayoutParser is the open-source package that lowers the barrier, while Meta's Segment Anything was, at the time of writing, not yet accurate or classificatory enough for document tasks. Dell's closing methodological argument (Section IX) generalizes all of these cases: for academic constraints — diverse applications, tiny annotation budgets, severely limited compute — sample-efficient bi-encoder embedding models beat seq2seq alternatives, and sample efficiency is a research design choice, not just an engineering convenience.

7. The workflow Dell recommends, and when deep learning is worth it

Figure 1 of the paper is a decision tree for approaching classification, and it doubles as the review's advice for when deep learning pays. The first question is whether the classes are enumerated ex ante; if they are not, or there are too many of them, work directly with embeddings. If classes are specified and modest in number, ask whether the application diverges from the pre-training corpus — historical data, document scans, and specialized settings imply domain shift, in which case a customized classifier tuned on labeled data will be needed; for straightforward tasks close to the corpus, off-the-shelf generative AI may be enough. The review's own rule is empirical: "If in doubt, a researcher can try an off-the-shelf method and switch to a customized classifier if performance is unsatisfactory."

Cost discipline runs through the entire review. The pre-training side can cost millions of dollars — "Training a deep neural model from scratch can require up to millions of dollars in compute, but fortunately, this is rarely necessary" — so the economist's budget is spent on the fine-tuning side, where a few hundred to a few thousand labels and commodity cloud compute (minutes of training on a $9.99/month Colab plan, hours of CPU deployment over millions of articles) dominate. Generative AI is the flexible, zero-shot option when the corpus is small enough to stay in budget; when it is not, or when reproducibility or fine-grained control matters, a custom classifier is the answer. Dell is equally clear about when the deep learning toolkit is the wrong instrument: for productivity uses of language models (Korinek's territory), for subjective constructs on which humans disagree, for tasks where exact term overlap is genuinely informative (sparse methods remain useful there), and — implicitly, throughout — whenever the domain shift and label costs of a tuned model cannot be justified against a strong off-the-shelf baseline.

8. Findings and evidence: what the review establishes

This is a survey whose evidence is threefold. First, its own controlled comparisons: the 19-topic historical-news classification exercise (Table 2 of the paper), the LinkTransformer evaluation across 20 linking tasks and six languages, the EffOCR sample-efficiency and cost comparisons against TrOCR, and the entity-disambiguation accuracy comparisons against LUKE and GENRE. Second, validity checks built by applying the tools at scale to real historical corpora — the 2.7-million-article newswire database (1878–1977) with topic tags, entity tags, Wikipedia disambiguation, and county coverage; the 430-million-article American Stories dataset; the 100-year entity-mention series whose pattern tracks Wikipedia Qrank and whose gender composition (4.7% women) is itself a finding. Third, the assembled external literature on architectures, losses, and benchmarks. The paper's overall empirical claim is stated flatly in the conclusion:

On social science tasks ranging from text classification to record linkage, entity disambiguation, and tracing the spread of reproduced content, deep learning can outperform traditional sparse methods by a wide margin (often by 20 points of F1/accuracy or more). (Dell, 2024, p. 41 of the PDF)

Two secondary findings deserve emphasis because they carry the review's strategy for academic adoption. Sample and compute efficiency are substitutes for scale: architectures that learn from tens to hundreds of examples and run on a student cloud account change which research questions are answerable at all, and they are what let open-source tools reach low-resource languages and settings. And the open-science foundation is not incidental to the method — it is constitutive:

Deep learning has been built to a remarkable degree upon open science and open data, although recent years have seen a pronounced shift towards proprietary models and data as the commercial potential of the technology has become increasingly clear. Nevertheless, the amount of open resources is staggering, and deep learning would not have made the strides it has without widespread sharing of models and datasets. Given the centrality of transfer learning and massive-scale pre-training, the field as we know it would not exist without open science. (Dell, 2024, p. 17 of the PDF)

Dell then turns the argument back on the profession: the more economics builds an open-science culture around big data, "the more we can benefit as a profession from the positive externalities of transfer learning," noting that deep learning researchers share code early to stake claims and release datasets early so a fast-moving literature uses them longer. The largest hub for models and data is Hugging Face — which, she notes, recently acquired timm, making it a one-stop shop for language and vision models.

9. Position in the literature

Within economics. The review's direct predecessors are the two canonical surveys it updates and delimits itself against: Gentzkow, Kelly and Taddy (2019) on "text as data," whose pre-2019 methods Dell extends, and Athey and Imbens (2019) on machine-learning methods for economists, which Dell leaves aside where it concerns shallow networks on structured data. It also partitions territory with Korinek (2023) on generative AI as an economist's productivity tool (out of scope here) and with the econometric literature it draws on for inference: conformal prediction (Shafer and Vovk, 2008; Chernozhukov, Wüthrich and Zhu, 2021), debiased machine learning (Chernozhukov et al., 2018), and the claim — which I read as one of the paper's more striking conceptual moves — that prediction-powered inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023; Zrnic and Candès, 2023) is equivalent to debiased machine learning, since causal inference and deep-learning imputation are both missing-data problems. Within applied measurement, the review consolidates a research program mostly authored or co-authored by Dell and collaborators — American Stories, LinkTransformer, EffOCR, entity disambiguation, noise-robust de-duplication — and in doing so it speaks to the economic-history record-linkage tradition surveyed by Abramitzky, Boustan and co-authors (2021) and Bailey et al. (2020): where those reviews document that rule-based methods still predominate, Dell marshals evidence that transformers outperform string matching across tasks and languages, often by a wide margin.

Within the deep learning literature it translates. The review's intellectual debts are the architecture papers it teaches — LeCun, Bengio and Hinton (2015) on representation learning; Vaswani et al. (2017) on the transformer; Devlin et al. (2019) on BERT's reusable backbone; Brown et al. (2020) and the Llama line on generative models; Dosovitskiy et al. (2020) on vision transformers; and the contrastive-learning sequence from Chopra, Hadsell and LeCun (2005) through Sentence-BERT (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019), DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020), CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), and supervised contrastive loss (Khosla et al., 2020). Its distinctive contribution is the economist's framing: a sustained argument that sample efficiency, not benchmark accuracy, is the relevant objective when annotation budgets are measured in hundreds of labels and hardware in a student cloud account — which leads it to champion bi-encoder embedding architectures over the field's seq2seq defaults.

Within the Hugging Face / open-AI topic library. Dell's review supplies the demand-side microfoundations for the ecosystem papers collected here. Laufer et al. (2025) count 2 million models and trace adaptation chains on Hugging Face; Dell explains why that adaptation activity exists at all — fine-tuning pre-trained backbones is the economist's (and everyone else's) route around the millions-of-dollars cost of pre-training, and the hub is where the backbones live. Wu, Yang and co-authors (2025) ask what makes models popular on the Hub; for an economist, the answer is inseparable from the workflow Dell documents (easy fine-tuning, task-specific heads). Longpre et al. (2025) trace power and participation in the open-weight ecosystem; Dell documents the same asymmetry from the user side — her applications run best in high-resource languages and deteriorate elsewhere, and her recurring examples (Japanese 1950s records, polytonic Greek, multilingual product linkage) are precisely attempts to push the ecosystem's frontier toward under-served settings. Azoulay et al. (2024) study whether open weights erode frontier firms' moats; Dell gives the complementary economist-side account of why openness is a research-input question: reproducible open backbones versus black-box commercial APIs whose deprecation ends reproducibility, and whose pricing can put large-corpus research out of reach. The "Legos" modularity she emphasizes is the architectural fact that Jacobides, Cennamo and Gawer (2018) would call modular complementarity, and it is what the platform-governance papers in the library — Yue and Nagle (2024) on PyTorch's open-sourcing, Wu, Lin and Li (2025) on Llama 2 as a commoditization shock, Khoshbash (2026) on specialization in open foundation-model ecosystems — treat as the substrate of complementor entry. Her observation that researchers release code and datasets early to stake claims and keep a fast-moving literature engaged is a field-level instance of the private-collective logic von Hippel and von Krogh (2003) describe, and it contextualizes the voluntary-contribution findings of Yeverechyahu et al. (2024). Even the topic's behavioral anchor, Greve (2003), connects: the problemistic-search variables Greve measures with archival R&D and innovation data are exactly the kind of outcome Dell's toolkit now builds at scale from unstructured corpora, and the review's data-exploration section argues that stylized facts from novel corpora are what motivate such hypotheses in the first place.

10. Limitations

Author-stated scope limits (paper claims). Dell is unusually explicit about what the review does not do: it excludes generative-AI productivity uses (Korinek, 2023), machine learning on structured data (Athey and Imbens, 2019), neural approximation of high-dimensional solution problems, and direct causal use of deep networks; it does not summarize how predictions have been used in economic studies, "as this literature is new and rapidly evolving"; and it restricts itself to imputing features "that humans are likely to agree on," warning that subjective constructs (implicit sentiment in media, political speeches, corporate reports) are unsafe territory where annotator bias and pre-training bias can both bite.

Evidence (what the review actually shows). The headline comparative claims rest on the author's own pipelines and corpora — the 19-topic classification exercise, LinkTransformer's 20 tasks, EffOCR's sample-efficiency curves — benchmarked as of mid-2024 (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o, June 2024) on historical newspaper content that, however diverse in topic, is concentrated in English-language high-resource settings plus a few Japanese and Chinese cases. The review itself flags the fragility of these numbers: GPT comparisons "may also change in the future"; results depend on label quality ("with lower-quality labels... a custom-trained classifier may well be consistently worse than GPT"); and prices fluctuate and are not cited. Claude's poor showing is attributed to refusal behavior and format compliance, which "may change with a future update."

My inferences (independent judgment, flagged as such). Several of the review's organizing claims outrun its evidence in ways the text does not fully address. First, the "deep learning beats sparse methods by 20 points" conclusion is documented on classification and duplication tasks where semantic generalization is decisive; it is less obviously true for tasks where exact term overlap is genuinely informative, which the review itself concedes. Second, the sample-efficiency argument for bi-encoders over seq2seq architectures is persuasive within the review's budget constraints but is an argument about academic constraints, not about achievable accuracy — Dell says as much in the OCR context, where full-sequence context "in theory" could help, and I read the architecture choice as a constrained-optimization result rather than a claim that retrieval beats translation at equal scale. Third, the review's examples are drawn overwhelmingly from the author's own research program, which is a strength for coherence but a limitation for independence: the reader is asked to take LinkTransformer, EffOCR, and their corpora as the canonical implementations of each method family. Fourth, the decision framework assumes a researcher who can label data and run fine-tuning; the generative-AI route is described as the low-startup-cost option, yet the review's own cost arithmetic implies that for large corpora the tuned-classifier route is the only affordable one, so the "choice" is often made by the budget constraint.

Open questions. The review leaves several threads explicitly open: active learning and representative sampling under severe class imbalance ("an active area of research, where economists have the potential to make important contributions"); uncertainty quantification, where conformal methods and prediction-powered inference are presented as early tools rather than settled practice; the reproducibility of commercial API results as models are deprecated; bias and fairness inherited from pre-training, acknowledged but not solved; and the general problem that even the best current OCR and layout tools degrade in low-resource languages, which the author identifies as a skew "towards settings that look more like high-resource commercial applications... not representative of the diversity of human societies." The review's own shelf-life is handled by design — the "Legos" framing and the continually updated EconDL website are the answer to the question "how quickly will this review become outdated?" — but that answer is a promise about infrastructure, not a guarantee.

11. Conclusion: takeaways for the open-AI economy

Three conclusions travel from this review into the rest of the topic library. First, economists' stake in deep learning is measurement: impute low-dimensional, uncontroversially defined structure from unstructured corpora at a scale no human could code, then analyze. Second, that measurement economy is built on an open, modular substrate — pre-trained backbones, public hubs, permissively licensed packages — so questions about the governance of Hugging Face, the openness of weights, and the terms of API access are, for a large class of researchers, questions about which measurements will be possible and reproducible at all; this is the same fault line Azoulay et al. (2024), Longpre et al. (2025), and White et al. (2024) study from the supply side. Third, sample and compute efficiency determine the representativeness of the knowledge produced: architectures and licenses that make fine-tuning cheap decide whether research reaches 1950s Japanese firm records and polytonic Greek rather than only English receipts, and whether the resulting corpora — like the 2.7-million-article newswire database whose entities are 4.7% women — expose rather than reproduce the biases of the historical record.

Deep learning can facilitate novel analyses by providing tools to impute structured information from unstructured data on a massive scale. When working with lightweight, sample-efficient pre-trained models, training and deployment are quite affordable, even for datasets with millions or billions of observations. — Dell, 2024 (p. 41 of the PDF)
