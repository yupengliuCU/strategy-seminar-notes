Foundation Models and AI Innovation: Evidence from the Hugging Face Platform

Authors: David Wu, Jinan Lin, Zhuoxin (Allen) Li (University of Wisconsin–Madison)
Source: ICIS 2025 Proceedings — Completed Research Paper No. 15; Forty-Sixth International Conference on Information Systems, Nashville, Tennessee, December 2025 · AIS Electronic Library: aisel.aisnet.org/icis2025/digitstrategy/digitstrategy/15
Nature: Empirical quasi-experimental study — a difference-in-differences (DiD) design with propensity-score matching on a developer-week panel drawn from the Hugging Face platform
Data & code status: Model metadata collected from the Hugging Face API for models published June 11 – August 26, 2023; cumulative download counts as of May 25, 2024; huggingface-hub library download series from clickpy. No public replication files or code are stated in the paper.

One-sentence summary

Using Meta's open-weight release of Llama 2 on Hugging Face (July 18, 2023) as a quasi-experimental commoditization shock to the text-generation domain, the authors show that pre-existing text-generation developers reallocated effort toward less-commoditized non-text-generation NLP tasks, that this reallocation was far stronger for experienced developers (a "capability retreat" of -28.7% in TG uploads, +12.0% in NTG uploads) than for novices, and that economic value — proxied by model downloads — migrated from the commoditized to adjacent domains.

Reading guide: This paper belongs to the "Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy" topic as a developer-level counterpart to the platform- and ecosystem-level studies in the library. Where Longpre et al. (2025) trace who contributes to and commercializes the open-weight hub, Laufer et al. (2025) map the adaptation network of 2 million models, and Khoshbash (2026) tracks provider-side specialization, Wu, Lin & Li ask what a single open-weight release does to the supply side: the individual developers who had been building text-generation models before Llama 2 existed. Their answer reframes open-weight publishing as a shock that devalues specialized downstream skills — the mirror image of the "openness wins" narrative — and adds a capability-retreat mechanism to the platform-competition theory of Wen & Zhu (2019) and the open-collaboration work of Yue & Nagle (2024). Watch how experience splits the response: incumbents retreat and narrow, newcomers explore broadly.

1. Background and motivation: when an open model commoditizes a skill domain

Digital platforms have long subsidized foundational technologies and offered them to ecosystem participants at low or zero cost, commoditizing the layer they provide. The authors open from this premise: "Economies of scale allow platform owners to make significant investments in foundational technologies and infrastructure, which they then offer to ecosystem participants at minimal or no cost" (p. 1 of the PDF). Their motivating case is Bloomberg's experience with BloombergGPT. Bloomberg spent over $10M training a GPT-3.5-class model on proprietary financial data in 2023, only to find that GPT-4 — trained without any finance-specific data — outperformed BloombergGPT on most finance tasks. The story is offered as a general economic phenomenon: when a general-purpose model absorbs the knowledge that a specialist paid to accumulate, the specialist's investment is devalued.

However, the commoditization of technologies often devalues previously specialized skills. For instance, Bloomberg spent over $10M training a GPT-3.5 class AI model, BloombergGPT (Wu et al., 2023) on their proprietary financial data in 2023 only to find that GPT-4, without specialized training in finance, outperformed BloombergGPT on most finance tasks (Li et al., 2023). (p. 2 of the PDF)

The research question follows directly: how do ecosystem participants strategically respond when a technology they specialize in is commoditized by an open release? The authors position this as a gap in the platform literature. Prior work on complementor responses has concentrated on platform-owner entry into complementor markets (Wen & Zhu, 2019; He et al., 2020; Li & Agarwal, 2017) and on firm-level competitive repositioning after entry (Seamans & Zhu, 2017; Wang & Shaver, 2014), and has largely overlooked experience-based heterogeneity among participants. Studies of open-source shocks have looked at governance changes rather than capability devaluation: Yue & Nagle (2024) study PyTorch's move from a for-profit corporation to a non-profit foundation, a shock to control of a core technology. And Lysyakov & Viswanathan (2023) show that crowdworkers threatened by an AI tool on a design platform responded by raising submission volume and quality — but that is a response within a fixed creative task, not effort reallocation across domains in response to a technology that touches a whole spectrum of activities.

The setting is chosen for clean identification: Meta released Llama 2 as an open-weight model on the Hugging Face platform on July 18, 2023, with parameter sizes from 7 billion to 70 billion. One of the best open-source LLMs at the time — comparable to GPT-3.5 — it "dramatically lowers the barriers to building text generation models, transforming a skill-intensive domain into a more accessible one." Text-generation (TG) developers are thus treated; developers working in other NLP domains are the natural control. The paper's headline findings, previewed in the introduction, are:

Our findings reveal heterogeneous responses to this commoditization shock from developers with different experiences. High-experience text generation developers strategically retreat from the commoditized domain, significantly reducing text generation model uploads (-28.7%) while repositioning toward non-text generation (NTG) domains and increasing NTG model upload activities (+12.0%). In contrast, low-experience developers demonstrate broad exploration across different domains. These differences are accompanied by a substantial shift in economic value, with high-experience developers’ TG models experiencing a decline in downloads (-102.4%), while their NTG models show significant gains (+58.4%). (p. 3 of the PDF)

The headline pattern is corroborated at ecosystem scale by derivative counts: "Meta's Llama alone has spawned over 65,000 derivatives on the Hugging Face platform" (p. 3 of the PDF). In the authors' framing, the deeper stakes are competitive concentration: if open models commoditize the foundational layer while frontier development concentrates among large firms and VC-backed startups, innovation diversity in the ecosystem may decline, and "these issues arising from AI commoditization may require regulatory intervention to preserve competition" (p. 3 of the PDF).

2. Core concepts and argument

The theoretical machinery has three parts: a characterization of the ecosystem, a theory of why an open-weight release is a commoditization shock specific to one domain, and an experience-based account of heterogeneous responses. Five hypotheses (H1–H5) organize the empirical work.

2.1 Open-source AI development ecosystems

Hugging Face and GitHub are open-source-centric ecosystems in which AI developers and researchers contribute models, datasets, and tools. Drawing on Greenstein et al. (2022), Jacobides et al. (2021), and Boudreau (2010), the authors note that open-source libraries and frameworks (Torch, TensorFlow, CNTK, PyTorch) were invented to catalyze research and were later adopted, maintained, and extended by large technology firms. Platform governance research has shown that access to core technologies matters: after PyTorch's governance shifted from Meta (for-profit) to a non-profit foundation, contributions from Meta fell while external companies' contributions rose (Yue & Nagle, 2024).

Foundation models are then distinguished from earlier open technologies as a "significant technological discontinuity." They are massive (billions of parameters), pre-trained on diverse data, and adaptable to many downstream applications with minimal fine-tuning (Bommasani et al., 2021). This makes them a general-purpose technology (GPT) in the sense of Bresnahan & Trajtenberg (1995): "the massive amount of embedded knowledge learned through pre-training" means a release like Llama 2 "expands the productivity frontier for AI developers by dramatically reducing the upfront costs of creating high-quality AI applications." Before open-weight releases, competitive foundation-model development was out of reach for most developers because of data, compute, and talent barriers (Achiam et al., 2023).

2.2 The commoditization shock and its domain-specificity

The core conceptual move is to read the Llama 2 release not as a productivity windfall but as a commoditization shock to the text-generation domain. Commoditization here follows Davenport (2005), Markus & Loebbecke (2013), and Shih (2018): as specialized knowledge becomes embedded in widely accessible tools, the technology playing field levels and opportunities for differentiation shrink. The authors cite historical analogues — the semiconductor industry's shift from aluminum to copper wiring in the late 1990s (Shih, 2018), CAD adoption in drafting (Boone et al., 2008), and AI-assisted coding via GitHub Copilot (Yeverechyahu et al., 2024). Text generation is singled out because it is the NLP task most directly absorbed by frontier LLMs; other NLP tasks "remain less affected by frontier foundation models." The shock is platform-wide in reach but domain-specific in force — all complementors face it, but its bite depends on how much of a developer's capability is embedded in the commoditized task. Hence:

H1. The release of Llama 2 represents a platform-wide commoditization shock that increases text generation developers' overall activities.
H2. Following the release of Llama 2, text generation developers will decrease their activities in the text generation domain and increase their activities in non-text generation NLP domains.

The reallocation logic behind H2 draws on the competition-driven repositioning literature (Wang & Shaver, 2014; Seamans & Zhu, 2017; Menon & Yao, 2017; de Figueiredo & Silverman, 2007; Lieberman et al., 2017): retreat need not mean exit. When a market is commoditized, firms relocate to adjacent markets where their distinctive capabilities still earn returns. The authors stress that skills in TG development are transferable to other NLP tasks, and that the NLP category on Hugging Face spans 12 subcategories (text classification, question answering, and so on), giving developers a nearby space to redeploy capability.

2.3 Developer experience as the moderator

Why should experience determine the response? The authors combine absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002) with the strategic-flexibility logic of the repositioning literature (Seamans & Zhu, 2017). Experienced developers have superior market intelligence, lower cost of evaluating declining returns, transferable skills, and the absorptive capacity to identify adjacent opportunities — so they should execute a sharper strategic withdrawal and redeployment. Novices, lacking established capabilities and facing lower repositioning costs, should read the shock as an entry opportunity and explore broadly:

H3. Following the release of Llama 2, high-experience text generation developers will respond more than low-experience text generation developers, in terms of decreased activities in the text generation domain and increased activities in non-text generation NLP domains.
H4. Following the release of Llama 2, low-experience text generation developers will exhibit more exploration activities and broader exploration relative to high-experience text generation developers.

2.4 Economic value capture

The final theoretical step connects effort to value. Because commoditization reduces the marginal economic return to capabilities in the affected domain (Boudreau, 2010, 2012), value — measured here as downloads of uploaded models — should migrate from TG to NTG models, with the migration mirroring the effort reallocation:

H5. Following the release of Llama 2, text generation developers will capture less value in the text generation domain and more value in non-text generation NLP domains.

3. Evidence and methods: a quasi-experiment around Llama 2

3.1 Empirical context

Hugging Face — founded 2016 — is the leading hub for open AI model development. By March 2025 the platform hosted over 1.48 million models and 324,364 datasets, served more than 100,000 daily users, and facilitated roughly 10 million downloads each day (p. 6 of the PDF). Text generation is one of its most active categories, covering chatbots, content generation, code completion, and translation. The authors argue that the Llama 2 release meets the conditions of a natural experiment: the release date was set by Meta's internal development timeline and strategy, independent of individual developers' behavior on the platform; and the shock hit TG developers but left non-TG NLP developers largely unaffected, creating treatment and control groups. A supporting fact is behavioral: daily downloads of the huggingface-hub library — the package enabling model downloading, uploading, and fine-tuning — spiked immediately after the release (the paper's Figure 1, p. 7 of the PDF), evidence that developers recognized and mobilized around the shock.

3.2 Sample construction

The sample is a developer-week panel built in three steps:

  • Window: all models published on Hugging Face between June 11 and August 26, 2023 — a 10-week window with five weeks before and five weeks after the Llama 2 release. The window trades off enough pre-treatment observations against the risk of confounding from other major technological events.
  • Model metadata: collected via the Hugging Face API (creation date, architecture, task category/pipeline tag, parameter count, training datasets, developer identifiers), plus a snapshot of each model's cumulative downloads as of May 25, 2024. Models lacking an explicit task category are dropped, and developers whose first model appeared after Llama 2 are excluded to focus on pre-existing developers.
  • Developer attributes: pre-treatment experience (number of prior models), developer type (individual vs. organization), and historical activity — used for propensity-score matching.

The final dataset holds 29,304 developer-week observations from 2,664 unique developers over the study period, and the matched sample pairs 1,332 treated with 1,332 control developers (p. 7 and p. 10 of the PDF).

3.3 Variables and measurement

Activity outcomes (log-transformed counts, $\log(\#+1)$, to tame right-skewed count data): All Models (total uploads in a week); TG Models (text-generation uploads); NTG Models (uploads of NLP models outside text generation). Allocation outcomes: TG Share and NTG Share (share of a developer's weekly uploads in each domain) and Unique Tags (the count of distinct pipeline-task categories a developer touched in a week — a diversity measure). Value outcome: Downloads — the average number of downloads (as of May 25, 2024) of models a developer uploaded in a given week, split into TG Downloads and NTG Downloads. The authors defend downloads as "a primary measure of visibility on the platform and represent one of the three core pillars of Hugging Face's organizational structure (Greenstein et al., 2023)."

Treatment and moderators: Treated = developers who uploaded at least one TG model in the pre-treatment period; Post = weeks after the Llama 2 release; High Exp = developers whose cumulative pre-release model count exceeds the sample mean (binary). High-experience developers make up 26% of the treatment group and 23% of the control group.

3.4 Matching and identification

To handle selection, the authors run 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity-score matching on pre-treatment developer characteristics — experience (models uploaded before treatment), development intensity (average weekly uploads), engagement consistency (active weeks), prior success (average downloads per model), and developer type (individual/organization). Balance tests on the matched samples show statistically insignificant differences between the groups. The baseline DiD specification is:

$$\log(\#Models+1)_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 Treated_i + \beta_2 Post_t + \beta_3 (Treated_i \times Post_t) + \alpha_i + \delta_t + \epsilon_{it}$$

where $\alpha_i$ are developer fixed effects and $\delta_t$ week fixed effects; the coefficient of interest $\beta_3$ identifies the differential change in treated developers' activity after the release. Heterogeneity by experience is estimated with a triple-difference extension:

$$\log(\#Models+1)_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 Treated_i + \beta_2 Post_t + \beta_3 (Treated_i \times Post_t) + \beta_4 (Treated_i \times Post_t \times HighExp_i) + \alpha_i + \delta_t + \epsilon_{it}$$

where $\beta_4$ is the additional effect for high-experience developers over low-experience developers ($\beta_3$). Standard errors are clustered by developer throughout; significance is flagged $^{***}p<0.001$, $^{**}p<0.01$, $^{*}p<0.05$. Identification rests on parallel trends and no-anticipation: the paper reports an event study of the pre-treatment period showing no significant trend differences before the release (its Figure 2, p. 10 of the PDF), argues that anticipation is implausible because Meta did not disclose the release timing in advance, and runs robustness checks with alternative control groups, alternative experience measures, and placebo tests (p. 9 of the PDF). 5. Position in the literature

The paper sits at the intersection of three conversations, and its sharpest contribution is to each of them.

Platform competition and complementor response. The most direct ancestor is the literature on complementor responses to platform-owner entry and competitive threats: Wen & Zhu (2019) on app developers facing Google's entry into their markets, He et al. (2020) and Li & Agarwal (2017) on entry spillovers, Foerderer et al. (2018) on platform-owner entry crowding out complementors, and Wang & Shaver (2014) and Seamans & Zhu (2017) on competition-driven repositioning. Wu, Lin & Li generalize these from firm entry by a platform owner to open-weight release by an external actor — a technological shock that affects the whole ecosystem at once rather than entering one complementor's market. Their mechanism label, "capability retreat", is meant to distinguish the response from the quality-improvement response found in crowdsourcing by Lysyakov & Viswanathan (2023) and from the output-enhancing effects of LLM coding tools in open-source contribution by Yeverechyahu et al. (2024):

Contrary to prior studies that suggest commoditization enhances the quality of developers’ offerings (Lysyakov & Viswanathan, 2023; Yeverechyahu et al., 2024), we find that commoditization induces experienced developers to strategically withdraw from the commoditized domain and pursue differentiation. This “capability retreat” mechanism highlights that even highly capable developers may abandon formerly lucrative niches when their specialized skills are devalued. (p. 14 of the PDF)

Because the treated population here is pre-existing developers, the result also speaks against the prediction that small, less-experienced complementors are the most vulnerable to platform shocks (Foerderer et al., 2018; Li & Agarwal, 2017): in this shock, the experienced incumbents lose value while novices gain — the opposite vulnerability ordering, driven by which group's capability base the shock erodes.

The economics of open AI and of the Hugging Face ecosystem (this topic's library). The paper is a natural counterpart to the other core papers. Greenstein et al.'s (2022) Hugging Face case supplies the institutional backdrop — the platform as an open, community-driven hub, with downloads as one of its three organizational pillars, a measure the authors adopt. White et al.'s (2024) Model Openness Framework is relevant as a caution: Wu, Lin & Li explicitly use "open-source" loosely to mean open weights (their footnote 1), exactly the gap the MOF taxonomy formalizes, so the "commoditization" they measure is a property of weight-sharing releases, not of fully open stacks. Azoulay, Krieger & Nagaraj (2024) ask at the firm level whether open-weight publishing erodes the proprietary moats of frontier builders; this paper asks the complementor-level analogue and finds the incumbents most hurt are not the frontier builders but the specialist app-model developers whose niche knowledge was absorbed. Yeverechyahu et al. (2024) find GitHub Copilot raises open-source output; the present result suggests the same class of technology can depress model-upload activity in the domain it most directly commoditizes. Yue & Nagle (2024) study a control-relinquishing shock to a core technology (PyTorch); here the shock is capability-commoditizing rather than governance-based, and the response is measured in product-market repositioning rather than contribution flows.

Within the platform itself, the paper connects to Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg's (2025) anatomy of 2 million Hugging Face models (the derivative tree that 65,000 Llama variants exemplify is the supply-side trace of the entry that the DiD captures as control-developer competition) and to Wu et al.'s (2025) registered report on what makes Hugging Face models popular (downloads serve both papers as the visibility measure). Longpre et al. (2025) trace corporate control and commercialization in the open-weight hub at the macro level; Wu, Lin & Li supply the micro-behavioral layer — how the developers who lose their niche respond — and both end at the same policy worry about concentration. Jin et al.'s (2026) evidence that U.S. export controls accelerated China's open AI ecosystems indicates one route by which further commoditization shocks of this type could originate. Khoshbash (2026) studies, on a longer Hugging Face panel (2022–2025), how model families and sponsors trade off specialization; the two papers divide the ecosystem between provider-side specialization choices and complementor-side retreat/repositioning behavior, with the 10-week window here trading breadth for exogeneity.

Behavioral and economic foundations. Although the authors do not cite it, Greve's (2003) behavioral theory of R&D search is conceptually adjacent: problemistic search predicts that firms redirect effort when performance falls below aspirations, and the "capability retreat" result is a platform-ecosystem instance of attention and effort being reallocated away from a domain whose returns have collapsed. The GPT literature supplies the demand side of the same phenomenon: Brynjolfsson et al. (2023) and Dell (2024) document how general-purpose prediction technology diffuses through tasks; Bresnahan & Trajtenberg (1995) is invoked directly for the GPT framing. The authors also add a Real Business Cycle analogy (Long & Plosser, 1983) — exogenous shocks propagate through the economy, rippling from the directly affected domain into adjacent ones — as a supplementary lens on why the NTG response appears so quickly.

6. Limitations

Caveats the authors themselves state. The paper is explicit that identification relies on parallel trends and no anticipation, defended by a pre-period event study with no significant trend differences and by the fact that Meta did not disclose the release in advance; the window is deliberately short (10 weeks) to limit confounding by other major events. The authors also flag that their measures are behavioral proxies — uploads for activity, downloads for economic value — and that their finding concerns pre-existing developers, not the full entrant population. They acknowledge alternative interpretations only lightly, and several of their design choices carry consequences they do not dwell on.

Independent judgment, with the textual basis layered as in the exemplar page:

  • Paper claim: The Llama 2 release operated as a commoditization shock to text generation, causing experienced TG developers to retreat and reposition (H2–H4) and value to migrate from TG to NTG (H5). This is asserted and estimated directly.
  • Evidence: The supporting evidence is the DiD/triple-difference panel on uploads, shares, tag diversity, and downloads (Tables 2–5), the pre-trend event study, and robustness checks with alternative control groups, experience measures, and placebos. The parallel-trends evidence is reported qualitatively ("no significant differences in trends") rather than shown with coefficients in the text, so its strength rests on figures the reader cannot inspect in the pdftotext extraction.
  • My inferences: (a) The paper treats log-coefficients as percentage changes — -1.024 is reported as "-102.4%" — which is fine for small coefficients but overstates level declines for the download outcomes; an exp() conversion would put the experienced developers' TG download decline nearer to a two-thirds drop in levels. The authors never make this conversion. (b) The "value" interpretation of downloads assumes downloads track economic value rather than, say, curiosity or visibility; for newly uploaded NTG models in an 11-month window this is plausible but unverified. (c) Control-group developers are "other NLP" developers; since Llama 2 is a general-purpose model, some spillover to their domains is likely, which would bias the DiD toward zero and make the measured effects conservative — but it also means "non-text-generation" is a cleaner label than "unaffected."
  • Open questions: ① The sample window ends five weeks after release; whether the retreat persists, reverses, or spreads as fine-tuned derivatives (and Llama 3, released a year later) accumulate is untested — the outcome data extend to May 2024 but the upload measures stop in August 2023. ② Developer "experience" is measured only by pre-release upload counts; capability in a deeper sense (model quality, fine-tuning skill, community standing) is unobserved, so the experience gradient may partly be an upload-intensity gradient. ③ The exclusion of developers whose first model postdates the release removes the purest entrants — the very developers who produce the 65,000 derivatives — so the paper measures incumbents' responses, not net ecosystem innovation. ④ Whether the value decline for incumbents reflects demand-side substitution (users prefer Llama derivatives) or supply-side crowding (too many similar models) is not identified. ⑤ The platform's own strategic responses (Hugging Face features, promotion of new tasks) and contemporaneous events (GPT-4 updates, other open releases) are held constant only by the short window.

7. Conclusion and takeaways

The paper's contribution is a mechanism and an ordering. The mechanism — capability retreat — says that when an open foundation model embeds the knowledge that made a downstream specialty scarce, the specialists respond by withdrawing from that niche and redeploying to adjacent, still-differentiated tasks, rather than by competing harder on quality inside the commoditized niche. The ordering — experience-based heterogeneity — says the burden of the shock falls on the most capable incumbents: their TG uploads fall ~2.5× more than novices', their overall uploads and downloads decline while novices' rise, and their only compensation is a narrower, deeper pivot into NTG tasks that captures value at roughly half the rate of novices' broad exploration.

For the "Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy" conversation, three takeaways stand out. First, open-weight releases are Janus-faced at the developer level: they democratize entry into a domain (newcomers flood in, spawn derivatives, and capture downloads) and simultaneously expropriate the incumbents who invested in that domain when it was closed — a micro-level value redistribution that complements Longpre et al.'s (2025) macro-level finding of corporate capture of the hub. Second, downloads-based measures of "success" in the ecosystem (cf. Wu et al., 2025) are, after a commoditization shock, partly measures of who was displaced: the same platform metric that rewarded experienced TG developers before Llama 2 punished them after it. Third, the paper supplies an empirical template for studying open-AI shocks as events with treated and control populations inside the same platform — the approach that Khoshbash (2026) extends longitudinally across model families, and that policy work (Azoulay et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2026) can use to forecast which participants absorb the losses of openness. If openness is to be an innovation policy rather than a rent-destruction event, the group this paper identifies as the loser — specialized, experienced, non-frontier developers — is the one that platform governance and research policy would need to watch.

The release of open-source foundation models like Llama 2 is a commoditization shock: experienced text-generation developers respond with capability retreat — sharply cutting commoditized TG work (-28.7%) and pivoting to adjacent NTG domains (+12.0%) — while novices, not incumbents, capture the new value through broad exploration. — Wu, Lin & Li (2025)
