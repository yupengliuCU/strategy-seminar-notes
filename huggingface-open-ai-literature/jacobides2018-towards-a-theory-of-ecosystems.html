One-sentence summary

Jacobides, Cennamo and Gawer propose that an ecosystem is a distinct form of economic organization — a set of actors held together by multilateral, nongeneric complementarities (unique/co-specialization or supermodular/Edgeworth) that is not fully hierarchically controlled — and that such arrangements become possible, and valuable, when modularity lets interdependent yet autonomous organizations coordinate through role-level standards and rules instead of fiat, with each member’s attachment governed by the fungibility (redeployability) of its investments.

Reading guide (tied to the “Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy” topic): This topic’s empirical papers describe a hub (Hugging Face) around which hundreds of thousands of developers, organizations and models form an open, loosely governed community — precisely the kind of object this 2018 paper gives a name and a mechanism to. Read it as the conceptual operating manual for the topic library: it supplies the vocabulary the empirical papers use implicitly (hub vs. complementor, openness vs. control, role-level rules, investment fungibility, ecosystem vs. market vs. hierarchy) and turns the Hub’s most visible facts into theory. A fine-tuned model has a unique, unidirectional complementarity with its base model (the fine-tune “does not function” without the base, while the base runs without most fine-tunes) plus supermodularity (more models on the Hub make the Hub and each model more valuable). The Hub itself is a “keystone” in the authors’ sense: it sets standards, interfaces and participation rules but does not decide what members contribute. The most consequential idea for this topic is the boundary the paper draws between ecosystems and markets: modularity alone pushes toward markets, so an open-weight hub only behaves as an ecosystem if nongeneric, role-level interdependencies and nonfungible investments keep members vested. Whether Hugging Face is best described as ecosystem, marketplace, or both is a live question the theory leaves to the empiricists — Wu, Lin & Li (2025), Laufer et al. (2025), Longpre et al. (2025), and Khoshbash (2026) in this library are, in effect, answering it.

1. Background and motivation: an “ecosystem” boom in search of a theory

The paper opens with a measurement of how fast the term has spread. In the 2014 prospectus of Alibaba — “the world’s largest IPO to date” — the word ecosystem appears no fewer than 160 times (p. 2 of the PDF). The vocabulary has moved beyond technology firms into financial services and manufacturing (the authors cite Deloitte, 2015). In strategy, Teece (2014, p. 1) is quoted proposing that “the concept of ecosystem might now substitute for the industry for performing analysis” (p. 2 of the PDF). Among the top strategy journals, the frequency of the keyword “ecosystem” in titles or abstracts “has increased sevenfold over the last five years” (p. 2 of the PDF). The intellectual lineage reaches back to Moore (1993), Iansiti and Levien (2004), and Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006), but the recent literature, in the authors’ diagnosis, concentrates on what ecosystems are and how they operate, and says much less about when and why they emerge and what makes them distinct from markets, alliances, supply chains, or integrated hierarchies.

That gap is the paper’s motivation. The authors state their objective plainly: “Our objective is to complement the literature’s interest in what ecosystems are, by analyzing how and why they differ from other phenomena, and by clarifying what unique mechanisms they have for value creation and value capture” (p. 2 of the PDF). They then ask when ecosystems will displace market-based arrangements or vertically integrated supply chains, and they sharpen “the distinction between the structures of ecosystems and the behaviors they give rise to” (p. 2 of the PDF). The paper is explicitly positioned as a companion to Adner (2017): Adner clarified how ecosystem research relates to established views; this article “takes a step further, synthesizing existing research to elucidate the key mechanisms behind the emergence and dynamics of ecosystems” (p. 2 of the PDF).

Despite the different emphases, the authors find broad agreement that ecosystems “require providers of complementary innovations, products, or services, who might belong to different industries and need not be bound by contractual arrangements — but have significant interdependence nonetheless,” so that “ecosystems do not fit into the classical firm-supplier relationship, Porter’s (1980) value system, or a firm’s strategic networks; neither are they integrated hierarchies” (p. 4–5 of the PDF). Most studies also assign deliberate intent to a central actor — the “lead firm” (Williamson & De Meyer, 2012), “keystone” (Iansiti & Levien, 2004), or “ecosystem captain” (Teece, 2014) — and, following Gulati, Puranam and Tushman (2012), treat the presence of an “architect” that sets a system-level goal, differentiates members’ roles hierarchically, and establishes standards and interfaces as an essential feature of an ecosystem (p. 5 of the PDF).

What remains open, in the authors’ reading, is a set of mechanism questions: what makes ecosystems — as opposed to integrated supply chains or arm’s-length relationships — “increasingly critical” (Adner, 2017, p. 53, quoted p. 5); what enables or inhibits alignment through an ecosystem; and what ecosystem thinking adds that other literatures cannot provide. Their foil is Adner’s (2017) alignment-based definition, which they quote verbatim:

“The ecosystem is defined by the alignment structure of the multilateral set of partners that need to interact in order for a focal value proposition to materialize.” Alignment structure, defined as “the extent to which there is mutual agreement among the members regarding positions and flows,” becomes the objective, pursued through a firm’s “ecosystem strategy” to “secure its role in a competitive ecosystem” (2017, p. 42, 47).

(p. 5 of the PDF; inner quotation is Adner, 2017, as reported by Jacobides et al.)

Jacobides and co-authors want to explain alignment rather than take it as the primitive: they will derive ecosystem structure from the type of complementarities and the fungibility of members’ investments, rather than treating “the resulting collaborative (or alignment) structures” as the starting point (p. 10 of the PDF).

3. Core argument I: modularity as the precondition for coordination without fiat

The first building block is modularity. Ecosystems, the authors posit, coordinate “interrelated organizations that have significant autonomy,” and in nearly all the empirical cases they know this is enabled by a modular architecture (Baldwin & Clark, 2000), in which the distinct parts of the ecosystem are organizations separated by “thin crossing points” (Baldwin, 2008) — discrete parts of the production process (p. 6 of the PDF). A hub may set the overarching architecture parameters, but each organization keeps “a large degree of autonomy in how they design, price, and operate their respective modules, as long as they interconnect with others in agreed and predefined ways” (p. 6 of the PDF). The iOS illustration is telling: app producers can set prices and freely decide the content of their products “even if there is a set of clear specs”; Uber drivers choose their availability and quality and face prices that are uniform within an area (footnote 6, p. 6–7 of the PDF).

The central claim is that modularity is what lets a hub give up explicit coordination:

Technological modularity allows interdependent components of a system to be produced by different producers, with limited coordination required. … The presence of modularity is also the condition that allows a hub to forego at least some degree of explicit coordination. Thus, modularity (though not necessarily open interoperability) creates the conditions for an ecosystem to emerge.

(p. 6 of the PDF)

Modularity is necessary but not sufficient. Building on Baldwin (2008), Langlois (2003), and Jacobides and Winter (2005), the authors stress that modularization, by reducing frictional transaction costs, “is more likely to lead to the emergence of markets” (p. 6 of the PDF). Ecosystems need the combination: a coordination need that markets cannot handle, yet which does not require the fiat of a central actor. The two tests — what separates an ecosystem from an aggregate of buyer–supplier relations, and from a market — are illustrated in Figure 1, “Different types of value systems” (p. 7 of the PDF): unlike a supply arrangement, in an ecosystem final customers can choose among the components supplied by each participant and, sometimes, how to combine them — the end user in the Android phones ecosystem “decides which apps to buy and from which provider, instead of buying a single, combined offering (‘as is’)” (p. 6–7 of the PDF). Unlike a pure market, the producers “are bound together through some interdependencies — by all adhering to certain standards, for instance,” so ecosystems “differ from networks (Powell, 2003), and represent webs of standardized formal or informal alliances between participants, where, for example, complementors can choose from a set menu of options and are treated similarly” (p. 7 of the PDF). Customers themselves must “affiliate” (Hagiu & Wright, 2015) with a group or platform to use its complements (p. 7 of the PDF). A clarifying footnote insists that modularity here means “the separability along a production (or production and consumption) chain,” not “plug and play” interoperability or free entry: Apple runs a modular system whose parts are open to different degrees — to its own organization, to suppliers, to complementors under stringent criteria, or with few restrictions (footnote 7, p. 6 of the PDF). Modularity does not entail openness.

The paper also stresses that ecosystem formation is “at least, in part, the result of deliberate experimentation and engineering” (Section 3.3, p. 9 of the PDF). Firms modularize processes deliberately, sometimes passing over generic complements on the market not only for design options but to build an ecosystem and create or extract more value. Even inside the Google/Android ecosystem, handset makers such as Samsung and Motorola are building sub-ecosystems by giving key app developers device-specific APIs, to “lock them in” with nonfungible investments (p. 9 of the PDF). Yet the process is not always governed by foresight: inkjet printer makers faced entry by unauthorized ink-cartridge producers, and when hackers developed the first apps for Apple’s iOS “they wound up in court — until Steve Jobs realized he could turn an unwitting ecosystem into a regulated one, and profit from it” (p. 10 of the PDF). The IBM System/360 and the early iPhone’s unauthorized-app ecosystem appear again in the research agenda as cases of “accidental” (or even illicit) ecosystems (p. 14 of the PDF).

4. Core argument II: types of complementarity — unique versus supermodular, generic versus nongeneric

Section 3.2 supplies the analytical core. The authors begin from Teece’s observation that “the literature on complements is both confused and complex” (Teece, 2018, p. 17, quoted p. 8 of the PDF — which page also records Samuelson’s 1974 remark that “the time is ripe for a fresh, modern look at the concept of complementarity”). They retain two types of complementarity that can be stated in unambiguous, mathematical terms.

Unique complementarity. The strict version is that “A doesn’t ‘function’ without B” — A and B can be specific items, “like the two ends of a pipeline (see Hart & Moore, 1990),” steps, or activities (p. 8 of the PDF). The general version is that the value of A is maximized with B rather than an alternative B′. Complementarity is a matter of degree, along “a continuum extending from strict or strong (where A requires B) to specific (where A requires B to be customized to it to be productive) to generic” (p. 8 of the PDF). Direction matters: unique complementarity can be one-way (A requires B but not vice versa) or two-way, and “two-way … is what underpins the idea of co-specialization (Teece, 1986)” (p. 8 of the PDF). The footnote connects the construct to its intellectual neighbors: transaction cost economics (TCE) has focused on unique complementarity arising from asset specificity, where integration becomes attractive (Williamson, 1985; Argyres & Zenger, 2012), while Hart and Moore’s (1990) strict complementarity makes coordinated investment critical because the assets are unproductive apart (footnotes 9–11, p. 8 of the PDF).

Generic complements, excluded from the ecosystem construct. Some complements are needed for a value proposition but are standardized enough to be sourced with little concern for governance or misappropriation (Helfat & Lieberman, 2002). “Electricity is needed for almost everything, but the fact that it can be purchased in generic terms means that this complementarity does not give rise to particular issues of economic organization, and as such, it can take place in markets instead (Adner, 2017)” (p. 8–9 of the PDF). The authors’ homely example is deliberate:

A teacup, boiling water, and a tea bag may all be needed to make a cup of tea, but the complementarities are generic, not specific. While consumers derive utility by combining these elements into a “product system” (i.e., a cup of tea), producers do not need to coordinate their investments through structures to enable such value. Consumers can thus buy them separately in the market and combine them on their own.

(p. 8 of the PDF)

Generic complementarities carry no vested interest in joint alignment and therefore get no special label. Strategically, however, firms can convert generic into specific complementarity: Nespresso “grew into one of Nestle’s most profitable units precisely by taking the generic complementarities between coffee and coffee-making machine and making them specific,” through capsules, co-specialized machines produced by outside firms such as Braun and Krups, and a “tightly integrated set of actors, bound together by standards and IP” (footnote 13, p. 8 of the PDF).

Supermodular (Edgeworth) complementarity. The second category, developed by Milgrom and Roberts (1990) on the basis of Topkis (1978, 1998), is summarized as “more of A makes B more valuable.” The authors quote the formal definition:

Milgrom and Roberts (1990, p. 6) noted that “a group of activities are (Edgeworth) complements if doing more of any subset of them increases the returns to doing more of any subset of the remaining activities.”

(p. 8 of the PDF; inner quotation is Milgrom & Roberts, 1990, as reported by Jacobides et al.)

Supermodularity appears in production — coordinated investments in A and B yield higher returns than uncoordinated equivalents, as with the Japanese production system (footnote 14; also Arora & Gambardella, 1990; Cassiman & Veugelers, 2006; Lee, Venkatraman, Tanriverdi & Iyer, 2010) — and in consumption, where it is “famously the basis of both direct and indirect network effects (e.g., Farrell & Saloner, 1985; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2005)” (p. 9 of the PDF). Direct network effects arise when consumers value other consumers’ use (fax machines); indirect network effects arise when a variety of complements creates value for other complements, so the final customer values variety (footnote 15, p. 9 of the PDF). The benefits of supermodularity are not symmetric between parties and can decline with saturation as the ecosystem grows (footnote 22, p. 11 of the PDF).

The two types can co-exist, and the authors’ canonical example is the operating-system/app dyad: the app and the platform have a unique complementarity (the app does not function without the OS — unidirectional, since the OS operates without most apps), and a supermodular complementarity (apps raise the OS’s value, and the breadth of the OS installation possibly raises the app’s value) (p. 9 of the PDF). This double structure explains, in the authors’ view, an empirical regularity in the literature: Adner and Kapoor (2010) found that upstream complements and downstream complements play different roles for ecosystem health, “which arises, we would argue, from the fundamentally different role of unique complementarity (dominant in production) from Edgeworth/supermodular complementarity (dominant in consumption, i.e., downstream)” (footnote 17, p. 9 of the PDF).

Section 3.2 then produces the paper’s first definitional statement: “ecosystems are groups of firms that must deal with either unique or supermodular complementarities that are nongeneric, requiring the creation of a specific structure of relationships and alignment to create value” (p. 9 of the PDF). The value proposition of the form follows:

The strength of ecosystems, and their distinctive feature, is that they provide a structure within which complementarities (of all types) in production and/or consumption can be contained and coordinated without the need for vertical integration.

(p. 9 of the PDF)

5. Core argument III: the ecosystem construct — roles, fungibility, and distributed control

Section 3.4 moves from building blocks to the construct itself. The authors announce a pragmatic epistemology: constructs are “vehicles to understand the world … judged by their usefulness in helping us do so” (Dewey; James; footnote 19, p. 10 of the PDF), and they choose to define ecosystems by “the nature of complementarities between ecosystem participants, and the fungibility of their investments, as opposed to the resulting collaborative (or alignment) structures” (p. 10 of the PDF). Their formal definition is the paper’s most-quoted sentence:

An ecosystem is a set of actors with varying degrees of multilateral, nongeneric complementarities that are not fully hierarchically controlled.

(p. 10 of the PDF)

Three attributes unpack the definition. First, the multilateral, nongeneric complementarities are unique complementarities (leading to some degree of co-specialization) or supermodular/Edgeworth complementarities (often found in complements-in-use). The narrow definition excludes generic complements: “We exclude generic complementarities because they do not give the parties any vested interest to align and act as a group,” and the uniqueness of ecosystems lies precisely “in the nongeneric nature of complementarities, which also entails some degree of customization” (p. 10–11 of the PDF). The authors concede that inclusive definitions may serve managerial purposes — warning managers not to underestimate external alignment when delivering an interdependent value proposition (Adner, 2012, 2017) — but they choose exclusion for analytical sharpness (footnote 20, p. 10 of the PDF).

Second, complementarities exist “at the level of the sets of roles (Adner, 2017) that link the different parties together — for example, hub(s), suppliers, or different types of complementors” (p. 11 of the PDF). Interdependencies tend to be standardized within each role:

What makes ecosystems unique is that the interdependencies tend to be standardized within each role, which creates the need for a new set of skills in terms of designing ecosystems (Helfat & Raubitschek, 2017).

(p. 11 of the PDF)

All apps in the Android or iOS ecosystems are treated identically; the interdependence is not between Apple and each of its millions of developers individually but “across the group as a whole,” and the definition and conditions of each role are themselves “a matter of strategic (research) design” (footnote 21, p. 11 of the PDF). This is “a fundamental shift from the usual mode of analysis — for example, in transaction cost economics (TCE) (Williamson, 1985)”: ecosystem arrangements “might be seen as webs of alliances, but these are standardized and set for each role in an ecosystem” (p. 11 of the PDF). The ecosystem canon is different from the TCE canon: TCE manages risk in dyadic relationships; the ecosystem canon asks how to maximize benefits by engaging a group of firms with complementary roles, where “neither prices nor qualities are fixed; they are left to vary, and to be chosen (by design!) as a function of the choice of a final user” (p. 11 of the PDF).

Third, the construct extends co-specialization (Teece, 1986, 2018) through fungibility — the redeployability of investments:

Because of the complementarities, connecting to an ecosystem involves some investment that is not fully fungible — that is, the investment, or assets in place, cannot be easily redeployed elsewhere without cost. This cost may derive from product/offering configuration adjustments … adjustments to the membership and transaction rules of other ecosystems … or coordination costs with other members’ activities.

(p. 11 of the PDF)

Fungibility “determines the economic basis of their attachment to that ecosystem” (p. 11 of the PDF) and, together with the nature and direction of dependencies, the intensity of complementarity, becomes “an important descriptor of an ecosystem that can help us understand when and why alignment occurs — or fails” (p. 11–12 of the PDF).

Finally, an ecosystem is not unilaterally hierarchically controlled: “What we think is analytically distinct in ecosystems is that their members all retain residual control and claims over their assets: No one party can unilaterally set the terms for, as examples, prices and quantities, in addition to standards” (p. 12 of the PDF). Ecosystems must be “both de jure and de facto run with decision-making processes that are to some extent distributed,” even though standards, rules, and interfaces are often set by a hub (p. 12–13 of the PDF). This is what separates ecosystems from supply chains, where “the hub (OEM, or buying firm) has hierarchical control — not by owning its suppliers, but by fully determining what is supplied and at what cost” (p. 13 of the PDF). The Toyota/Apple contrast in footnote 25 is the sharpest statement:

Toyota, which is at the center of a group of co-dependent suppliers that occupy different parts of the value chain and co-specialize with it (Nishiguchi, 1994), unilaterally decides what it will procure, from whom, and at what cost. Toyota is not, by our definition, the keystone of an ecosystem. Apple, on the other hand, with its App Store, is a keystone. It manages participation criteria, standards, and rules … but does not decide what specifically they contribute to the ecosystem (which app they should produce) … nor does it set prices beyond setting an acceptable range.

(p. 13 of the PDF; footnote 25)

There is wide variance in how open and democratic rule-setting is — from Apple’s strictly and fully controlled interfaces to Linux’s involvement of ecosystem participants (footnote 24, p. 12 of the PDF). Figure 2, “Types of complementarities and ecosystems” (p. 12 of the PDF), sorts real-world arrangements by the type of complementarity and whether it operates in production or consumption, yielding producer-based ecosystems, platform-based ecosystems, and multisided platforms. Multisided platforms (MSPs) may or may not be ecosystems by the authors’ definition: an MSP that requires only generic supermodular complementarity and no nonfungible investment is not an ecosystem, whereas Amazon Marketplace sustains an ecosystem “with Amazon at its core” because providers specialize into Amazon-specific interface, stocking, and shipping requirements (e.g., aligning with Amazon Prime) (footnote 23, p. 12 of the PDF).

6. Implications: a research agenda on ecosystem dynamics

Section 4 derives implications under four headings, each yielding contingent predictions.

Coordination (4.1). Modularity is a critical facilitator of ecosystem emergence, giving a testable prediction: longitudinal, within- or cross-industry research linking changes in modularization with the emergence and growth of ecosystems. But modularity is not exogenous — it “results from the agency of key industry participants,” and ecosystems can coalesce even without a focal firm’s wish to open up (IBM System/360; early iPhone) (p. 14 of the PDF).

Collaboration (4.2). The type of complementarity predicts behavior and organizing structure. Under unique complementarities, participants care about ecosystem health only insofar as its demise would eliminate demand; supermodularity increases the attractiveness of members’ own offerings and hence collaboration propensity (p. 14 of the PDF). The headline strategic tension concerns fungibility: “The greater the supermodularity and the lower the fungibility, the easier it will be to align effort for current participant members … However, the lower the fungibility the harder it will be to recruit ecosystem participants, who may fear being locked in” (p. 14 of the PDF). Alignment of incumbents and recruitment of newcomers pull in opposite directions. A comparative lens is needed across firms that choose ecosystems, markets, or vertical integration — the electric-vehicle case shows Tesla (vertically integrated, with supply networks), Nissan (open market), and Wanxiang (ecosystems with distinct, incompatible charging standards) taking different routes (footnote 28, p. 14 of the PDF).

Value creation and capture (4.3). The directionality and symmetry of co-specialization affect whether actors cooperate or expropriate, and recruitment versus retention. As an ecosystem becomes dominant, “recruitment takes care of itself, so that value distribution may become more lopsided” (p. 14 of the PDF). Across ecosystems, rules and required fungibility respond to rivals’ actions (Android vs. iOS). Regulators and prospective members will push for “interchangeable standards and generic complementarities that allow free entry and exit,” even though this may discourage hub investment in ecosystem formation (p. 15 of the PDF). The framework also yields a resilience prediction: “We would expect ecosystems with supermodular complementarities to be more resilient than those resting purely on unique complementarities” (p. 15 of the PDF). The historical illustration is Symbian, which held 67% of the smartphone OS market yet “lost ground to the young upstart Android” — much of it, per Pon, Seppälä and Kenney (2014), due to differences in organizational efficiency, governance, and the nature of co-specialization (p. 15 of the PDF).

Governance and regulation (4.4). Behavior and success depend on rules of engagement and on whether standards and interfaces are open or closed, imposed or emergent; membership may be open to anyone accepting minimal rules or strictly controlled by committee or hub, and rules can change over time (Facebook; Claussen, Kretschmer & Mayrhofer, 2013) (p. 15 of the PDF). Video-game console histories show Nintendo imposing strict exclusivity rules while Sony and Microsoft adopted more laissez-faire policies (footnote 32, p. 15 of the PDF). Open questions: when does control become so unilateral that an ecosystem becomes a supply chain; where does distributed governance succeed, as in the open-source movement (O’Mahony & Bechky, 2008); what can be learned from failed would-be hubs; and what of the complementors — “in July 2014 there were 2.3 million individuals working as app developers, with very limited power (Pon, 2016)” (p. 16 of the PDF)? Multihoming — developing for several ecosystems at once — is the one complementor strategy the literature has studied (Bresnahan, Orsini & Yin, 2015; Mantena, Sankaranarayanan & Viswanathan, 2010; Cennamo, Ozalp & Kretschmer, 2018), and developers such as Electronic Arts have used it deliberately to raise their value-capture ability against any single platform (footnote 35, p. 22 of the PDF).

Back to strategy (4.5) and the Appendix. If firms gain from others’ participation but cannot fully control them, resource-based views that stop at owned resources miss where advantage now lives: “How should this perspective change when the resources exist not at the level of the firm, but at the level of the ecosystem?” (p. 16 of the PDF). Helfat and Raubitschek (2017) supply the capability list — innovation, sensing, and integrative capabilities for orchestration — and the open question is how the value of resources differs by role (hub vs. participant). The Appendix relates ecosystems to industry analysis, industry architecture and bottlenecks (Baldwin, 2014; Jacobides & Tae, 2015; Hannah & Eisenhardt, 2016), alliances (ecosystem participation as a standardized, role-specific loose alliance; distinct from dyadic alliance research, Dyer & Singh, 1998; and from alliance portfolios, Wassmer, 2010), and networks (ecosystems can be mapped as networks but are segment-spanning and tied by group-level complementarity). Recent empirical exemplars it commends include West and Wood (2013) on Symbian’s conflicting incentives, Ansari, Garud and Kumaraswamy (2015) on TiVo shaping its television ecosystem, Kapoor and Furr (2015) on complementarities shaping entry choices in solar photovoltaics, and Gatignon and Capron (2017) on Natura deliberately not becoming the sole hub (p. 22 of the PDF).

7. Evidence and methods

The paper is a theory-building exercise, and its “evidence” is of three kinds. First, a structured literature review: the authors read all papers with “ecosystem” in title and abstract published in the top management journals, sorted them into the three streams of Table 1, and validated the groupings with NVivo textual analysis and independent research-assistant coding (footnote 1, p. 3 of the PDF). Second, formal economic constructs imported from the mathematics of complementarity — Topkis’s lattice theory and the Milgrom–Roberts definition of supermodularity, Hart and Moore’s property-rights treatment of strict complementarity, Teece’s co-specialization, Williamsonian asset specificity — which give the taxonomy unambiguous definitions. Third, a large set of illustrative cases drawn from prior scholarship and the business press, used to demonstrate that each cell of the typology has real-world referents: app stores (iOS, Android), console ecosystems (Nintendo vs. Sony vs. Microsoft), Amazon Marketplace, SAP’s independent software vendors, Nespresso, Uber, inkjet printers, the iPhone’s unauthorized apps, Symbian vs. Android, electric vehicles, Traipse, Wintel, TiVo, Natura, and solar photovoltaics. There are no new data, no hypothesis tests, and no formal model: the paper’s propositions are argued from definitions and examples and offered as predictions for future empirical work, in several places explicitly labeled as such (“This leads to a straightforward empirical prediction, which can be tested by longitudinal, within-industry or cross-industry research,” p. 14 of the PDF).

8. Findings: the theoretical results

Because the paper is conceptual, its findings are the framework’s claims and predictions, which can be summarized as five results:

  1. Ecosystems occupy a distinct governance space. An ecosystem is not a market (it carries nongeneric, multilateral complementarities that markets cannot coordinate), not a supply chain or integrated hierarchy (members retain residual control over assets; no one unilaterally sets prices and quantities), and not a network or alliance portfolio (its relationships are standardized at the role level, not bespoke or dyadic).
  2. Modularity is the enabling condition, but the demand condition is coordination need. Modularity creates the possibility of coordination without fiat; nongeneric complementarities create the reason. Alone, modularization tends to dissolve into markets.
  3. Complementarity type determines ecosystem type, value-add, and dynamics. Unique (one- or two-way, co-specialized) complementarities dominate production; supermodular/Edgeworth complementarities dominate consumption and network effects; generic complementarities fall outside the construct. The value-add of an ecosystem is to contain and coordinate these complementarities without vertical integration.
  4. Roles, standardization, and fungibility are the microstructure of attachment. Interdependencies standardized within roles let thousands of members coordinate without dyadic contracts; nonfungible investments determine who is vested in the collective enterprise — which is why low fungibility aligns incumbents but deters recruits, and why multihoming weakens attachment.
  5. Dynamics follow from the same variables. Supermodular ecosystems should be more resilient; dominant ecosystems skew value capture; hubs govern through standards, interfaces, and membership rules, and cross a line into supply-chain control if they start setting what is produced and at what cost; regulators push toward genericity and interchangeability, trading short-term openness against long-term hub investment.

All five are the authors’ claims about how ecosystems work, argued analytically rather than demonstrated empirically; the empirical illustrations (Symbian’s decline, Nespresso’s rise, Toyota vs. Apple) are offered as consistency checks, not tests.

9. Position in the literature

Within its own research stream. The paper is a consolidation-and-extension move in the strategy-of-ecosystems conversation of the late 2010s. It answers Moore (1993), Iansiti and Levien (2004), Teece (2007, 2014), and Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) by giving the hub/keystone idea a structural foundation (what hubs can and cannot control). It generalizes the innovation-ecosystem tradition of Adner (2006, 2012) and Adner and Kapoor (2010) by deriving when alignment structures arise, and it offers a reinterpretation of Adner and Kapoor’s upstream/downstream asymmetry as unique (production) versus supermodular (consumption) complementarity. It engages Adner (2017) most directly: where Adner defines the ecosystem by its alignment structure and treats ecosystem strategy as securing a role within it, Jacobides and co-authors make complementarity type and investment fungibility the primitives and alignment the outcome. It also converses with the platform-economics literature (Rochet & Tirole, 2003; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2005; Hagiu, 2006; Hagiu & Wright, 2015) and the governance-of-platform-ecosystems literature (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Cennamo & Santaló, 2013; Gawer & Henderson, 2007; Wareham et al., 2014) by carving ecosystems out of multisided markets where the definitional test — nongeneric complementarity and nonfungible investment — is not met.

Against its economic foundations. The paper is in dialogue with transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1985): it keeps TCE’s governance logic but replaces the dyadic transaction with the role-level, group-level interdependence as the unit of analysis, and it switches the canonical problem from risk mitigation in a buyer–supplier pair to maximizing joint value across a group with complementary roles. It draws on Milgrom and Roberts (1990) and Topkis (1978, 1998) for supermodularity, on Hart and Moore (1990) for strict complementarity, and on Teece (1986) for co-specialization — turning economics constructs that were developed for pairs of activities or assets into descriptors of inter-organizational structure. On modularity it extends Baldwin and Clark (2000), Baldwin (2008), Langlois (2003), and Jacobides and Winter (2005), with the twist that modularity is a strategic, partially designed variable rather than an exogenous technology. For the open-source and governance question it gestures at the distributed governance of the open-source movement (O’Mahony & Bechky, 2008) — the bridge to the governance-of-community literature represented in this topic’s supporting classics by O’Mahony (2003), O’Mahony and Ferraro (2007), and O’Mahony and Karp (2022), and to the selective-revealing and open-platform strategies of Henkel (2006) and Boudreau (2010). Wareham, Fox and Cano Giner (2014) — also in this supporting library — supply the complementary governance lens: where Jacobides et al. give the structural conditions under which ecosystems emerge, Wareham et al. theorize the governance tensions (generativity vs. control vs. cohesion) inside an existing software ecosystem; and Adner and Kapoor (2010) supply the empirical interdependence analysis that this paper re-reads.

Connections to the Part I core on Hugging Face and the open-AI economy. The four empirical anchors of this topic library read naturally as tests and applications of this framework (these connections are this reader’s mapping, not claims the 2018 paper makes — it never mentions Hugging Face or AI):

  • Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025), “Anatomy of a Machine Learning Ecosystem: 2 Million Models on Hugging Face.” This is the large-scale, network-style anatomy that Jacobides et al. call for when they note the value of documenting ecosystem network structure (Appendix; citing Iyer et al., 2006). Mapping adaptation chains, forks, and contributor roles across two million models operationalizes the paper’s role-level view: the Hub’s graph is organized around roles (base-model uploader, fine-tuner, dataset and Space contributor) with standardized interdependencies (the model card, the transformers/Safetensors interface), exactly the “sets of roles that face similar rules” of the 2018 definition.
  • Longpre et al. (2025), “Economies of Open Intelligence.” Tracing the open-weight hub’s contributions, commercialization, and corporate control speaks directly to the paper’s governance questions: which actors set standards and interfaces, how open or closed membership is in practice, and when a hub’s de facto control over the core tips the arrangement toward something more hierarchical than the definition allows. It also documents the complementor-side power asymmetry that Section 4.4 flags (2.3 million app developers “with very limited power,” p. 16 of the PDF).
  • Wu, Lin & Li (2025, ICIS), “Foundation Models and AI Innovation.” The Llama-2 open-weight release studied with a difference-in-differences design is an access shock: a hub-adjacent sponsor modularizes and opens its core, expanding downstream developer activity on the platform. In this paper’s terms the release lowered the cost of producing nongeneric complements (fine-tunes, applications) off a standardized core — an empirical instance of modularity enabling ecosystem growth without fiat, while raising the question of whether the resulting activity is ecosystem-like (role-standardized, partly nonfungible) or market-like (generic, easily redeployed), a distinction Wu et al. do not need to make but this framework forces.
  • Khoshbash (2026), “Specialization Trade-offs in a General-Purpose Technology.” The closest direct descendant in the library. Sponsor fine-tuned variants behave like the paper’s packaged interfaces or boundary resources (the hub setting standards that leave complementors room to differentiate), while MoE architectures internalize expertise in the core — the hub moving along the continuum toward occupying the complementors’ space. Khoshbash’s finding that sponsor specialization reallocates downstream effort and disproportionately deters resource-constrained developers is, in effect, an empirical estimate of the recruitment-versus-alignment and who-can-differentiate tensions that Sections 4.2–4.4 leave as open hypotheses.

The other Part I papers fit alongside: Yue & Nagle (2024) on PyTorch open-sourcing is a hub “foregoing explicit coordination” by relinquishing control to grow an ecosystem; Yeverechyahu et al. (2024) on Copilot measures a tool that shifts the cost structure of complementary production; White et al. (2024) on the Model Openness Framework provides the fine-grained vocabulary of openness (weights, code, data) that the 2018 paper deliberately leaves coarse (“modularity does not necessarily entail openness,” footnote 7); and Greenstein et al. (2022) gives the case-level picture of Hugging Face as keystone — setting rules and interfaces, not dictating what members contribute.

10. Limitations

Author-stated claims and caveats. The authors are explicit that the article is “a first step towards a positive theory” (p. 2 of the PDF) and that the research agenda sections are questions, not answers. They state the boundary choices of their definition openly: generic complementarities are excluded by design, and they acknowledge that an inclusive definition may be preferable for managerial purposes (footnote 20, p. 10 of the PDF). They concede that modularity is not exogenous and that ecosystem formation is often accidental (pp. 9–10, 14 of the PDF), that prior streams’ hub-mechanism claims rest on limited empirical support (p. 3 of the PDF), and that the interpretability of the growing ecosystem literature depends on the nature of interdependencies, which limits generalizability, especially of platform studies (footnote 29, p. 14 of the PDF). Their positive predictions — e.g., that more supermodularity and less fungibility ease alignment but hinder recruitment, or that supermodular ecosystems are more resilient — are offered as empirically testable conjectures, not tested results (p. 14–15 of the PDF).

Evidence base (what supports the claims). The evidence for the framework is illustrative and interpretive: curated cases chosen to fit the taxonomy, and a re-reading of others’ findings (notably Adner & Kapoor, 2010) through the new lens. This is consistent with the paper’s purpose but means its claims about directionality, fungibility, and resilience have not been independently verified; the Symbian story and the Toyota/Apple contrast are single cases used rhetorically.

Independent judgment (my assessment, not the authors’). Four observations. First, the taxonomy is cleaner than most real arrangements: the boundaries between strict/specific/generic complementarity, and between an ecosystem and an MSP or a supply chain, are presented as sharp lines but in practice are continua, and the paper offers no measurement guidance for the central construct of fungibility — a serious operational gap for the empirical agenda it issues. Second, the framework is not formalized: although it imports lattice-theoretic complementarity, it never specifies the objective functions or the coordination game that would make its predictions precise, so the “theory” is a disciplined typology plus conjectures rather than a model in the economics sense the authors invoke. Third, the mapping of unique complementarity to production and supermodularity to consumption, used to explain Adner and Kapoor’s finding, is asserted as an interpretation rather than derived or tested, and real products (e.g., an OS and its apps) exhibit both types simultaneously, as the authors themselves note — which blurs the very production/consumption split the interpretation relies on. Fourth, the paper’s stance is hub-side: complementors appear mainly as responders to fungibility and rules, and the call to study complementors (p. 16 of the PDF) is not followed by even a sketch of their strategic problem, beyond multihoming. None of these observations invalidates the framework; they delimit what it delivers.

Open questions the paper leaves. When does hub control become so unilateral that an ecosystem degenerates into a supply chain (asked at p. 15–16 of the PDF)? How do “accidental” or illicit ecosystems (iPhone’s early apps; IBM 360) get governed into regulated ones, and at what cost? Where does distributed, open-source-style governance succeed, and when does it fail against hub-led ecosystems? How does participation in multiple competing ecosystems change the intensity of complementarities and thus value capture (Cennamo et al., 2018; footnote 35)? And what role should regulators play when hubs resist interchangeability? For this topic’s purposes, the most pointed open question is empirical: how much of Hugging Face’s activity is ecosystem (role-standardized, nonfungibly invested) versus market (generic, easily redeployed), and does the answer change as the core models mature?

11. Conclusion and takeaways

The paper’s contribution is to give “ecosystem” a definition with teeth — a set of actors joined by multilateral, nongeneric complementarities and not fully hierarchically controlled — and to explain when such arrangements are likely to emerge and why they persist. Modularity supplies the mechanism of coordination without fiat; complementarity type supplies the reason such coordination is needed and the source of ecosystem value-add; fungibility supplies the glue of attachment; and role-level standardization supplies the microstructure that lets thousands of autonomous members align without dyadic contracts. The framework separates ecosystems from markets (which handle generic complementarity), from supply chains and hierarchies (which use fiat), and from networks and alliance portfolios (which are dyadic or bespoke), and it converts a fashionable word into a set of contingent predictions about alignment, recruitment, resilience, and value capture.

For the “Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy” topic, this is the canonical paper in the strongest sense: nearly every empirical result in the topic library is a statement about where, on the map this paper draws, open-weight AI activity sits. The Hub’s role-standardized architecture (base models, fine-tunes, datasets, Spaces), its keystone governance (rules and interfaces, not command over what members build), and the nonfungible investments developers make in model families are all predicted features of an ecosystem; the open question is how much of the platform has tipped toward the market side that the authors warn modularity alone produces. Reading Laufer, Longpre, Wu–Lin–Li, and Khoshbash against this 2018 framework turns their descriptive and causal findings into an ongoing empirical test of a theory of when ecosystems form, thrive, and cede control.
