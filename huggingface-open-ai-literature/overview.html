Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy
An overview of the topic and how its two libraries fit together

Topic synthesis · English · companion to the topic index and the literature map
Source: the merged reading library Dropbox/Hugging face/ (2026-09-04) — 14 core papers (Part I) + 21 supporting classics (Part II). Written for the research group; treat working papers as non-archival.

One-paragraph overview. Hugging Face has become the default commons of machine learning — a platform where model weights, datasets, and applications are shared, adapted, and reused at a scale the open-source software literature never observed (millions of models, hundreds of thousands of daily users). The reading library in Hugging face/ studies that object with two complementary bodies of work. Part I is the empirical core: platform-level anatomy of the Hub, the economics of open-weight releases, and the effect of foundation models on who innovates and how. Part II is the canonical theory the core speaks to: why contributors reveal private work for collective goods (private-collective innovation, selective revealing, commons governance), how platforms and ecosystems create and capture value (ecosystem structure, governance, complementor responses), and how open software and generative AI show up in firm productivity and entrepreneurship. Read together, they turn one empirical question — what does openness buy in AI, and to whom? — into a structured research conversation spanning organizational theory, strategy, and applied economics.

1 · The object of study: Hugging Face as a platform commons

Hugging Face started in 2016 as a chatbot startup and became, by 2021–22, the de-facto hub for sharing machine-learning models — most of it volunteered by an open-source community the firm does not direct. The 2022 Harvard Business School case (Greenstein, Yue, Herman & Gulick, Part I) is the descriptive anchor: a decentralized organization that adapts its development goals to its community rather than the reverse. The quantitative anchors follow. Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025) map 2 million Hub models as a network of adaptation and fine-tuning; Longpre et al. (2025) trace participation, commercialization, and corporate control in the open-weight ecosystem after 2024; Wu et al. (2025, “Learning From the Best”) ask which features make a model popular in the first place. These papers convert “open-source AI” from a slogan into a measurable structure of who builds on whom, and who captures value.

2 · The core questions Part I asks

  1. What does “open” mean in AI? Open weights are not open models. White et al. (2024) build the Model Openness Framework (MOF) — 17 components × open/partial/closed classes — to say precisely what a release does and does not disclose. This taxonomy is the vocabulary every other Part I paper implicitly uses.
  2. Does opening the core technology help or hurt the opener? Yue & Nagle (2024) study PyTorch and argue that relinquishing control of a core technology ignited downstream innovation; Azoulay, Krieger & Nagaraj (2024) ask whether open-weight publishing erodes the proprietary moats of frontier model builders. The two papers bracket the central strategic tension of open AI.
  3. How do foundation models reshape the innovation ecosystem? Wu, Lin & Li (2025) treat Meta’s Llama 2 release as a commoditization shock that pushed Hub developers out of generic text generation toward differentiated domains. Yeverechyahu, Mayya & Oestreicher-Singer (2024) ask whether AI coding tools (GitHub Copilot) expand voluntary open-source contributions. Khoshbash (2026) models the specialization trade-off: provider specialization widens adoption but squeezes complementors’ room to differentiate.
  4. Does policy shape the openness equilibrium? Jin, Kunievsky, Lou, Sun & Evans (2026) argue U.S. export controls unintentionally accelerated China’s shift to open-source AI — a geopolitical boundary condition on the whole literature.
  5. What drives innovation behavior and how do we measure outcomes? Greve (2003) supplies the behavioral-theory machinery (problemistic and slack search) for why firms change innovation effort; Dell (2024) supplies the deep-learning measurement toolkit for reading unstructured data at scale; Atlan (2026) is a practitioner benchmark on what AI deployments return in the field.

3 · The theoretical canon Part II contributes

Part I’s findings are only interpretable against the literature collected in Part II — the 21 classics the group pulled in from projects, Zotero, and seminars. Three streams do most of the work.

  • Open source & commons (II.1). von Hippel & von Krogh (2003) give the organizing theory — the private-collective model, in which contributors privately invest in goods they freely reveal; Henkel (2006) shows firms reveal selectively, keeping proprietary advantage while contributing; O’Mahony (2003) and O’Mahony & Ferraro (2007) explain how commons communities protect and govern their work; von Hippel (2005) generalizes to user innovation; O’Mahony & Karp (2022) bring the analysis to platform leaders who change access rules; Fleischmann & Haeussler (2026) measure degrees of openness in open-source AI itself.
  • Platforms & ecosystems (II.2). Adner & Kapoor (2010) and Adner (2017) define value creation through interdependence; Jacobides, Cennamo & Gawer (2018) theorize ecosystems via complementarities and modularity; Wareham et al. (2014) identify the governance tensions of software ecosystems; Boudreau (2010) separates granting access from devolving control; Rietveld et al. (2019) and Wen & Zhu (2019) analyze how platforms steer or threaten complementors; Wen, Ceccagnoli & Forman (2016) connect IP strategy to open-source entry.
  • Firm-level and AI-at-work economics (II.3). Nagle (2019) and Wright, Nagle & Greenstein (2023) quantify what open software does for incumbent firms and for entrepreneurship; Conti, Peukert & Roche (2025) show OSS engagement signals quality to investors; Haese & Peukert (2026) study firms moving their proprietary core to open source; Baker & Nelson (2005) contribute bricolage as the behavioral micro-foundation of resource-constrained innovation; Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2023) give the canonical generative-AI productivity field experiment.

5 · The live debates

  • Openness spectrum, not binary. MOF (White et al.) insists most “open” AI is partially open; the empirical papers operationalize the spectrum in different places — weights only, code, data, or ecosystem access. This makes cross-paper comparison of “open” a measurement problem, not just a semantic one.
  • Openness as strategy vs. openness as commons. Azoulay et al. and Yue & Nagle frame releasing the core as a strategic choice with competitive consequences; the commons literature (O’Mahony, von Hippel & von Krogh) frames contribution as a governance puzzle. Hugging Face sits at the seam: a firm that commoditizes its own platform layer to build ecosystem gravity.
  • Who captures value? Anatomy papers (Laufer, Longpre) document concentration and corporate entry in the Hub; ecosystem theory (Adner & Kapoor, Jacobides et al., Rietveld et al.) supplies the mechanism vocabulary; Khoshbash formalizes the specialization trade-off that decides where complementors can still differentiate.
  • Control points move. When weights are free, rents migrate to compute, data, evaluation, and downstream services — Jin et al. and Longpre et al. both observe control migrating rather than disappearing, which is exactly the pattern Wen & Zhu and Boudreau describe for platform owners.

  • Provenance: file renames and dedup decisions are recorded in _literature_admin/2026-09-04_merge/ (rename_map.csv, source_manifest.csv, validation.csv, acquisition_log.md).
  • Citation care: several Part I sources are working papers (Yeverechyahu, Yue & Nagle, Jin et al., Khoshbash, Longpre et al., Atlan) or in-press (Haese & Peukert; Conti et al.); check for published versions before formal citation. The annotated PDFs in 01_Coauthor_Original_14 are reader-annotated copies of the public versions.
  • This topic is not a course: it lives alongside the seminar directories in the same Reading Notes site, without course-week structure; new papers append to the closest thematic group on the topic index.
The question behind this library is not whether AI is open — it is which parts are open to whom, and what that openness buys the opener, the complementor, and the community.
