One-sentence summary

The paper theorizes a specialization tension in open foundation-model ecosystems: when the ecosystem orchestrator tailors a general-purpose technology toward specific domains, specialization can expand adoption by improving fit and lowering use costs, yet it can also internalize expertise in the core and limit complementors’ room to differentiate — and the evidence shows the two provider specialization moves (sponsor fine-tuned variants versus Mixture-of-Experts architectures) have asymmetric consequences, shifting downstream effort from core-modifying innovation (fine-tuning) toward interface-based application development, with larger models and organizational (versus individual) developers partially buffering the discouraging effect.

Reading guide (tied to the “Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy” topic): This topic asks whether open-weight hubs and open foundation models constitute a GPT-like co-invention structure and where the value-creation and value-capture lines run inside it. Most empirical work in the topic library studies shocks to access: a single release event (Wu, Lin & Li 2025 on Llama 2), a tool rollout (Yeverechyahu et al. 2024 on Copilot), or an openness decision (Yue & Nagle 2024 on PyTorch; Azoulay et al. 2024 on open weights). Khoshbash studies a different margin: the orchestrator’s specialization choices made after openness is granted, and it disaggregates downstream innovation into interface-based application development versus core-modifying fine-tuning. Read the theory section as a duel between two lenses — the GPT lens (provider specialization narrows the downstream co-invention space) and the ecosystem lens (specialized models act as packaged boundary resources that lower adoption costs) — and read the results as the paper’s attempt to let the two lenses win in different places: sponsor fine-tuned variants behave like ecosystem interfaces, while MoE architectures behave like internalized expertise that crowds out downstream modification. This is the closest the topic library comes to measuring who can still differentiate as open-model cores mature, which connects directly to the classic complementor-deterrence logic of Wen & Zhu (2019) and to the managed-ecosystem governance of Altman, Nagle & Tushman (2022).

1. Background and motivation: an open GPT whose providers also specialize

The paper opens with a familiar claim about the state of AI: frontier development firms have accelerated their investment in generative models, and much of this activity is unfolding inside open-source software, where firms release open models so that others can modify and experiment with them, including specializing a model to specific tasks through fine-tuning (p. 1–2). Generative AI is widely discussed as a plausible general-purpose technology, and the clean division of labor assumed in that discussion is that model providers build models that generalize across many domains while “downstream complementors take on the burden of diversification” (p. 2, citing Bommasani et al. 2022). The platforms on which this happens are what Altman et al. (2022) call managed ecosystems: contribution is broadly open, but the sponsor firm retains directional control through architecture, rules, and licensing.

The paper’s motivating observation is empirical rather than theoretical: the orchestrators themselves increasingly steer the core technology toward specialized configurations. It identifies two such moves. First, providers release fine-tuned versions of their own base models — an approach that specializes the core model for particular contexts (for example, Meta publishing Code Llama alongside its general Llama 3/3.1 models). Second, a new generation of models built on the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture embeds specialization directly into the architecture itself: multiple specialized sub-models (“experts”) are trained and selectively activated, making a single model internally modular across different kinds of tasks or knowledge (p. 3). Both moves happen at the model level, upstream of the developer community, and both therefore have the potential to reshape where downstream innovators concentrate their effort.

The historical analogy the paper reaches for is the transistor era: when general-purpose technology providers diversified their designs to meet application requirements, downstream adoption costs fell only as long as the providers did not themselves enter downstream markets (Bresnahan & Trajtenberg 1995; Conti et al. 2019a, 2019b; Gambardella & McGahan 2010; p. 4). The gap the author identifies is that this trade-off “has rarely been examined in terms of ecosystem formation” — that is, how an orchestrator’s upstream design choices in the core technology shape the downstream innovation activities of developers acting as ecosystem complementors (p. 4).

This framing yields the paper’s central tension, stated in the abstract:

I theorize a central tension: specialization can expand adoption by improving fit and lowering use costs, yet it can also internalize expertise in the core and limit complementors’ room to differentiate. As a result, specialization reallocates complementor effort across two distinct innovation activities: interface-based application development that uses models “as-is,” and deeper, core-modifying innovation that adapts models through fine-tuning.

(p. 1 of the PDF)

The paper then asks two research questions (p. 13): how upstream specialization choices by technology providers reallocate innovation activities between core-modifying innovation (fine-tuning) and interface-based complementary innovation (application development); and how this reallocation varies with capability conditions such as model size and with complementor heterogeneity such as organizational versus independent developers.

2. The theoretical tension: two lenses on the same upstream move

The theory section sets up the tension as a collision between two bodies of work that reach opposite predictions about provider-led specialization of a GPT.

The GPT lens. In the Bresnahan–Trajtenberg tradition, a GPT has three defining characteristics: broad use across many sectors, ongoing technical improvement, and the ability to spur complementary innovation in application sectors (Bresnahan & Greenstein 1999). The literature has generally assumed that the GPT provider has limited incentive to specialize toward application sectors, because adopters’ own experimentation creates specialized complements that raise the value of the core (Bresnahan et al. 1996). Co-invention is the keyword: downstream innovators develop specialized technology on top of a general one, and the provider’s role is to create an environment that induces future recombinant innovation rather than performing it (Bresnahan 2013). On this view, when the provider itself specializes the technology — whether by selling targeted variants (Conti et al. 2019a, 2019b) or, as here, by embedding specialization in the core — it narrows the opportunity space that downstream innovators would otherwise occupy in their co-invention process (Bresnahan & Greenstein 1999; Gambardella et al. 2021). Conti, Gambardella & Novelli (2019a, 2019b) formalize the mirror-image strategy: providers that stay upstream and trade general-purpose resources through intermediate markets can sustain a long-term strategy, especially when downstream demand is broad rather than deep. The paper deliberately turns this literature on its head: here the general technology providers themselves enter specialization, and the question is what that does to the ecosystem’s downstream innovators.

The ecosystem lens. Ecosystem scholars treat innovation outcomes as hinging on a focal firm orchestrating complementarity, technology architecture, and coordination among independent yet interdependent actors (Jacobides, Cennamo & Gawer 2018; Adner 2017; Adner & Kapoor 2010). In managed open-source ecosystems — Altman, Nagle & Tushman’s (2022) governance mode in which the locus of activity sits outside the firm while a focal firm keeps directional control inside it through architectures, rules, and incentives — core modification occurs through creating and recombining branches of the main project (Medappa & Srivastava 2019; Wright et al. 2023). Boudreau, Jeppesen & Miric (2025) treat foundation models as technology platforms that enable downstream innovation by opening part of the technology to complementors.

From the ecosystem lens, the same provider moves look favorable. Specialized assets released by the orchestrator are, in effect, packaged interfaces or boundary resources: more modular components that perform better for particular innovation needs and thereby bolster application development (Gawer & Cusumano 2014; Kuan & West 2023; Wareham et al. 2014; Baldwin et al. 2024). A specialized model used as an interface lowers the cost of adoption for complementors who want to build in a niche. The counterweight comes from a third body of work on platform-owner entry: when the orchestrator moves into the complementors’ own space — and fine-tuning is precisely a lever that modifies the core technology — the move can be read by developers as a signal that the provider is entering their specialization realm, disincentivizing core-modifying innovation, in the spirit of Wen & Zhu’s (2019) finding that platform-owner entry leads complementors to reduce innovation (p. 12).

The two lenses therefore agree that provider specialization rearranges downstream activity but disagree about which activity grows. The author’s synthesis is that the answer depends on how specialization is delivered: whether it arrives as an interface-like specialized release that complements application builders, or as architecture-internal specialization that leaves less of the core for developers to modify. 3. Core concepts: two innovation activities, two specialization moves

To make the tension testable, the paper defines the downstream activities and the upstream moves it studies. Each definition follows the paper’s own sections.

3.1 Two downstream innovation activities

The paper classifies developer innovation on top of foundation models into two categories that it argues are “fundamentally different” (p. 16). Application-layer innovation uses a model as-is: developers host models or use inference endpoints and build demo applications (Hugging Face “Spaces”) on top of them without changing the model — conceptually the complementor activity familiar from platform ecosystems, where innovation happens through interfaces decoupled from the core (Baldwin & Woodard 2009; Rietveld, Schilling & Bellavitis 2019). Core-modifying innovation changes the model itself — its weights, architecture, or components — through fine-tuning, adaptation (e.g., adding layers), quantization, or merging, which the paper treats as deeper innovation requiring model-architecture understanding and domain-tailored data (Shi & Jiang 2025; Wang & Huang 2025).

Two features of the setting make this taxonomy consequential. First, in open-source AI, complementary innovation aims less at enhancing the core architecture and more at specializing the core — expanding how the model behaves and where it performs better (p. 10). Second, because the model’s capability is embodied in reusable parameters, developers can recombine and extend the core in ways that resemble contributing to the core of open-source software (Baldwin & Clark 2006) — an activity that is part of the nature of a managed open ecosystem, in contrast to platform ecosystems where the sponsor controls the core and complements live at the application layer (Altman et al. 2022; Rietveld et al. 2019; p. 26).

3.2 Two upstream specialization moves

The paper defines model specialization along two dimensions (p. 15–16). First, some models are released specialized from the outset, typically as fine-tuned variants of a base general model targeted at one domain or task: “Fine-tuning refines a general model into a domain-oriented version, making it more knowledgeable about that domain and typically improving performance on its related tasks” (p. 11). Second, MoE architectures build specialization into the architecture itself by training multiple expert sub-networks and activating only a subset of them for each input at inference (Cai et al. 2025), so that distinct components take responsibility for different patterns of computation, tasks, or knowledge. The text is careful to note that experts are not assigned to named domains (e.g., “mathematics” or “coding”) a priori: during training the router learns to group related words and patterns and routes them to the same expert or a small cluster of experts (footnote 1, p. 11).

Within one foundation model family, the two moves are the two “folded” forms of provider specialization:

Within a single foundation model family released into an ecosystem, these specialized models are two-fold: 1) a model released specialized by fine-tuning of a base general model on a specific domain; 2) a base model that is internally and granularly specialized into smaller experts (conceptually similar to multiple models in one model) on multiple domains.

(p. 11 of the PDF)

Two moderators complete the framework. Model size (parameters in billions) proxies both capability and residual generality: “a 10B-parameter model specialized in coding is likely to have absorbed a far larger and more diverse coding-relevant corpus than a smaller coding model … potentially leaving more headroom for downstream developers” (p. 16). A larger specialized model can therefore flip the specialization–innovation relationship. Developer type (organizational account versus individual) captures heterogeneity in resources, goals, and knowledge, and the expectation that specialization, by raising the minimum expertise, data, and compute thresholds to innovate, disproportionately discourages resource-constrained (individual) developers (p. 12, 27).

4. Evidence and methods: the Hugging Face longitudinal panel

4.1 Setting and sample

The empirical setting is Hugging Face, whose Hub hosts pretrained models that users can download, adapt, and re-host, and whose complementary resources — datasets, open-source Python libraries, and hosted infrastructure for inference — lower the cost of downstream development; Spaces support application-layer innovation as lightweight hosted demos (p. 13–14). The author compiled a longitudinal panel of monthly Hugging Face snapshots capturing metadata and repository artifacts for 1,529 foundation generative AI models (plus linked organizations and datasets) from March 2022 to December 2025 (p. 15). Of these, 488 are base models and 784 are fine-tuned derivation models published by the ecosystem sponsor, with the remainder being versions of the same models (quantized versions, alternative formats, and other derivations); all models have more than 500 million parameters and satisfy the Bommasani et al. (2021) foundation-model criterion of broad-data, at-scale training that can be adapted to many downstream tasks (p. 15).

The 12 ecosystems and their sponsors are: Llama (Meta), OLMo (Allen AI), Bloom (Big Science), Deepseek (Deepseek AI), Pythia (EleutherAI), Gemma (Google), Granite (IBM), Phi (Microsoft), Mistral (Mistral AI), Nemotron (Nvidia), Qwen (Qwen AI), and Stable (Stability AI) (p. 15). Seven of these ecosystems have incorporated MoE architectures — the text names Mixtral-8x7B (Mistral) and Llama-4Maverick (Meta) as examples — and the developer sample comprises 2,768 developers (individuals and organizations) who built models on top of these foundation models or their derivatives in the window (p. 15). The abstract reports the same scale: “12 major model families, 1,529 foundation models, and 2,768 developers” (p. 1).

4.2 Model-level analysis

At the model level, the observations are model-months (N = 24,373; p. 32). The dependent variables are FineTunesmt — the number of models released by developers as fine-tuned using model m in month t — and Appsmt — the number of demo applications (Spaces) released on top of model m in month t. The independent variables are two dummies: Specialized by FT and Specialized by MoE. Model size (parameters in billions) enters both as a moderator (interacted with the two specialization dummies) and, because larger models proxy higher performance, as a control. Controls also include model age (months since release), license openness (47 license variants hand-collected and coded into three levels: 3 = unrestrictive, e.g., MIT/Apache-2.0; 2 = restrictive but commercial, e.g., copyleft-like or use-based limits; 1 = highly restrictive, e.g., noncommercial/research-only), a binary has-dataset indicator, and community openness (coded 3 = ungated access, 2 = gated with automatic approval, 1 = gated with manual review), plus ecosystem (model-family) fixed effects and month fixed effects (p. 16–17). Model fixed effects are deliberately excluded because time-invariant controls such as license and community openness would be perfectly collinear with them (p. 17). Because both dependent variables are positive, skewed count-like outcomes, the models are estimated by OLS on ln(1+DV) transforms and by Poisson pseudo-maximum-likelihood (PPML), with standard errors clustered on models (p. 19).

The author is candid that this design is associative: “More specialized models may be shaped by unobserved factors that also influence both the models themselves and their downstream adoption” (p. 20). The clean identification therefore comes from the second design.

4.3 Developer-level staggered difference-in-differences around MoE introduction

At the developer level the paper exploits the staggered introduction of MoE architectures as plausibly exogenous events. Sponsor fine-tuned releases cannot be studied this way — “Introduction of fine-tuned models happens even in the early days of releasing base models; therefore, it makes it impossible to be studied under a quasi-experimental design” (p. 20) — but the emergence of MoE models is a new technology generation. A developer becomes treated if they had any engagement (fine-tuning, application development, or other development on top of a model) with a specific model family-type during the 12 months before that family-type’s first MoE introduction; treatment is assigned at the earliest qualifying introduction, and “once treated, a developer remains treated for all subsequent months” (p. 18). Control developers are those never observed engaging with any family-type that introduces MoE during the sample (p. 18).

Treatment is defined at the family-type level deliberately: a developer who worked only with Qwen’s vision–language models is not treated when the MoE introduction occurs in Qwen’s text-generation line, because their experience and innovation activity are tied to a type that did not undergo the architectural change; likewise a developer working only with text-generation models of a family that never introduces MoE (Nvidia in the author’s example) is not treated by MoE introductions in other families (p. 18). The dependent variables are developer-month counts of fine-tunes (FineTunesdt) and applications (Appsdt) built on any of the foundation models. The control set includes a measure of total developer activity (DTAdt, the number of models uploaded other than fine-tunes and apps) plus developer and month fixed effects (p. 18–19).

Two complementary estimators are used (p. 20): a two-way fixed effects (TWFE) panel regression with developer and month fixed effects, clustered on developers, with an interaction between treatment and an organizational-account dummy (is-org) to separate organizational from individual responses; and the Callaway & Sant’Anna (2021) group-time average treatment effect on the treated (ATTgt), using not-yet-treated and never-treated developers as the control group, chosen specifically “to address staggered adoption and avoid known TWFE weighting issues under heterogeneous treatment effects” (p. 20). The results are also presented as TWFE-style estimates following Borusyak et al. (2024) (p. 24).

The author flags one concern explicitly: cohort composition is skewed toward the Llama family’s MoE introduction within text-generation models, so the staggered design could in effect identify the MoE effect from a single dominant cohort; the chosen remedy is to examine cohort-specific estimates within the Callaway & Sant’Anna framework (p. 22). Parallel-trends diagnostics use event-study patterns from both the TWFE event study and the ATTgt event study over a ±20-month window with event time −1 as the reference: “the applications series remains tightly centered around zero prior to treatment, while the fine-tuning series shows more noise and one non-zero fluctuation in the pre-period, but without a sharp pre-treatment trend break at the treatment boundary, supporting the plausibility of parallel trends more strongly for applications than for fine-tuning” (p. 23). A placebo test shuffles cohort dates across treated developers and finds no systematic post-placebo shift, reducing the likelihood that the main findings are driven by spurious timing or broad calendar shocks — while the author notes the fine-tuning results warrant a more cautious interpretation given noisier pre-period behavior (p. 23).

5. Findings: asymmetric effects of the two specialization moves

5.1 Model-level results

Model-level regressions (Models 1–2, paper’s Table 2) deliver the paper’s headline asymmetry. Sponsor fine-tuned specialization shows no significant association with attracting more downstream fine-tuning, but a positive significant association with application development (OLS β = 0.105, p < 0.01 in model 2a; β = 0.108, p < 0.01 in model 2b). MoE specialization shows the reverse pattern on the deeper margin: it is significantly associated with attracting fewer downstream fine-tuners (model 1b OLS β = −0.293, p < 0.01; model 1a PPML −1.059, p < 0.001; model 1b PPML −2.173, p < 0.001), and has no influence on application creation (p. 21).

Although the influence of SpecializedbyMoEm appears more robust and shows a larger influence regardless of its sign, the more exploratory and significant result is that different types of model specialization can have different effects on downstream innovation. Fine-tuning, which reflects deeper innovation, gets attenuated with MoE models. However, fine-tuned models released by ecosystem sponsors appear to generate more application-oriented innovation that does not require deep innovation and instead builds on interfaces where models are hosted as is, rather than being modified.

(p. 21 of the PDF)

Model size moderates the MoE effect. The interaction of MoE specialization with size shows a consistent positive relationship with both fine-tuning and application development (model 1b OLS β = 0.158, p < 0.001; model 1b PPML β = 1.442, p < 0.001; model 2b OLS β = 0.128, p < 0.05), which the author reads as scale preserving residual opportunity space (p. 22):

…although MoE models have a negative influence on fine-tuning in general, when MoE models are released that have greater sizes, they leave room for innovation for downstream complementors.

(p. 22 of the PDF)

Model size without interaction is itself informative: it has a negative relationship with fine-tuning when a large model carries no specialization (model 1b PPML; Table 2 reports −1.167, p < 0.001) and a consistent positive relationship with application development (model 2a OLS β = 0.077, p < 0.01; model 2b OLS β = 0.106, p < 0.001; model 2b PPML 0.203, p < 0.05): large general models attract hosting and application use rather than deep modification (p. 22).

5.2 Developer-level staggered DiD results

The staggered difference-in-differences around MoE introductions (paper’s Tables 4 and 5) confirms the discouraging effect on the deeper innovation margin. For fine-tuning, the TWFE estimate on treated is −0.609 (p < 0.001) and the aggregated ATT is −0.401 with 95% CI [−0.694, −0.107], “indicating a statistically meaningful decline in fine-tuning activity following exposure to MoE adoption” (p. 24). For applications, the effects are smaller but still negative: TWFE −0.009 (p < 0.05) and aggregated ATT −0.011, 95% CI [−0.021, −0.002], which the author describes as “a small and negligible outcome” (p. 24). The point estimates are summarized as: “MoE introduction is associated with reduced downstream innovation, with the contraction more pronounced for the fine-tuning margin than for application development” (p. 24).

Two heterogeneity results follow. First, the negative fine-tuning effect is not uniform across ecosystems: the cohort-aggregated estimates indicate statistically significant negative effects for the Llama–text-generation and MistralAI–text-generation cohorts, so “the evidence does not imply a uniformly negative impact across all cohorts, but rather that the contraction in fine-tuning is concentrated in these two cohorts” (p. 24). Second, the effects are detected more consistently among individuals than organizations. For fine-tuning, the TWFE moderation model shows a strongly negative effect for individuals (Treated Individual = −0.537, p < 0.001) while the incremental organizational effect (Treated × is-org = −0.628) is not statistically distinguishable from zero; the ATTgt split-sample pattern is directionally similar — individuals −0.163, CI [−0.250, −0.075] — while the organizational estimate is much larger in magnitude (−2.041) but with a CI including zero ([−4.330, 0.252]), “showing no specific negative innovation outcome for organizations” (p. 25). For applications, individuals show a small negative effect (TWFE −0.010, p < 0.05; ATTgt −0.012, CI [−0.022, −0.002]) while organizations show a non-significant −0.002 (CI [−0.015, 0.021]) (p. 25).

6. Discussion: specialization reallocates, it does not simply dampen

The discussion interprets the results as showing that provider specialization reallocates downstream innovation rather than simply increasing or decreasing it. The two specialization dimensions map onto the two lenses: sponsor fine-tuned releases behave like packaged interface/boundary resources that lower adoption costs and stimulate application-layer development, whereas MoE is architectural specialization that internalizes expertise in the core:

In contrast, MoE represents architectural specialization that internalizes expertise and compresses the remaining opportunity space for developers to differentiate through changing weights, leading to a pronounced reduction in downstream fine-tuning.

(p. 27 of the PDF)

Three theoretical contributions are claimed. First, the paper advances the innovation-ecosystems literature by studying a setting in which the core technology is itself a GPT (open foundation models), theorizing how provider specialization choices reconfigure complementors’ downstream activity along two distinct dimensions: specialization in the mix of models in the ecosystem’s core, and specialization embedded in the architecture itself (p. 26). Second, it identifies core-modifying innovation as a distinctive feature of generative-AI managed ecosystems — a deeper form of innovation than the application-layer complements that dominate platform-ecosystem research — and shows that provider moves can trade application-layer gains for core-modifying losses (p. 26). Third, it contributes a design implication to the economics-of-innovation view of GPT development: the technology should preserve room for downstream innovation, because doing so strengthens incentives for industry-specific innovators to build specialized applications (p. 26). The boundary-condition results sharpen this: large-scale MoE models “signal residual generality and ‘headroom,’ partially offsetting MoE’s negative average effect on fine-tuning even when specialization remains embedded in the architecture,” and the stronger deterrence observed among individual developers “reinforces the idea that when specialization raises the minimum expertise, data, and compute thresholds to innovate, it disproportionately discourages resource-constrained developers” (p. 27). The summary line of the discussion is that the evidence “shifts the theory from asking whether specialization increases or decreases innovation to showing that specialization reallocates innovation, changing where it occurs and who can participate” (p. 27).

7. Position in the literature

Within GPT economics. The paper is a direct extension of Bresnahan & Trajtenberg’s (1995) co-invention framework and of the modern GPT revival (Bresnahan 2010, 2013, 2024; Goldfarb et al. 2023; Eloundou et al. 2023, 2024 on generative AI as a plausible GPT). Its sharpest interlocutors are Conti, Gambardella & Novelli (2019a, 2019b) and Gambardella & McGahan (2010), who theorize when providers stay upstream and trade general technology through intermediate markets. Khoshbash studies the opposite strategy — providers that enter specialization — and asks what happens to the downstream innovation that GPT theory says should live outside the provider. The paper’s design implication (preserve room for downstream innovation) echoes Gambardella, Heaton, Novelli & Teece’s (2021) argument about profiting from enabling technologies, and the “junior GPT” / enabling-technology strand (Teece 2018; Rathje & Katila 2021; Kapoor & Teece 2021) supplies the continuum view of GPT-ness on which the paper leans.

Within ecosystem and platform research. The paper imports managed-ecosystem governance (Altman, Nagle & Tushman 2022 — the “translucent hand”), ecosystem-as-structure (Adner 2017; Adner & Kapoor 2010; Jacobides, Cennamo & Gawer 2018), platform architecture and modularity (Baldwin & Woodard 2009; Baldwin & Clark 2000, 2006; Baldwin, Bogers, Kapoor & West 2024; Kuan & West 2023), technology-ecosystem governance (Wareham et al. 2014), and complementor heterogeneity (Rietveld, Schilling & Bellavitis 2019). The closest theoretical relative is Wen & Zhu’s (2019) platform-owner-entry study, which the paper reads as predicting reduced core-level innovation when the orchestrator enters complementors’ space — but the paper refines this into a two-move story in which only architecture-embedded specialization (MoE) deters core-modifying innovation, whereas interface-like specialized releases (sponsor fine-tuned variants) support application development.

Within the open-AI and Hugging Face literature. The paper cites and builds on the openness-and-control analysis of generative AI (Azoulay, Krieger & Nagaraj 2025), AI-as-platform arguments (Boudreau, Jeppesen & Miric 2025), and open-source-AI governance (Shrestha, von Krogh & Feuerriegel 2023; Shi & Jiang 2025; Jacobides, Brusoni & Candelon 2021). Empirically it stands alongside the other Hugging Face studies in this topic library, and its distinct contribution is clearest in contrast with them. Greenstein et al.’s (2022, 2024) cases document the platform and its governance; Laufer, Oderinwale & Kleinberg (2025) map the structure of roughly two million models on the Hub; Longpre et al. (2025) trace power and participation in the open-weight ecosystem; Wu, Yang, Xu, Wang, Le & Lo (2025) ask what makes individual models popular. Wu, Lin & Li (2025) — cited here as Wu & Wu (2025) — use a difference-in-differences around the Llama 2 release to measure how an open-weights shock shifts Hugging Face developers’ innovation; Khoshbash instead treats openness as the baseline and identifies off architectural transitions within families (the staggered MoE introductions), while splitting outcomes into applications versus fine-tunes and developer types — a split closer to the ecosystem-vs-core logic of the topic than headline release-count analyses. It complements the access-shock studies elsewhere in the library (Yeverechyahu et al. 2024 on Copilot; Yue & Nagle 2024 on PyTorch control-relinquishment) by studying what orchestrators do with open cores after releasing them, and it supplies a downstream-complementor counterpart to Wang & Huang’s (2025) and Shi & Jiang’s (2025) analyses of resource accessibility and commercialization in open-source LLM ecosystems. Methodologically it sits in the modern staggered-DiD tradition it cites (Callaway & Sant’Anna 2021; Borusyak et al. 2024; Roth et al. 2023), the same toolkit family used by Wu, Lin & Li (2025).

Within the course’s supporting classics. The paper’s mechanism is a specialization-of-the-core application of the private–collective and selective-revealing logic of open innovation (von Hippel & von Krogh 2003; Henkel 2006): the sponsor reveals the core selectively but keeps directional control, and the openness of the core is precisely what makes core-modifying downstream innovation possible. Its dependent-variable split maps onto the governance of technology between core and complements that Wareham et al. (2014) and Jacobides et al. (2018) formalize, and its size-moderated headroom result is an empirical instance of the complementarity and modularity logic of Adner & Kapoor (2010) and Baldwin & Clark (2000).

8. Limitations

Limitations the author states. Four are explicit in the text. First, identification: the fine-tuned sponsor releases are introduced so early in ecosystem life that there is no credible control period, so only the MoE introductions receive a clean quasi-experimental treatment (p. 20, 27). Second, a visibility boundary: the analysis is bounded by what is publicly represented on Hugging Face; “substantial downstream innovation may occur privately (e.g., internal fine-tunes, on-prem deployments, or proprietary applications) and therefore remains unobserved” (p. 27), and future work should theorize the selection into public release, expand measurement to other repositories, and add interview evidence. Third, parallel trends hold more strongly for applications than for fine-tuning, whose pre-period shows more noise and one non-zero fluctuation (p. 23), so the author urges a more cautious reading of the fine-tuning outcomes. Fourth, cohort skew: the design is heavily weighted toward the Llama text-generation cohort, which the author addresses by reporting cohort-specific ATT estimates rather than by claiming uniform effects (p. 22). The author also offers countervailing evidence that public Hugging Face activity is substantively meaningful — leaderboard-topping community derivatives, intermediary firms publishing hundreds or thousands of derivatives, and the OpenVLA case (a Llama-based vision-language-action model with more than one million downloads that became a major open robotics model), which suggests “Hugging Face is not merely a ‘demo layer,’ but also a major channel for diffusion and recombination” (p. 28).

My independent judgment (layered and labeled):

  • Author’s claims: that provider specialization reallocates downstream effort from core-modifying to application-layer innovation; that MoE introductions reduce downstream fine-tuning with negligible application effects; that larger MoE models leave room for downstream innovation; that individual developers are more consistently deterred than organizations. These are the paper’s assertions, supported by its own regressions.
  • Evidence: the evidence is observational repository metadata — monthly counts of released fine-tunes and Spaces. The paper never observes the underlying effort, quality, revenue, or private use of the artifacts; the MoE mechanism (router-internalized expertise compressing the weight-modification opportunity space) is inferred from coefficients, not directly measured; and the “reallocation” interpretation rests on the assumption that public artifact counts track real innovation effort, which the text defends indirectly (leaderboards, intermediary firms, OpenVLA) rather than directly.
  • Inferences: reading sponsor fine-tuned variants as interface-like boundary resources and MoE as internalized expertise is the author’s interpretive gloss on a pattern of coefficients; the positive MoE × size interaction is read as residual generality/headroom, but a reader could equally read it as a mechanical scale effect (larger models have more downloads and activity of every kind). The contrast between a statistically significant developer-level application effect (−0.009/−0.011, labeled negligible) and a null model-level application effect is interpreted by the author as negligible impact; the difference itself is not discussed.
  • Open questions: ① whether the negative fine-tuning response persists as developers adapt or as post-MoE tooling (parameter-efficient fine-tuning, adapters) changes the cost of core modification; ② what happens to the quality and diversity of surviving fine-tunes and apps, since only counts are measured; ③ how much of the individual-developer deterrence reflects exits from the ecosystem versus substitution across families; ④ value capture — whether reduced fine-tuning redistributes value toward the sponsor’s hosted services; ⑤ generalizability beyond the 12 large, well-resourced families to smaller open-model ecosystems; and ⑥ the meaning of the organizational ATTgt estimate (−2.041), which is large in magnitude yet statistically imprecise in a small organizational sample (N = 5,049), leaving the org-vs-individual comparison unresolved in different specifications.

Textual consistency caveats (my observation, not the author’s): this reads as an unpolished working paper. The same developer sample is described as building on 1,457 foundation models in the introduction (p. 5) but on 1,529 in the data section and abstract (pp. 1, 15); the number of ecosystems with MoE architectures is given as seven in the introduction and data section (pp. 5, 15) but as six model families in the research design (p. 15); the appendix Table 2 header reads “Developer Level Regression Results” although the prose treats it as the model-level results table for Models 1–2 (p. 32); the prose drops the minus sign on the model-size coefficient for fine-tuning (printing β = 1.167 where Table 2 shows −1.167, p. 22); and the appendix of Table 4 prints the ATT upper bound as 0.107 where the text reports the CI as [−0.694, −0.107] (pp. 24, 33). A reader should verify coefficients against a final version.

9. Conclusion and takeaways

The paper’s bottom line is that in open foundation-model ecosystems the boundary between generality and specialization is a strategic variable held by the orchestrator, and where that boundary sits changes both what downstream innovators do and who can do it. Sponsor fine-tuned variants behave like ecosystem interfaces: they are associated with more application-layer innovation and are neutral for fine-tuning. MoE introductions behave like internalized expertise: at the developer level they reduce downstream fine-tuning (TWFE −0.609; ATT −0.401) with a small, negligible application response, and the contraction is concentrated in the Llama and Mistral text-generation cohorts and among individual developers. Scale is the counterweight: larger MoE models attenuate and can reverse the discouraging effect, because a bigger specialized model leaves more residual generality and headroom for complementors to build on.

For the “Hugging Face & the Open-AI Economy” topic, the paper’s contribution is to show that the open-model economy is not only opened or closed at the boundary of the firm — it is continuously re-shaped inside the ecosystem by design choices about how much specialization sits in the core. The same Hub that hosts “the closest we have come to general-purpose capability in AI” is also where providers progressively occupy the specialization space their downstream developers would otherwise fill. Whether an open model ecosystem remains a GPT-like engine of distributed co-invention depends, on this evidence, on providers leaving room at the core — and on who among the complementors has the resources to keep innovating when that room shrinks.
