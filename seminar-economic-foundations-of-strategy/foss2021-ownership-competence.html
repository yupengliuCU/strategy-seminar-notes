一句话总结

一句话总结

所有者（owners）行使使用（use）、收益（appropriation）、转让（transfer）三项财产权利的技能水平参差不齐；所有权能力（ownership competence）——即知道拥有什么（what to own，匹配 matching）、如何拥有（how to own，治理 governance）、何时拥有（when to own，时机 timing）——对价值创造的解释力，胜过代理理论（agency theory）与不完全契约理论（incomplete-contracting theory）所强调的激励对齐（incentive alignment）。

在第 3 周的主线里——古典产权理论（classical property-rights theory）→ 现代产权理论（modern property-rights theory）→ 战略管理转译（strategic-management translation）→ 所有权能力（ownership competence）→ 实证检验（empirical test）——本文提供的是能力观（capability view）：它把所有权能力界定为一个构念（construct），分解为植根于使用、收益、转让古典权利三分的匹配能力（matching competence）、治理能力（governance competence）与时机能力（timing competence）。Alchian & Demsetz (1972) 诊断的是计量问题（metering problem）；Grossman & Hart (1986) / Hart & Moore (1990) 把剩余控制权（residual control rights）建模为事前投资激励装置；Foss, Klein, Lien, Zellweger 与 Zenger (2021) 则主张所有权还承载着异质的技能（skill）——由此把现代产权经济学接回古典产权经济学（Alchian；Barzel），又向前接到以判断为基础的企业观（judgment-based view of the firm；Knight；Foss & Klein 2012）。它为主线末端将要进行的实证检验铺好了议程。

1. 背景与动机（Background and motivation）

本文针对的是主导所有权战略研究的三支经济理论共同拥有的盲点：代理理论（agency theory）、不完全契约理论（incomplete-contracting theory）与（古典）产权经济学（property-rights economics）。在这三支理论中，所有权之所以重要，是因为它重塑激励（incentives）。代理理论把所有权与剩余索取权（residual claimancy）相连——即索取契约约定的要素支付之后所余收益的权利——并主张以持股与监督来对付所有者与经理人之间的利益分歧。不完全契约理论把所有权与剩余控制权（residual control rights）相连——即在契约未描述的状态下决定资产用途的权利——并把所有权配置给关系专用性投资最重要的一方，以缓和套牢（hold-up）。产权经济学则强调权利的多维结构——使用（use）、收益（income）、转让（transfer）——以及通过有效控制实现外部性的内部化（internalization of externalities）（见下文表 1）。

作者的批评集中于该框架在一个具体意义上的不完整之处，而非否定激励本身的作用：它把所有者视为认知上同质（cognitively homogeneous），于是唯一的问题只是给多少所有权、给谁，从不问所有者将拥有得多娴熟。激励观隐含地假定行为者彼此相同，无论谁占据所有者角色都会高效地行使它，以最大化价值的方式管理与投资（p. 303）。然而，关于通往价值创造之最佳路径的判断（judgment）是个人特有（idiosyncratic）且不可让渡（inalienable）的；价值创造的战略并非广为人知；高效的所有者也不会自动与资产匹配。论文的核心直觉如下：

Building on the notion that ownership affords control in resource deployment, we develop the argument that ownership can be exercised with greater or lesser competence and that this matters to value creation.

>

Foss et al. (2021), p. 303, Introduction

>

中文复述：基于“所有权在资源配置中赋予控制权”这一看法，作者提出：所有权可以被更高或更低的能力来行使，而这影响价值创造。

两个框架性观察推动焦点从所有权集中度（ownership concentration）转向所有者身份（owner identity）。其一：更换资源的所有者，其影响可能大于改变所有权集中度——Steve Jobs 与苹果（Apple）的所有者取得对 Xerox 处组成之资源的许可控制权（licensed control）时，或 Sam Walton 的沃尔玛（Wal-Mart）接管小镇地产时，所创造的价值很难归因于激励的边际变化（p. 304）。其二：一条脚注指出，代理理论的经典框架预设股权高度分散（Jensen & Meckling 1976），而绝大多数企业是个人独资（sole proprietorships），所有者全权控制并参与日常经营——对这类企业，“激励对齐并非真正的核心挑战”（p. 306, n. 1）。在此背景下，论文援引关于“聪明钱”（smart money）的风险投资（venture capital, VC）研究：Sorensen (2007) 把资深 VC 的价值分解为筛选（sorting，投资于更好的公司）与影响（influence，重新配置资源组合），并把 VC 回报的持续性解读为“这些投资者技能异质性”的证据（p. 307）。论证的要点是：所有者身份具有激励类理论无法表达的战略内容。

2. 核心论点 / 框架（Core argument / framework）

2.1 所有权的三重含义与各自的缺陷（The three meanings of ownership and the problem with each）

论文第 2 节循法律与经济文献，区分了三种所有权概念化（conceptualizations of ownership）（论文表 1，p. 305；下表重制）。对三者的批评同出一辙：每种都把所有权当作一件工具，只要“正确”地部署，就能对齐激励、最大化共同创造的价值。被遗漏的是：所有者与潜在所有者对最优资源构形（resource configurations）持有彼此分歧的理论，而哪一套理论经由所有权获得授权，决定了结果。所有者可以把使用权拆分并委派给经理、董事会与员工，却无法完全委派这类终极决定——资产究竟是做什么用的、哪些权利委派给谁、所有权是否以及何时应当易手。因此所有者的认知——判断（judgment）、信念（beliefs）、认知框架（cognitive frames）——是激励理论所忽略的一个战略变量。

2.2 能力视角的四项假设（Four assumptions of the competence perspective）

第 3.1 节陈述该框架赖以成立的假设。其一，所有者处于 Knight 不确定性（Knightian uncertainty）与巨大复杂性之中：事前未知的不只是结果，连可能的资源与战略集合也是未知的，因此所有者与企业家（entrepreneurs）一样，把关于手段–目的关系的信念投射到现实之上。在真正的不确定性之下，让所有者得以按判断行事的是控制权（control），而非契约（contract）；若没有不确定性，所有权就没有必要——契约性的远见已足够。其二，判断的要素市场（factor markets）是“封闭的”（closed）：关于价值创造的信念是个人特有的，往往无法说服他人，且恰恰在独一无二时最有价值，因此其表达与验证成本高得惊人，没有哪个市场能有效地为它定价。其三，所有权是把自身判断放到现实中检验、并捕获其创造之价值的唯一可靠途径——契约做不到这一点，因为他人不信这套理论；所有权允许进入、退出以及快速地重新捆绑资产（re-bundling of assets）。其四，所有权的功能不止于收益权，还扩展到使用权与转让权——它们让所有者得以改变方向、从先前的部署中学习、清算，并对自身判断的质量作出最终评估。

2.3 定义及其产权基础（The definition and its property-rights foundations）

该框架把古典产权经济学映射为一套所有者技能类型学（typology）。任何人拥有一项资源时都保有 (a) 使用权、(b) 索取其收益的权利（the right to appropriate its returns）、(c) 转让这些权利的权利（Alchian 1961; Libecap 1986）。所有者行使各项权利时的能力各不相同——由此引出论文的核心建构：

We call these competences (a) matching competence (knowing what to own), (b) governance competence (knowing how to own), and (c) timing competence (knowing when to own), respectively. We thus define ownership competence as the skills with which asset owners exercise matching, governance, and timing competence (Castanias & Helfat, 1991; Helfat & Peteraf, 2015).

>

Foss et al. (2021), p. 309, Section 3.2

>

中文复述：作者分别称这三种能力为 (a) 匹配能力（知道拥有什么）、(b) 治理能力（知道如何拥有）、(c) 时机能力（知道何时拥有），并由此把所有权能力定义为资产所有者行使匹配、治理、时机能力时所具备的技能。

映射是一一对应的：使用权奠基匹配能力（拥有什么）；收益权奠基治理能力（如何拥有）；转让权奠基时机能力（何时拥有）——参见论文图 1 与表 2（p. 310），以及下文表 2 的重制。所有权能力经由关于拥有什么、如何拥有、何时拥有的卓越技能创造经济价值。作者认同 Alchian (1961: 63) 的说法：所有权能力“包括对风险承担的态度、对不同人生产能力（productive abilities）的了解、远见（foresight），当然还有判断（judgment）”（p. 309）。

一个展开的例子固定住这套逻辑（pp. 310–311）：某行动者预见到自家街区能跑出租车生意，买下一辆合适的车（拥有什么——匹配）；她决定自己开还是雇一名司机；若雇，如何监督他、如何分成（如何拥有——治理）；日后她可能把生意卖给一家比自己更看重这桩生意的竞争性出租车公司（何时拥有——时机）。司机行使的是关于日常运营的派生判断（derived judgment）；所有者则保有不可让渡的权利，决定出租车生意究竟存不存在、由谁经营、利润如何分割、所有权何时转手他人。

2.4 三种能力详解（The three competences in detail）

匹配能力（matching competence）：知道拥有什么（what to own）。 匹配能力是预见、判断或理论化地构想有价值资源组合的能力——这类组合服务于某个特定的、有意的目的，或解决某个独特的问题。它要求先界定目的或问题——这是使用权之下所有者特有的权能（prerogative）——再构造出能引导搜寻（自己的与他人的）资源之有价值（重新）组合的心智表征（mental representations）。由于资源具有多重属性，而组合空间浩瀚无边，试验、思想实验与理论化便替代了事前的详尽说明（论文将此与 Demsetz 的观察相连：可拥有的权利集合无法被完全描述，Demsetz 1988: 19）。正是匹配能力让所有者克服“功能固着”（functional fixedness）、识别被低估的资源，并借由决定拥有什么来划定企业边界。在 VC 情境中，它对应的是选中赢家（picking winners；Sorensen 2007; Baum & Silverman 2004）；私募股权（private equity）投资者之所以保留对组合内公司（portfolio companies）聚焦方向、结构与边界的权利，正是为了行使这一能力（p. 312）。

治理能力（governance competence）：知道如何拥有（how to own）。 治理能力是通过有效的治理与组织设计，把设想的资源束（resource bundle）组合起来的能力。它最接近“把激励弄对”（getting the incentives right），但同样是一种技能：挑选经理，使管理技能与资源配置要求相匹配；在信息不对称下选择自己管理还是委托他人；设计收益分享（rent-sharing）、派息（payout）、激励与监督制度（基于行为还是基于结果、留意动机挤出 motivation crowding）；按所有者自身的风险特征（risk profile）校准激励；并塑造对外关系，让作出关系专用性投资的利益相关者安心、免受套牢。论文呼应 Williamson (1996: 9)——各方“向前看、察觉风险，并把这些风险折回契约关系之中”（look ahead, perceive hazards, and factor these back into the contractual relation）——并补充道：能干的所有者在向伙伴投入资源时，也要保护自己免受套牢（p. 313, n. 6）。

时机能力（timing competence）：知道何时拥有（when to own）。 时机能力是为最大化价值创造而把握投资进出项目、资产乃至所有权本身的时机。它含三个成分。其一，市场择时（market timing）：买入被低估、卖出被高估的资源——市值高时发股、低时回购（Baker & Wurgler 2002），在市场高点附近推动公司上市（Lerner 1994）。其二，分期投入（staging）：把投资摊开在时间轴上，让价值逐步显现，从而限制下行风险、产生类实物期权（real option）式的战略灵活性——即日后行动的权利，而非义务。其三，继任与退出规划（succession and exit planning）：判断自己是否仍是“最佳所有者”（best owner；Dobbs, Huyett & Koller 2009）。竞争力量与技术变迁会使在位所有者的能力过时，因此具备时机能力的所有者能识别何时另一位所有者拥有更优的匹配能力或互补资源，接受高于该资产对自身价值的整体报价（lump-sum price），并规划自己的退出（pp. 314–315）。时机能力由此把企业边界的比较能力逻辑（comparative-capabilities logic）操作化（Argyres & Zenger 2012; Jacobides & Winter 2005; Poppo & Zenger 1998）。

2.5 区分性、习得性与情境（Distinctiveness, learning, and context）

三种能力彼此不同、可以分离：所有者可能某一种强而另几种弱。匹配能力卓越的创始人型所有者往往掌舵太久（时机弱）；家族企业可能在治理（吸引非家族经理）与时机（跨代控制意图使主观估值偏高）上吃亏，却凭行业知识与网络在匹配上出色（p. 315）；公司掠夺者（corporate raiders：T. Boone Pickens、KKR）兑现的是时机能力，激进投资者（activist investors：Nelson Peltz）兑现的是匹配能力；Warren Buffett 被树为时机能力尤强的范例（p. 316）。所有权能力看来也可习得：行业内的经验、治理的经历与反复的资产转让都能提升它们，连环创业者（serial entrepreneurs）每创立并拥有一个企业就精进一分（Gompers et al. 2006; Lafontaine & Shaw 2016, p. 316）。

所有权能力的价值依情境而定。所有权集中度（ownership concentration）被重新表述为调节变量（moderator）而非原因：集中持股放大能干所有者的效应、压制无能所有者的效应；集中度的真正作用是让按能力筛选出来的所有者获得控制权（Feldman & Montgomery 2015 发现：持股甚多但缺乏高层经验的董事与企业价值负相关，p. 316）。沿企业生命周期（life cycle），起步阶段匹配能力主导（拼出与创始人愿景契合的初始资源配置）；随增长与专业化，治理能力走到台前；企业成熟、必须剥离、购并并重新择时资产时，时机能力最重要。在环境不确定性之下，所有权能力比激励对齐更有价值：最优的所有者–资产匹配事前难以看清，受控实验不可能，个人特有的判断取代了循规则的搜寻——“当关于拥有什么、不拥有什么的边界抉择发生在效率相关性较低的不确定环境之中时，能力观应当支配激励观”（p. 318）。最后，要素市场效率（factor-market efficiency）给该理论画下边界：若资产市场按资源最可设想的用途完美定价，所有权能力便无法增值；它恰恰在失衡（disequilibrium）——资源落在错误的人手中——之时创造价值（p. 318）。

3. 证据 / 例证（Evidence / examples）

这是概念性论文，其“证据”由思想示例、推演案例与从既往研究引来的实证结果构成——用途是说明框架，而非检验框架。论辩由三种装置承载。

推演示例（Worked example）。 出租车生意的例子（pp. 310–311）在单一决策序列中走遍三种能力——买车（匹配）、雇司机并监督、分成（治理）、把生意卖给更看重它的竞争者（时机）——并用它区分所有者的原始判断（original judgment）与经理的派生判断（derived judgment）。

历史与传记案例（Historical and biographical cases）。 论文选用价值效应难以单用激励解释的所有权更迭：Steve Jobs 与苹果所有者对 Xerox 资源的重组；Sam Walton 的沃尔玛收购小镇地产（p. 304）；公司掠夺者拆解与再分配“低效的公司投资组合”（inefficient corporate portfolios）；激进投资者以少数股权强制推行更优的匹配判断；以及作为时机能力原型的 Warren Buffett（pp. 315–316）。这些以似真性案例（plausibility cases）呈现，并非系统性证据。

借自相邻文献的实证结果（Empirical results borrowed from adjacent literatures）。 论文倚重几组发现：(i) VC“聪明钱”研究——Sorensen (2007) 区分筛选（sorting）与影响（influence），以 VC 回报的持续性作为投资者技能异质性的证据；另有 Hsu (2004) 的发现：高声誉 VC 以 10–14% 的折价获得初创企业股权（p. 312, n. 5）；(ii) 公司治理与股权结构研究——Thomsen & Pedersen (2000) 表明所有者身份（创始家族、银行、机构、其他公司、政府）在所有权集中度之外还影响利润目标、股息、资本结构与增长（p. 307），以及 Feldman & Montgomery (2015) 关于缺乏高层经验的董事持有大额持股的研究（p. 316）；(iii) 关于时机的金融研究——Baker & Wurgler (2002) 论围绕市场价值的发股与回购，Lerner (1994) 论资深 VC 在市场高点附近推动公司上市（p. 314）；(iv) 家族企业研究中治理与时机之弱、匹配之强的证据（Neckebrouck et al. 2018; Zellweger et al. 2012）；(v) 连环创业的证据——所有权能力经由反复创立与拥有企业而习得（Gompers et al. 2006; Lafontaine & Shaw 2016, p. 316）。

以可检验的预测代替检验（Testable predictions instead of tests）。 讨论部分把框架转译为新的预测（第 5.2 节）：在其余方面相同的企业里持股相同的两位所有者，会因能力不同而作出不同的决策；所有权集中度调节能力–绩效关系；无能所有者是独立于激励不足或小股东掠夺（minority-shareholder expropriation）之外的价值毁灭来源——错配善意的项目、留住本应出售的资产；无能所有者会招致经理人的恶行，并可能使有能力的经理人泄气到保留努力或离职——道德风险（moral hazard）“不是代理理论所预言的投机（opportunism）的后果，而是低水平所有权能力所导致的挫败与动机低落的后果”（p. 321）；公司控制权市场把资产分派给最有能力的所有者，而不只是给最好的经理。这些以有待检验的假说形式陈述；第 5.3 节勾勒了测量路径（借鉴 Danneels 2016 的能力测量技术；更精细的所有权变动测量，如资产买卖、并购与剥离、重组与重构）。论文未提供原始数据。

4. 在文献中的位置（Position in the literature）

在第 3 周的产权主线内，本文位于第四个节点——能力/胜任力观（capability/competence view）——夹在产权经济学的战略管理转译（Kim & Mahoney 2005; Foss & Foss 2005）与收官主线的实证检验之间。其独特的一步是给现代产权理论“搬了戏眼”。Grossman & Hart (1986) 与 Hart & Moore (1990) 把剩余控制权当作事前投资激励装置：所有权配置给关系专用性投资最重要的一方，资产的互补性（complementarity）证成共同所有权。Foss 等接受剩余权利这套机制，但拒绝其中隐含的假设——无论谁拥有都会娴熟地行使控制。在他们手中，所有权之所以要紧，不仅因为它保护激励，更因为它赋权于判断（judgment）：即按照关于价值创造的个人特有、不可让渡的信念来组合资源的权利。因此，本文是对 Grossman & Hart (1986)、Hart & Moore (1990) 与 Hart (1995)——即形式化的剩余控制权分支——的自觉批判与延伸，方向指向古典产权经济学（Alchian 1961, 1965; Demsetz 1964, 1967; Barzel 1997）与奥地利学派/判断传统（Knight 1921; Foss & Klein 2012; Foss, Klein & Bjørnskov 2019）。

论文还整合而非反驳若干非产权理论（non-PRT）的战略学脉络。它自 Penrose (1959) 与资源基础观（resource-based view, RBV）取来“资源组合与能力是价值创造之核心”的思想，但指出多数 RBV 与动态能力（dynamic capabilities）研究很少谈到：组装这类资源束何时必须诉诸所有权（而非租赁或契约）——所有权能力“可被理解为坐落于所有者层面的一组高层级能力”（p. 320）。它自管理能力研究（Castanias & Helfat 1991; Helfat & Peteraf 2015; Sirmon, Hitt & Ireland 2007）借取技能词汇与“能力异质、可习得、栖身于个人”的看法；自企业家判断与基于理论观（theory-based view）文献（Felin & Zenger 2009, 2017; Benner & Zenger 2016）取来这一主张：行动者是“理论家”（theorists），其关于价值创造的分歧性信念才是真正稀缺的资源。定位（positioning）、交易成本经济学（transaction-cost economics）、战略要素市场（strategic factor markets）与增加值逻辑（added-value logic）（Porter 1985; Williamson 1985; Barney 1986; Brandenburger & Stuart 1996）在第 5.1 节被逐一重访，并被表明各自预设——却从未言明——一个具备匹配、治理或时机能力的行动者。该构念还与董事会能力（board competence）区分开来；公司控制权市场被重新解释为一台把资产分派给最胜任所有者的筛选装置。

论文明确点出自己的谱系：它遵从 Alchian (1961: 63) 的箴言——“人作为所有者的天赋各不相同”（people differ in their talents as owners）——并把“所有权能力”一词的提出归功于 Pelikan (1993)（在比较政治制度的语境中，很可能由其首创）：之所以需要自由市场，恰恰因为中央计划者无法评估或配置这种能力（p. 309, n. 2）。Foss & Lien (2010) 与 Foss & Klein (2012) 提供了以判断为基础的地基。Foss & Klein (2012) 把企业家判断（entrepreneurial judgment）视为不确定性之下关于资源配置的决策；本文则进一步收紧分析单元：匹配能力是“企业家判断得以行使的准确度”（p. 312, n. 4）。在课程主线里，这篇论文是从现代产权理论通向其后关于所有权假说实证检验的理论桥梁。

5. 局限（Limitations）

5(a) 作者自陈的局限（The authors' own stated limitations）

  • 论文对所有权能力的前因（antecedents）说得很少：“我们对所有权能力的前因说得很少”（p. 322）。连环创业者的证据暗示经验与决策频率要紧，但论文没有给出能力从何而来、如何发展的理论。
  • 测量被承认悬而未决。作者提议借用为其他能力开发的技术（Danneels 2016; Grant & Verona 2015; Stadler, Helfat & Verona 2013）与更精细的所有权变动测量，但测量工具尚不存在；各项调节效应（集中度、生命周期、不确定性、要素市场效率）“值得研究”，而非已经确立（p. 322）。
  • 所有权能力位于所有者层面而非资产层面，因此同一位所有者可能擅长拥有某些资产、不擅长拥有另一些（能干的企业家可能是糟糕的金融资产投资者；Zellweger 2017）。能力在跨资产组合中如何加总，悬而未决。
  • 关于公司控制权市场（把资产分派给最佳所有者）与三种能力相对重要性的预测，是以“亟待实证检验”（rife for empirical testing）的假说形式陈述的，并无直接测量（p. 322）。
  • 框架把目的括在括号之外：所有者持有资产的目的可能超出“为所有者创造价值”，因此评估能力应透过资产持有目的来过滤（Henderson & Serafeim 2020）——提及了，未展开。

5(b) 独立评断（Independent judgment）

  • [论文论断] 核心前提——代理、不完全契约与产权理论都把所有者视为同质、从而漏掉技能——是论文自身的刻画（pp. 303–304）。这一论断对形式化的 GHM 传统是公允的，对古典产权经济学则稍欠公允：后者早已在 Alchian 的“所有者的天赋”（talents as owners）一语中埋下了能力的种子，而论文自己就引用了这句话。
  • [证据] 支撑性经验材料（VC 回报持续性、Thomsen & Pedersen 2000、Feldman & Montgomery 2015、连环创业者结果）借自相邻领域，属示例性质。Feldman & Montgomery (2015) 研究的是持股甚多而高层经验有限的董事——这是所有权能力的间接代理指标（proxy），不是直接测量。
  • [推断] 存在潜在的循环论证风险：若所有权能力是从已实现的价值创造中反推出来的，“能干的所有者创造价值”就近乎定义上为真（definitionally true）。作者提出的测量（所有权变动、连环成功）能缓解却消除不了这一点，因为能力与有利机会中的选择效应事前难以分离。论文没有处理这一顾虑。
  • [推断] 治理能力与普通管理能力之间的边界，比论文暗示的更薄。既然所有者可以把治理设计委托给雇来的经理与董事会，论文所称治理能力的大部分，可能只是所有者层面关于把权委给谁的判断——而这又滑回匹配与时机。三个维度在操作上的可分性，可能低于概念上的可分性。
  • [开放问题] 所有权能力究竟是个体特质（trait）、所有者群体的属性，还是企业层面的组织能力，尚无定论；论文在“所有者”、创始团队与所有者群体之间摇摆，第 5.3 节也承认跨组合的加总无人研究。
  • [开放问题] 该理论的预测内容取决于能力稀缺、异质且不可契约化（non-contractible）。在要素市场（近乎）有效之处——拥有流动市场、强力中介与成熟董事会的大型上市公司——所有权能力能够增值的领地可能远比论文的修辞所暗示的窄；论文自己承认这条效率边界，但没有量化现实经济体落在边界的何处。
  • [推断] 若时机能力包含把资产卖给更优的所有者，匹配能力包含识别被低估的资源束，则框架隐含：缺乏能力的所有者往往无法认清这一事实——作者承认了这点（“所有权无能常造成持续错失的机会”，p. 321），但关于市场如何纠错的含义（接管机制依赖某个有能力的人来买，而非无能的卖方自己察觉）值得更锋利的处理。

6. 结论（Conclusion）

论文确立了一种所有权的能力观，作为对主导代理理论与不完全契约理论的激励观（incentive view）的补充——以及部分修正。其持久的贡献在于：所有权能力这一构念——定义为以技能行使所有权以创造价值；把它分解为匹配（拥有什么）、治理（如何拥有）、时机（何时拥有）三种能力，各自植根于三项古典财产权利之一；论证了异质的所有者技能——而不只是所有权集中度或激励强度——驱动价值创造；并给出一个横跨测量、调节变量、生命周期动态，以及把公司控制权市场重新解释为“把资产分派给最有能力所有者的市场”的研究议程。论文的收尾句承载一句规范性的总结：

We stress that value is created when those who possess competence also assume ownership.

>

Foss et al. (2021), p. 323, Conclusion

>

中文复述：我们强调：当拥有能力的人同时承担所有权时，价值便被创造出来。

对经理人与投资者的战略启示直截了当：所有权是一份有技能要求的工作——治理应围绕筛选并留住“有能力拥有”（competent to own）的所有者来搭建；当能力匹配恶化时，更换所有者，就像经理人的技能不再匹配时更换经理一样自然。

"People differ in their talents as owners." (Alchian, 1961: 63, as quoted in Foss et al., 2021, p. 304)

Ownership competence

Authors: Nicolai J. Foss, Peter G. Klein, Lasse B. Lien, Thomas Zellweger, Todd Zenger
Journal: Strategic Management Journal, Vol. 42, No. 2, pp. 302–328
Year: 2021 (first published online 9 August 2020)
DOI: 10.1002/smj.3222
Nature: conceptual / theory-development paper — a competence (capability) view of ownership: from incentives to the skill of owning
Keywords: assets, economic value creation, ownership, theory of the firm (as printed in the article)

One-sentence summary

Owners vary in the skill with which they exercise the property rights of use, appropriation, and transfer, and ownership competence — knowing what to own (matching), how to own (governance), and when to own (timing) — explains value creation better than the incentive alignment stressed by agency and incomplete-contracting theories.

Reading note. In the Week-3 arc (classical property-rights theory → modern property-rights theory → strategic-management translation → ownership competence → empirical test), this paper supplies the capability view: it defines ownership competence as a construct and decomposes it into matching, governance, and timing competence rooted in the classical triad of use, appropriation, and transfer rights. Where Alchian & Demsetz (1972) diagnose the metering problem and Grossman & Hart (1986) / Hart & Moore (1990) model residual control rights as an ex-ante investment incentive device, Foss, Klein, Lien, Zellweger, and Zenger (2021) argue that ownership also carries heterogeneous skill, linking modern property-rights economics back to classical property-rights economics (Alchian; Barzel) and forward to the judgment-based view of the firm (Knight; Foss & Klein 2012). It prepares the agenda that the arc's empirical test then probes.

1. Background and motivation

The paper attacks a shared blind spot in the three economic theories that dominate strategy research on ownership: agency theory, incomplete-contracting theory, and (classical) property-rights economics. In all three, ownership matters because it reshapes incentives. Agency theory ties ownership to residual claimancy — the right to capture what remains after contractually stipulated factor payments — and prescribes ownership stakes and monitoring against divergent owner–manager interests. Incomplete-contracting theory ties ownership to residual control rights — the right to decide asset use in states not described by contract — and allocates ownership to the party whose relationship-specific investments matter most, softening hold-up. Property-rights economics emphasizes the multidimensional structure of rights — use, income, and transfer — and the internalization of externalities through effective control (Table 1 below).

The authors' objection is not that incentives are irrelevant but that this framing is incomplete in a specific way: it treats owners as cognitively homogeneous, so the only question is how much and to whom to give ownership, never how skillfully the owner will own. The incentive view implicitly assumes actors are alike and that whoever occupies the ownership role will exercise it efficiently, managing and investing in ways that maximize value (p. 303). Yet judgment about the best path to value creation is idiosyncratic and inalienable, strategies for value creation are not widely understood, and the efficient owner is not automatically matched with the asset. Their headline intuition:

Building on the notion that ownership affords control in resource deployment, we develop the argument that ownership can be exercised with greater or lesser competence and that this matters to value creation.
Foss et al. (2021), p. 303, Introduction

Two framing observations motivate the shift of focus from ownership concentration to ownership identity. First, changing who owns a resource can matter more than changing the concentration of ownership — the value created when Steve Jobs and Apple's owners gained licensed control of resources composed at Xerox, or when Sam Walton's Wal-Mart took over small-town real estate, is hard to attribute to a marginal shift in incentives (p. 304). Second, a footnote presses that agency theory's canonical framing presumes widely dispersed ownership (Jensen & Meckling 1976), whereas the overwhelming majority of firms are sole proprietorships with full owner control and owner involvement in day-to-day management — for such firms "incentive alignment is not really the central challenge" (p. 306, n. 1). Against this backdrop the paper adduces venture-capital research on "smart money": Sorensen (2007) decomposes experienced VCs' value into sorting (investing in better companies) and influence (reconfiguring the resource portfolio), and reads persistence in VC returns as evidence of "heterogeneity in the skills of these investors" (p. 307). Owner identity, the argument runs, has strategic content that incentive-based theories cannot express.

2. Core argument / framework

2.1 The three meanings of ownership and the problem with each

Section 2 of the paper distinguishes, following the legal and economic literature, three conceptualizations of ownership (the paper's Table 1, p. 305; reproduced below). The critique of all three is that each treats ownership as a tool that, deployed "correctly," aligns incentives and maximizes jointly created value. The missing element is that owners and potential owners hold divergent theories about optimal resource configurations, and which theory is empowered through ownership determines outcomes. Because the owner can partition and delegate use rights to managers, boards, and employees, yet cannot fully delegate the ultimate decision of what an asset is for, which rights are delegated and to whom, and whether and when ownership should change, the owner's cognition — judgment, beliefs, cognitive frames — is a strategic variable that incentive theories ignore.

2.2 Four assumptions of the competence perspective

Section 3.1 states the assumptions on which the framework rests. First, owners operate under Knightian uncertainty and vast complexity: not just outcomes, but the set of possible resources and strategies is unknown ex ante, so owners, like entrepreneurs, project beliefs about means–ends relations onto reality. Under genuine uncertainty, control — not contract — is what lets an owner act on judgment; absent uncertainty ownership would be unnecessary because contractual foresight would suffice. Second, factor markets for judgment are "closed": beliefs about value creation are idiosyncratic, often unpersuadable, and most valuable precisely when unique, so their cost of articulation and verification is prohibitive and no market efficiently prices them. Third, ownership is the only sure route to test one's judgment against reality and to capture the value it creates — contracting cannot do this because others disbelieve the theory; ownership allows entry, exit, and rapid re-bundling of assets. Fourth, ownership functions extend beyond income rights to use and transfer rights, which let an owner change course, learn from prior deployments, liquidate, and make the final assessment of the quality of her own judgment.

2.3 The definition and its property-rights foundations

The framework maps classical property-rights economics onto a typology of owner skills. Whenever an individual owns a resource she retains (a) the right to use it, (b) the right to appropriate its returns, and (c) the right to transfer these rights (Alchian 1961; Libecap 1986). Owners vary in the competences with which they deploy each right, giving rise to the paper's central construction:

We call these competences (a) matching competence (knowing what to own), (b) governance competence (knowing how to own), and (c) timing competence (knowing when to own), respectively. We thus define ownership competence as the skills with which asset owners exercise matching, governance, and timing competence (Castanias & Helfat, 1991; Helfat & Peteraf, 2015).
Foss et al. (2021), p. 309, Section 3.2

The mapping is one-to-one: the right to use grounds matching competence (what to own); the right to appropriate grounds governance competence (how to own); and the right to transfer grounds timing competence (when to own) — see Figure 1 and Table 2 of the paper (p. 310) and the reconstruction in Table 2 below. Ownership competence generates economic value through superior skill about what, how, and when to own. The authors align with Alchian's (1961: 63) claim that ownership ability "includes attitude toward risk bearing, knowledge of different people's productive abilities, foresight, and, of course, judgment" (p. 309).

A worked example fixes the logic (pp. 310–311): an actor who foresees a taxi business in her neighborhood buys a suitable car (what to own — matching); she decides whether to drive it herself or hire a chauffeur, and if she hires, how to monitor him and split income (how to own — governance); and she may later sell to a competing taxi firm that sees more value in the business than she does (when to own — timing). The chauffeur exercises derived judgment about running operations; the owner retains the inalienable right to decide whether the taxi business exists at all, who runs it, how profits are split, and when ownership passes to someone else.

2.4 The three competences in detail

Matching competence (what to own). Matching competence is the capacity to foresee, judge, or theorize about valuable resource combinations that serve a specific, intended purpose or solve a unique problem. It requires defining the purpose or problem — the owner's prerogative under the use right — and composing mental representations that guide search for valuable (re)combinations of owned and others' resources. Because resources have multiple attributes and the space of combinations is vast, experimentation, thought experiment, and theorizing substitute for ex ante specification (the paper connects this to Demsetz's observation that the set of ownable rights is not fully describable, Demsetz 1988: 19). Matching competence is what lets owners overcome "functional fixedness," recognize underpriced resources, and define the boundary of the firm by deciding what to own. In the VC context it corresponds to picking winners (Sorensen 2007; Baum & Silverman 2004); private-equity investors retain rights over portfolio-company focus, structure, and boundaries precisely to exercise it (p. 312).

Governance competence (how to own). Governance competence is the capacity to compose an envisioned resource bundle through effective governance and organization design. It is the competence closest to "getting the incentives right," yet it, too, is a skill: selecting managers and aligning managerial skill with resource-configuration requirements; choosing between self-management and delegation under asymmetric information; designing rent-sharing, payout, incentive, and monitoring regimes (behavioral versus outcome-based systems, motivation-crowding awareness); calibrating incentives to the owner's own risk profile; and shaping relations to reassure stakeholders who make relationship-specific investments against hold-up. The paper echoes Williamson (1996: 9) — parties "look ahead, perceive hazards, and factor these back into the contractual relation" — and adds that competent owners also protect themselves against hold-up when they commit resources to partners (p. 313, n. 6).

Timing competence (when to own). Timing competence is the skill of timing investment into and out of projects, assets, and ownership itself to maximize value creation. It has three components. First, market timing: acquiring undervalued and selling overvalued resources — issuing equity when market values are high and repurchasing when they are low (Baker & Wurgler 2002), taking companies public near market peaks (Lerner 1994). Second, staging: spreading investments across time so that value is progressively revealed, which limits downside risk and generates real-option-style strategic flexibility — the right, not the obligation, to act later. Third, succession and exit planning: judging whether one is still the "best owner" (Dobbs, Huyett & Koller 2009). Because competitive forces and technological change can render a current owner's competences obsolete, a timing-competent owner recognizes when another owner has superior matching competence or complementary resources, accepts a lump-sum price above the asset's value to herself, and plans her own exit (pp. 314–315). Timing competence thus operationalizes the comparative-capabilities logic of firm boundaries (Argyres & Zenger 2012; Jacobides & Winter 2005; Poppo & Zenger 1998).

2.5 Distinctiveness, learning, and context

The three competences are distinct and separable: owners can be strong in one and weak in others. Founder-owners with brilliant matching competence often stay at the helm too long (weak timing); family firms may suffer in governance (attracting nonfamily managers) and timing (transgenerational control intentions bias subjective valuation upward) while excelling in matching through industry knowledge and networks (p. 315); corporate raiders (T. Boone Pickens, KKR) and activist investors (Nelson Peltz) monetize timing and matching competences respectively; Warren Buffett is presented as an exemplar especially strong in timing (p. 316). Ownership competences also appear learnable: experience within industries, through governing, and from repeated asset transfers improves them, and serial entrepreneurs improve with each firm founded and owned (Gompers et al. 2006; Lafontaine & Shaw 2016, p. 316).

The value of ownership competence is contextual. Ownership concentration is recast as a moderator, not a cause: concentrated ownership amplifies the effects of competent owners and dampens incompetent ones, and concentration's real role is granting control to owners selected for competence (Feldman & Montgomery 2015 find that directors who own large shareholdings but lack top-level experience are negatively associated with firm value, p. 316). Over the firm life cycle, matching competence dominates at start-up (assembling the initial resource configuration that fits the founder's vision), governance competence moves to the foreground with growth and professionalization, and timing competence matters most as the firm matures and must shed, acquire, and re-time assets. Under environmental uncertainty, ownership competence becomes more valuable than incentive alignment: optimal owner–asset matches are hard to discern ex ante, controlled experiments are impossible, and idiosyncratic judgment substitutes for rule-based search — "the competence view should dominate the incentive view of ownership when the boundary choice about what to own and not to own takes place in an uncertain environment where efficiency is less germane" (p. 318). Finally, factor-market efficiency bounds the theory: if asset markets perfectly priced resources at their best conceivable use, ownership competence could add no value; it creates value precisely in disequilibrium, where resources sit in the wrong hands (p. 318).

3. Evidence / examples

This is a conceptual paper, so its "evidence" consists of thought examples, worked cases, and empirical results adduced from prior research to illustrate rather than to test the framework. Three devices carry the argument.

Worked example. The taxi-business example (pp. 310–311) walks through all three competences in a single decision sequence — buying the car (matching), hiring and monitoring a chauffeur and splitting income (governance), selling the business to a competitor who values it more (timing) — and uses it to separate the owner's original judgment from the manager's derived judgment.

Historical and biographical cases. The paper uses ownership changes whose value effects resist an incentive-only reading: the Xerox resources composed by Steve Jobs and Apple's owners; Sam Walton's Wal-Mart acquiring small-town real estate (p. 304); the unbundling and redistribution of "inefficient corporate portfolios" by corporate raiders; activist investors taking minority stakes to impose superior matching judgments; and Warren Buffett as the archetype of timing competence (pp. 315–316). These are presented as plausibility cases, not systematic evidence.

Empirical results borrowed from adjacent literatures. The paper leans on several bodies of findings: (i) VC "smart money" research — Sorensen's (2007) distinction between sorting and influence and the persistence of VC returns as evidence of investor-skill heterogeneity, plus Hsu's (2004) finding that high-reputation VCs acquire start-up equity at a 10–14% discount (p. 312, n. 5); (ii) corporate-governance and ownership-structure studies — Thomsen & Pedersen (2000) showing that owner identity (founding family, banks, institutions, other companies, governments) affects profit goals, dividends, capital structure, and growth over and above ownership concentration (p. 307), and Feldman & Montgomery (2015) on large shareholdings held by directors lacking top-level experience (p. 316); (iii) finance results on timing — Baker & Wurgler (2002) on equity issuance and repurchase around market values, Lerner (1994) on seasoned VCs taking companies public near market peaks (p. 314); (iv) family-business research on governance and timing weaknesses and matching strengths (Neckebrouck et al. 2018; Zellweger et al. 2012); and (v) serial-entrepreneurship evidence that ownership competence is learnable through repeated founding and owning (Gompers et al. 2006; Lafontaine & Shaw 2016, p. 316).

Testable predictions instead of tests. The Discussion converts the framework into new predictions (Section 5.2): two owners with identical stakes in otherwise identical firms will make different decisions because they differ in competence; ownership concentration moderates the competence–performance relationship; incompetent owners are a distinct source of value destruction (misallocating well-intentioned projects, keeping assets that should be sold) beyond poor incentives or minority-shareholder expropriation; incompetent owners invite managerial mischief and can demotivate competent managers into withholding effort or exiting — moral hazard "not a consequence of opportunism as predicted by agency theory, but a consequence of frustration and demotivation enabled by low levels of ownership competence" (p. 321); and the market for corporate control sorts assets to their most competent owners, not merely to the best managers. These are stated as hypotheses to be tested, and Section 5.3 sketches measurement paths (competence-measurement techniques à la Danneels 2016; refined measures of ownership change such as asset purchases and sales, acquisitions and divestitures, reorganizations and restructurings). No original data are presented.

4. Position in the literature

Within the Week-3 property-rights arc, the paper sits at the fourth node — the capability/competence view — between the strategic-management translation of property-rights economics (Kim & Mahoney 2005; Foss & Foss 2005) and the empirical test that closes the arc. Its distinctive move is to relocate the action of modern property-rights theory. Grossman & Hart (1986) and Hart & Moore (1990) treat residual control rights as an ex-ante investment-incentive device: ownership is allocated to the party whose relationship-specific investment matters most, and complementarity of assets justifies common ownership. Foss et al. accept the machinery of residual rights but reject the implicit assumption that whoever owns will exercise control skillfully. Ownership matters, in their hands, not only because it protects incentives but because it empowers judgment: the right to compose resources according to idiosyncratic, inalienable beliefs about value creation. The paper is thus a deliberate critique-and-extension of Grossman & Hart (1986), Hart & Moore (1990), and Hart (1995) — the formal residual-control-rights branch — in the direction of classical property-rights economics (Alchian 1961, 1965; Demsetz 1964, 1967; Barzel 1997) and of the Austrian/judgment tradition (Knight 1921; Foss & Klein 2012; Foss, Klein & Bjørnskov 2019).

The paper also integrates several non-PRT strategy lines rather than rebutting them. From Penrose (1959) and the resource-based view it takes the idea that resource combinations and capabilities are central to value creation, but argues that most RBV and dynamic-capability work says little about when ownership (rather than renting or contracting) is required to assemble such bundles — ownership competence "can be understood as a set of higher-level competences situated at the level of the owner" (p. 320). From managerial-capability research (Castanias & Helfat 1991; Helfat & Peteraf 2015; Sirmon, Hitt & Ireland 2007) it borrows the skill vocabulary and the notion that competences are heterogeneous, learnable, and seated in individuals; from the entrepreneurial-judgment and theory-based-view literature (Felin & Zenger 2009, 2017; Benner & Zenger 2016) it takes the claim that actors are "theorists" whose divergent beliefs about value creation are the real scarce resource. Positioning, transaction-cost economics, strategic factor markets, and added-value logic (Porter 1985; Williamson 1985; Barney 1986; Brandenburger & Stuart 1996) are each revisited in Section 5.1 and shown to presuppose — without articulating — an actor with matching, governance, or timing competence. The construct is also distinguished from board competence, and the market for corporate control is reinterpreted as a sorting device for owners.

The paper names its own lineage explicitly: it follows Alchian's (1961: 63) dictum that "people differ in their talents as owners" and credits Pelikan (1993) with having likely first coined "ownership competence" in the context of comparative political systems — free markets are needed precisely because a central planner cannot assess or allocate such competence (p. 309, n. 2). Foss and Lien (2010) and Foss & Klein (2012) supply the judgment-based groundwork. Where Foss & Klein (2012) treat entrepreneurial judgment as decision-making about resource allocation under uncertainty, the present paper sharpens the unit of analysis: matching competence is "the accuracy with which entrepreneurial judgment is exercised" (p. 312, n. 4). In the course arc this note is the theoretical bridge from modern PRT to the empirical test of ownership-related hypotheses that follows.

5. Limitations

5(a) The authors' own stated limitations

  • The paper says little about the antecedents of ownership competence: "We have said little about the antecedents of ownership competence" (p. 322). Evidence from serial entrepreneurs suggests experience and decision frequency matter, but the paper offers no theory of where competence comes from or how it develops.
  • Measurement is acknowledged as open. The authors propose using techniques developed for other competences (Danneels 2016; Grant & Verona 2015; Stadler, Helfat & Verona 2013) and refined ownership-change measures, but no instrument exists yet, and the contingency effects (concentration, life cycle, uncertainty, factor-market efficiency) "are worth studying" rather than established (p. 322).
  • Ownership competence sits at the level of the owner, not the asset, so owners may be good owners of some assets and poor owners of others (effective entrepreneurs can be poor investors in financial assets; Zellweger 2017). How competence aggregates across a portfolio of assets is left open.
  • The predictions about the market for corporate control (sorting assets to best owners) and about the relative importance of the three competences are stated as hypotheses "rife for empirical testing" without direct measures (p. 322).
  • The framework brackets purpose: owners may hold assets for purposes beyond value creation for owners, so assessing competence should be filtered through the purpose for which assets are held (Henderson & Serafeim 2020) — noted, not developed.

5(b) Independent judgment

  • [paper claim] The central premise — that agency, incomplete-contracting, and property-rights theories treat owners as homogeneous and thus miss skill — is the paper's own characterization (pp. 303–304). The claim is fair to the formal GHM tradition but somewhat less fair to classical property-rights economics, which already contains the competence seed in Alchian's "talents as owners" remark that the paper itself quotes.
  • [evidence] The supporting empirical material (VC persistence, Thomsen & Pedersen 2000, Feldman & Montgomery 2015, serial-entrepreneur results) is borrowed from adjacent fields and is illustrative. Feldman & Montgomery (2015) concerns directors with large shareholdings but limited top-level experience — an indirect proxy for ownership competence, not a direct measure.
  • [inference] There is a latent circularity risk: if ownership competence is inferred from realized value creation, then "competent owners create value" risks becoming definitionally true. The authors' proposed measures (ownership changes, serial success) mitigate but do not eliminate this, since competence and favorable selection into opportunities are hard to separate ex ante. This concern is not addressed in the paper.
  • [inference] The boundary between governance competence and ordinary managerial capability is thinner than the paper suggests. Because the owner can delegate governance design to hired managers and boards, much of what the paper calls governance competence may be an owner-level judgment about whom to delegate to, which then shades back into matching and timing — the three dimensions may be less separable operationally than they are conceptually.
  • [open question] Whether ownership competence is an individual trait, an owner-group property, or a firm-level organizational capability is unresolved; the paper fluctuates between "the owner," founding teams, and ownership groups, and Section 5.3 concedes that aggregation across portfolios is unstudied.
  • [open question] The theory's predictive content depends on competences being scarce, heterogeneous, and non-contractible. Where factor markets are (nearly) efficient — large public firms with liquid markets, powerful intermediaries, and sophisticated boards — the domain where ownership competence adds value may be much narrower than the paper's rhetoric suggests; the paper itself concedes the efficiency bound but does not quantify where real economies sit on it.
  • [inference] If timing competence includes selling to a superior owner and matching competence includes recognizing undervalued bundles, the framework implies that owners who lack competence should often be unable to recognize that fact — the authors acknowledge this ("ownership incompetence often engenders persistent foregone opportunities," p. 321) but the implication for how markets correct the problem (the takeover mechanism depends on someone competent buying, not on the incompetent seller noticing) deserves sharper treatment.

6. Conclusion

The paper establishes a competence view of ownership as a complement — and partial corrective — to the incentive view that dominates agency and incomplete-contracting theory. Its durable contributions are the construct of ownership competence, defined as the skill with which ownership is exercised to create value; its decomposition into matching (what to own), governance (how to own), and timing (when to own) competence, each rooted in one of the three classical property rights; the demonstration that heterogeneous owner skill — not just ownership concentration or incentive intensity — drives value creation; and a research agenda spanning measurement, moderators, life-cycle dynamics, and the reinterpretation of the market for corporate control as a market sorting assets to their most competent owners. The closing statement of the paper carries the one-line normative takeaway:

We stress that value is created when those who possess competence also assume ownership.
Foss et al. (2021), p. 323, Conclusion

The strategic takeaway for managers and investors is direct: ownership is a job with skill requirements, so build governance around selecting and retaining owners who are competent to own — and change who owns when the competence match deteriorates, as much as you would change managers when their skills no longer fit.
