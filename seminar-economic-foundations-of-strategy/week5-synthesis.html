1. A map of the explanatory questions

The week contains several connected questions. Penrose explains how productive services and opportunities develop. Wernerfelt examines resource positions across product markets. Barney identifies conditions under which advantages resist competitive duplication. Kogut–Zander and Grant explain productive knowledge and its organization. Nelson–Winter explain continuity and change through routines. Teece, Pisano and Shuen ask how firms renew competences under changing conditions. Treating these as successive answers to one identical question would hide their different outcomes, units and assumptions. This organization is our synthesis, grounded in the seven assigned readings.

2. Antecedents: several starting problems

Judgment and economic change. Knight links uncertainty to judgment and responsible control. Schumpeter explains development through new combinations. They supply different starting points: who makes consequential judgments, and how productive activity is recombined. The Knight–property-rights connection here is a comparison proposed for the seminar. Schumpeter is explicitly engaged by Nelson–Winter, Kogut–Zander and Teece et al.; the relevant passages are listed below.

Dispersed knowledge and bounded choice. Hayek asks how knowledge dispersed across people can become usable in economic coordination. Simon develops choice under cognitive limits. These works help specify a coordination problem that extends beyond incentive alignment. Hayek’s societal coordination question and Grant’s organizational integration question can be compared without presuming they recommend the same institutional solution. Nickerson and Zenger explicitly engage Hayek when developing a problem-solving account of governance.

Selection, skills and organization. Alchian analyzes selection under uncertainty. Nelson and Winter develop organizational foundations based on skills, routines, memory and conflict. Their chapter expressly acknowledges Polanyi’s influence and the organization-theory tradition. Polanyi, March–Simon and Cyert–March are visible in the genealogy through the core authors’ own acknowledgments; this edition does not count separately unread original books as additional library records.

Growth, competence and coordinated industry. Penrose explains the development of productive services. Rubin examines the allocation of experienced resources between production and expansion. Richardson distinguishes similar activities, which require similar capabilities, from complementary activities, which require coordination. This distinction creates room for cooperation among specialized firms. Richardson explicitly acknowledges Penrose and describes his analysis as compatible with Coase’s coordination-cost logic. His article is a particularly useful bridge into the earlier weeks.

Earlier strategy and organization scholarship. The historical accounts in Mahoney and Pandian and Conner locate RBV alongside distinctive-competence and industrial-organization traditions. These are secondary reconstructions of ancestry. They warrant a label such as “historical account of influence”; they do not substitute for reading every earlier text cited in those reconstructions.

4. Debates that deserve separate treatment

Acquisition, accumulation and the price of advantage

Barney (1986) asks whether expected resource returns are already incorporated in acquisition prices. Dierickx and Cool examine assets accumulated through flows of investment, with time compression, connected stocks and causal ambiguity affecting imitation. Internal development can make assets difficult to copy, while competition over accumulation can still absorb prospective rents. Peteraf supplies a useful organization of rent conditions. The comparison should distinguish investment expenditure, accumulated capacity and the cost of obtaining the capacity.

Resource value and testability

Priem and Butler question explanatory content, the treatment of value and empirical testability. Barney’s response belongs beside the critique; its reading scope remains explicit in the library. Kraaijenbrink and colleagues distinguish several kinds of criticism and discuss capacity, action and resource rights. Our methodological implication is to specify the resource, its use, its expected contribution and the competitive environment before examining performance. The current selected readings support a map of the dispute; they do not justify announcing that one side has conclusively won.

Individual knowledge, social knowledge and the reason for a firm

Grant foregrounds individual knowledge and application; Kogut and Zander emphasize enduring organizing principles, transfer and creation. Conner and Prahalad deliberately bracket opportunism to investigate knowledge substitution. Nickerson and Zenger include knowledge-exchange and search hazards when comparing governance. These are different KBV arguments. A general statement that “KBV ignores incentives” would fail to represent this range.

Renewal, ordinary capabilities and costly change

Teece et al., Eisenhardt and Martin and Winter differ in emphasis and scope. Winter distinguishes ordinary capabilities, higher-order learned capabilities and ad hoc problem solving, with economic costs relevant to the choice among them. Helfat and Peteraf describe capability development and branching over a lifecycle. Peteraf et al. make the disagreements explicit and offer conditional reconciliation. A change event alone does not establish the presence of a recurring dynamic capability.

Competitive advantage and who receives the returns

Coff links capability development with changing stakeholder bargaining positions. Han and Tong examine how knowledge use can be separated from ownership when protection supports exchange. These arguments connect productive services to rights, contracts and appropriation. Our synthesis is to follow value through four distinct steps: creation, competitive protection, exchange and distribution. Firm profitability records only part of that chain.

Governance today and capabilities tomorrow

Argyres and Zenger caution that observed capability differences reflect governance and organizational histories. Jacobides and Winter examine the coevolution of capabilities and transaction costs at the industry level. These arguments complicate a regression that treats capability and governance as independent explanations. Past integration may build a capability that later helps explain a boundary choice; outsourcing may also change future learning opportunities.

7. How to read the empirical evidence

Publication counts measure coverage, not independent replications. Several papers study related firms, projects or settings. The Hansen and Haas–Hansen studies in particular should be treated as connected research programs until sample overlap is checked. Reviews, meta-analysis and simulations are counted separately from original empirical articles.

Most original studies here are observational. Historical cases help trace processes and rejected alternatives but cannot estimate their prevalence in a population. Surveys and panels can compare firms or projects, while capability investment, governance choice and performance may remain jointly determined. Chondrakis et al. offer a difference-in-differences design based on changing coordination requirements in patent prosecution; its interpretation remains conditional on the exposure and parallel-trend assumptions. Leiblein et al. explicitly address governance selection, making it useful to compare adjusted and unadjusted results.

Newbert’s review and Crook et al.’s meta-analysis answer different synthesis questions. Support across hypothesis tests and an average resource–performance association are not equivalent evidence about the full causal sequence. The meta-analysis reports a positive corrected association across its included studies; it does not independently resolve reverse causality, resource measurement or rent appropriation.

8. Reading priorities before choosing the integration narrative

  1. Deepen the three individual presentations. Use the full assigned-text guides for Penrose, Wernerfelt and Barney. Keep growth, resource strategy and sustained advantage distinct, then explain their connections.
  2. Reconstruct the strongest theoretical disagreements. Pair Priem–Butler with Barney’s response; compare Kogut–Zander with Grant; compare Teece et al., Eisenhardt–Martin and Winter. Complete any abstract-only reading before adjudicating detailed arguments.
  3. Choose evidence by the proposition it tests. For measurement, start with Henderson–Cockburn, Ray et al. and Ethiraj et al. For mixed capability effects, use Drnevich–Kriauciunas and Macher. For governance, compare Leiblein et al. with Chondrakis et al.
  4. Develop two or three cross-week mechanisms. Richardson connects capability similarity to coordination; Nelson–Winter connect memory with conflict; Han–Tong and Coff connect knowledge use with protection and appropriation. Existing Week 2–4 pages supply the other side of each comparison.
  5. Retain unresolved alternatives. A future slide narrative can be organized around growth, knowledge coordination, renewal or governance. This library preserves material for all four; it does not impose a presentation-duration filter.

9. Scope and source discipline

This is a relevance-screened seminar collection, assembled through local course files, the ORICO full-text corpus, available Zotero/local material and public source searches. It is not a systematic review with a preregistered search protocol, an exhaustive citation network or a meta-analysis. Antecedent and later-development links were checked in the passages named above; conceptual similarities are labeled as comparisons.

Availability and reading depth are separate fields. A PDF in the collection does not imply that every section was read. The seven assigned works were read in full within the assigned scope, including only the specified chapters of the two books. Extension records identify selected sections or abstract coverage, and the next useful reading step. Source metadata, inclusion reasons, acquisition outcomes and deduplication checks are retained with the research files.

AI assistance was used for local retrieval, source inspection, drafting, organization and website production. The summaries distinguish author claims, empirical findings and our comparisons. The prose review focused on evidence scope, attribution and clarity. Original papers remain the authority for quotations, numerical estimates and final presentation claims.

Wesley M. Cohen; Daniel A. Levinthal (1990)

Absorptive Capacity: A New Perspective on Learning and Innovation

Empirical · quantitative

Setting. 1,719 business units from 318 firms in 151 lines of business.

Design. Cross-sectional linkage of R&D-manager technological-opportunity surveys with FTC Line of Business data.

Measures. Business-unit R&D intensity, industry knowledge sources, appropriability and technological opportunity.

Mechanism. Why does prior related knowledge affect a firm’s ability to benefit from external technological information?

Results. Patterns of R&D investment were consistent with an absorptive role for internal research.

Inference. Indirect empirical assessment of a learning mechanism; not direct causal estimation of absorptive capacity.

Alternatives. Unobserved innovative opportunity, coarse industry knowledge measures and endogenous R&D investment.

Classroom use. Use to explain why equally available information produces unequal learning and to distinguish access from assimilation.

Dennis Epple; Linda Argote; Rukmini Devadas (1991)

Organizational Learning Curves: A Method for Investigating Intra-Plant Transfer of Knowledge Acquired Through Learning by Doing

Empirical · quantitative

Setting. Production observations from an automobile assembly plant before and after addition of a second shift.

Design. Generalized learning-curve estimation with tests of complete carryover, cross-shift transfer and retention restrictions.

Measures. Output, labor and production inputs, accumulated experience, shift-specific and shared knowledge parameters.

Mechanism. Is production knowledge fully embodied in equipment, or does some of it remain associated with people and organizational experience?

Results. Complete transfer was rejected; substantial knowledge carried over, with incomplete later cross-shift transfer.

Inference. A discriminating observational test against complete technological embodiment.

Alternatives. Model misspecification, workforce differences and contemporaneous production changes.

Classroom use. Use as a concrete test of where organizational knowledge resides, with a clear prediction that could be rejected.

Dorothy Leonard-Barton (1992)

Core Capabilities and Core Rigidities: A Paradox in Managing New Product Development

Empirical · qualitative

Setting. Twenty product/process projects across Ford, Chaparral Steel, Hewlett-Packard and two anonymous firms.

Design. Comparative qualitative cases; cross-team analysis, interviews, site visits and member checks.

Measures. Four capability dimensions and their alignment with project requirements.

Mechanism. How can the same core capability support successful development and obstruct a project requiring new knowledge?

Results. The same interdependent knowledge system both enables projects and constrains unfamiliar requirements.

Inference. Inductive mechanism inference; not a universal causal performance relationship.

Alternatives. Project differences, retrospective accounts, company participation and mixed definitions of project success.

Classroom use. Use to explain the mechanism behind capability rigidity before presenting later statistical evidence on adaptation and innovation.

Rebecca Henderson; Iain Cockburn (1994)

Measuring Competence? Exploring Firm Effects in Pharmaceutical Research

Empirical · mixed methods

Setting. Ten major European and U.S. pharmaceutical firms; research-program analysis focused on 1975–1988.

Design. Program-level patent-output models combined with qualitative organizational histories.

Measures. Research investment, prior patents, portfolio scope, publication incentives and resource-allocation structure.

Mechanism. Can measured organizational competences explain research productivity beyond firm size, portfolio composition and unexplained firm effects?

Results. Some architectural practices and prior success associate with output; portfolio and firm effects complicate interpretation.

Inference. Associations consistent with competence, explicitly not a convincing separation from all unobserved heterogeneity.

Alternatives. Scientist quality, scientific opportunities, patent propensity, agency problems and research-portfolio composition.

Classroom use. Use as a canonical attempt to open the firm-effect black box, with its own measurement cautions retained.

Udo Zander; Bruce Kogut (1995)

Knowledge and the Speed of the Transfer and Imitation of Organizational Capabilities: An Empirical Test

Empirical · quantitative

Setting. 35 major Swedish innovations analyzed; the questionnaire initially targeted 44 innovations associated with 20 firms.

Design. Observational analysis of time to capability transfer and product imitation.

Measures. Codifiability, teachability, complexity, employee turnover, continuing development and transfer/imitation timing.

Mechanism. Do the knowledge characteristics that facilitate internal replication also make a capability easier for competitors to imitate?

Results. Knowledge codification and teachability accelerated transfer; imitation effects were qualified and influenced by mobility and continued development.

Inference. Evidence links knowledge attributes to transfer hazards without experimentally assigning those attributes.

Alternatives. Recall error, selected successful innovations, product/manufacturing outcome mismatch and omitted protection mechanisms.

Classroom use. Use to make causal ambiguity and transferability concrete, and to test the simple proposition that anything transferable internally must be equally easy to imitate externally.

Danny Miller; Jamal Shamsie (1996)

The Resource-Based View of the Firm in Two Environments: The Hollywood Film Studios from 1936 to 1965

Empirical · quantitative

Setting. Seven major Hollywood studios, 1936–1965; RKO excluded because it ceased operations before the end.

Design. Historical autoregressive/heteroscedastic regressions separated by era.

Measures. Star contracts, theater control, Academy Awards and prior production costs; ROS, profits and share.

Mechanism. When do property-based resources and knowledge-based resources contribute to performance under different environmental conditions?

Results. Property-based resources stronger in stable era; knowledge-based indicators stronger in uncertain era.

Inference. Historical conditional association, not an isolated causal effect of uncertainty.

Alternatives. Other era changes, survival selection, legal change and imperfect archival proxies.

Classroom use. Use as the central empirical illustration that valuable resources must be specified relative to environmental conditions.

Gabriel Szulanski (1996)

Exploring Internal Stickiness: Impediments to the Transfer of Best Practice Within the Firm

Empirical · quantitative

Setting. 271 questionnaires concerning 122 transfers of 38 practices across eight companies.

Design. Two-stage survey with source, recipient and third-party reports; canonical correlation analysis.

Measures. Transfer stickiness by stage; absorptive capacity, causal ambiguity, relational difficulty and motivational barriers.

Mechanism. Why are superior practices difficult to transfer between units that belong to the same firm?

Results. Largest reported barriers were recipient absorption, causal ambiguity and arduous relationships.

Inference. Associational comparison of barriers; canonical weights do not yield randomized causal effects.

Alternatives. Selected transfer histories, respondent recall, measurement overlap and unstable smaller-sample estimates.

Classroom use. Use as core empirical evidence that internal replication remains costly, and as a bridge between sustained advantage and the difficulty of scaling it.

Jeffrey H. Dyer (1996)

SPECIALIZED SUPPLIER NETWORKS AS A SOURCE OF COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE: EVIDENCE FROM THE AUTO INDUSTRY

Empirical · quantitative

Setting. Two Japanese and three U.S. automakers plus selected partner and arm’s-length suppliers.

Design. Comparative surveys/interviews, descriptive network plots, selected regressions and rank correlations.

Measures. Plant proximity; nonredeployable assets; face-to-face contact; quality, inventory, development cycle and ROA.

Mechanism. Can specialized relationships between separate firms create competitive advantage, and which partners capture the resulting gains?

Results. Some specificity dimensions associate with better network performance; physical specificity–quality prediction is unsupported.

Inference. Suggestive observational comparisons; five-producer outcomes do not support strong causal claims.

Alternatives. National institutions, trust, selection of supplier ties, product quality and bargaining/appropriation.

Classroom use. Use to connect value creation through specialization with appropriation and governance, while showing the limits of small-network comparisons.

Nicholas Argyres (1996)

CAPABILITIES, TECHNOLOGICAL DIVERSIFICATION AND DIVISIONALIZATION

Empirical · quantitative

Setting. 106 large U.S. multidivisional firms; 1975 structure and 1974–1981 patent information.

Design. Cross-sectional OLS models with sector and diversification controls.

Measures. Patent technological diversity; divisions/subsidiaries relative to revenues; sector and strategic diversification.

Mechanism. How is technological diversification related to the internal division structure of a multidivisional firm?

Results. Negative diversity–divisionalization association, conditioned by sector’s technological progressiveness.

Inference. Associational organization–strategy fit; causal direction is explicitly ambiguous.

Alternatives. Simultaneity, patenting propensities, size scaling and unobserved internal decision rights.

Classroom use. Use as a bridge from knowledge complementarities to internal organizational design and the limits of highly autonomous divisions.

Steven Maijoor; Arjen Van Witteloostuijn (1996)

AN EMPIRICAL TEST OF THE RESOURCE-BASED THEORY: STRATEGIC REGULATION IN THE DUTCH AUDIT INDUSTRY

Empirical · quantitative

Setting. Dutch audit profession in the postwar period; membership histories and selected income years.

Design. Historical longitudinal industry analysis; comparisons of regulatory and nonregulatory periods.

Measures. Disclosed accounts, qualified employees and partners, concentration, promotions and professional income.

Mechanism. How can regulation make professional human capital valuable and protect the rents attached to it?

Results. Regulation enlarges demand; professional and partnership constraints help protect scarce positions.

Inference. Historical and statistical association; no isolated regulatory treatment estimate.

Alternatives. Secular demand growth, technology/productivity changes and limited income data.

Classroom use. Use to ask where valuable, scarce resources come from and how institutional rules protect or distribute their returns.

Constance E. Helfat (1997)

Know-how and asset complementarity and dynamic capability accumulation: the case of r&d

Empirical · quantitative

Setting. 26 major U.S. energy firms, 1976–1981.

Design. Tobit regressions; lagged regressors; alternative lagged-dependent-variable and firm-effect specifications.

Measures. Coal-conversion R&D/sales; refining R&D capital; complementary physical assets; oil prices.

Mechanism. How do existing technological knowledge and complementary physical assets shape investment in new capabilities after an environmental change?

Results. Related knowledge predicts coal-conversion R&D; some complementary-asset coefficients depend on specification.

Inference. Association in capability investment, not causal evidence of performance advantage.

Alternatives. Oil prices, firm size, firm effects, prior spending and correlated knowledge stocks are considered.

Classroom use. Use to explain where dynamic capabilities come from and why resource complementarities remain contingent on market conditions.

Thomas C. Powell; Anne Dent-Micallef (1997)

Information technology as competitive advantage: the role of human, business, and technology resources

Empirical · quantitative

Setting. 65 retail firms; home-office responses with remote store-manager validation.

Design. Cross-sectional resource-performance regressions supplemented by interviews and interrater checks.

Measures. Human/business resources, in-store and beyond-store IT, financial and IT performance.

Mechanism. Why do firms using similar information technologies achieve different performance, and can IT alone explain advantage?

Results. Human complements predict performance; IT alone is nonsignificant; business effects are weaker with human resources controlled.

Inference. Association only; authors explicitly state sustainability and causal direction are not established.

Alternatives. Reverse causality, common-method perceptions, industry specificity and technology diffusion.

Classroom use. Use as a concrete case showing that buying a visible resource may not reproduce the organizational system behind successful use.

Five debates to organize the literature

1. Acquisition versus accumulation. Barney directs attention to the price paid for strategic resources: expected product-market returns can be capitalized in acquisition costs. Dierickx and Cool examine assets that must be built through time. Their stock-flow distinction makes the pace, history, and interdependence of investment central to imitation. Their footnote 2 accepts that competition can operate over accumulation as well, preventing a simple inference that internal development automatically generates rents. Read Barney 1986, publisher abstract, with Dierickx and Cool 1989, pp. 1504–1506 and 1509–1510. A useful research question is whether the explanatory variable measures current expenditure, accumulated capacity, or the cost of acquiring that capacity.

2. Resource value, testability, and useful explanation. Priem and Butler ask how resource value is explained and how resource-based claims gain empirical content. Barney’s direct response must accompany this critique; this library currently has only his primary abstract, so it does not adjudicate the complete logical exchange. Kraaijenbrink and colleagues provide a later assessment that distinguishes relatively manageable specification problems from deeper problems concerning resources, value, and conditions for sustained advantage. Their proposed distinction between capacity and action supports process studies and more precise treatment of ownership. Sources: Priem and Butler 2001, pp. 22–24 and 36; Barney 2001, abstract; Kraaijenbrink et al. author manuscript, pp. 5–6, 20–22. Our methodological implication is to specify resource measures, deployment processes, and outcomes separately before interpreting success.

3. Average empirical association versus a test of the full theory. Newbert finds modest support that varies by the variables and theoretical approaches tested. Crook and colleagues report a corrected resource–performance correlation of .22 across 125 studies and more than 29,000 organizations. They also find stronger associations for resource measures meeting theoretical criteria. These assessments use different synthesis methods and inclusion decisions. Test counting asks how often specified hypotheses receive support; meta-analysis estimates relationships across studies. The latter’s positive association does not establish the complete causal sequence or eliminate reverse causality. Sources: Newbert 2007, abstract and conclusion; Crook et al. 2008, abstract, Results, and opening Discussion. Their conclusions should be compared with attention to measurement and design rather than treated as competing votes.

4. Firm resources versus resources embedded in relationships. Dyer and Singh locate some rent-generating combinations across firm boundaries. Teece’s multiproduct argument asks why gains from shared use require internal organization instead of contracts. Together they encourage a distinction between the productive benefits of combining resources and the governance arrangement that realizes those benefits. Sources: Dyer and Singh 1998, abstract; Teece 1982, pp. 39–40 and 44–45. Our comparison connects Week 5 with transaction-cost and property-rights questions: locate the productive dependency, then explain coordination, control, and division of returns.

5. Capability renewal, context, and levels of explanation. Peteraf and colleagues show that foundational dynamic-capability formulations contain genuine differences, including the conditions under which common practices can contribute to sustained advantage. Their reconciliation is conditional. Zott’s simulation supplies model-generated mechanisms involving timing, cost, and learning; it is not an observed-firm causal test. Sirmon and colleagues specify managerial processes, while Ployhart and Moliterno ask how individual attributes become a unit-level resource. Sources: Peteraf et al. 2013, introduction and conclusion; Zott 2003, abstract and introduction; Sirmon et al. 2007, pp. 273–275; Ployhart and Moliterno 2011, abstract. Our synthesis separates individual capacity, managerial action, organizational routines, and firm performance so that a mechanism at one level is not silently inferred from another.

Five mechanism comparisons

1. Productive coordination and safeguards explain different parts of organization

Conner and Prahalad deliberately remove opportunism while retaining bounded rationality and different individual knowledge. Their question is whether organizational mode changes the knowledge applied to production. This yields a direct comparison with Week 2: if purposeful cheating is absent, does authority still change productive possibilities? Their answer depends on knowledge substitution and the relative costs of employment and contracting. The absence of opportunism is an analytical assumption, not a finding about real firms. Conner and Prahalad, 1996, abstract, pp. 477–478 and knowledge-substitution discussion.

Nickerson and Zenger change the unit of analysis to the problem and the central task to generating a solution. Complexity affects whether search requires shared knowledge and coordination. Their knowledge-appropriation and strategic-search hazards reconnect cognition with self-interest. This makes the article useful after Conner and Prahalad: different KBV accounts do not all make the same claim about incentives. Nickerson and Zenger, 2004, pp. 617–619, problem complexity, knowledge-exchange hazards and governance propositions.

Richardson supplies an earlier alternative vocabulary. Similarity concerns capabilities; complementarity concerns the need to coordinate activities. Closely complementary but dissimilar activities can require cooperation between specialists. The final footnote explicitly presents this account as compatible with Coase’s relative coordination-cost logic. His licensing discussion also considers assurances against subsequent exploitation, so describing this article as ignoring all incentive problems would misrepresent it. Richardson, 1972, pp. 888–892, 893–896.

Seminar question: Which observation would distinguish a cognitive-coordination explanation from a safeguard explanation, and which observation would be consistent with both?

2. Access, assimilation and transfer are separate steps

Cohen and Levinthal explain why access to information does not imply an ability to use it: prior related knowledge affects recognition, assimilation and application. Lane and Lubatkin make this relationship-specific. The same firm may learn more readily from a compatible partner than from an equally knowledgeable but organizationally distant one. This shifts attention from the quantity of R&D to the match between the knowledge and arrangements of the two parties. Cohen and Levinthal, 1990, conceptual development and pp. 142–143; Lane and Lubatkin, 1998, pp. 461–462, measures and results.

Hansen separates discovery from transfer: weak ties can lower search costs, while transferring complex knowledge requires more intensive interaction. Reagans and McEvily add network cohesion and range, which concern motivation and the ability to communicate across knowledge differences. These mechanisms make different predictions even when the legal firm boundary is fixed. Hansen, 1999, pp. 82, 105–106; Reagans and McEvily, 2003, pp. 240, 248–252 and results.

Seminar question: Is a failed transfer caused by not knowing where expertise resides, insufficient shared knowledge, insufficient explanation, or unwillingness to cooperate? Each answer implies a different organizational remedy.

3. Replication preserves knowledge but can constrain adaptation

Winter and Szulanski distinguish a working template from the idealized transferable knowledge underlying successful replication. When causal understanding is incomplete, the template preserves combinations of practices that managers cannot yet specify accurately. Szulanski and Jensen’s Israeli franchise case gives this argument process evidence: early modifications that removed the diagnostic value of the original accompanied stalled growth, while closer adherence accompanied faster expansion. The case supports attention to sequence and comparison; it does not justify a universal prescription to copy every practice unchanged. Winter and Szulanski, 2001, template/Arrow-core and precision-replication sections; Szulanski and Jensen, 2006, field setting, measures and results.

Zander and Kogut help separate growth from imitation. Codifiability and teachability influenced the speed of internal transfer, but the imitation results were mixed and involved other factors. Product imitation and manufacturing-capability transfer are also different outcomes. Their results qualify the simple claim that easier internal transfer necessarily produces equally fast imitation by rivals. Zander and Kogut, 1995, p. 77, sample and transfer/imitation models.

Seminar question: Which features must be copied to preserve a capability, which may change, and what evidence would let managers tell the difference?

4. Routines can change through performance as well as deliberate redesign

Feldman’s four-year study observes changes within five recurring routines as participants react to their consequences. Feldman and Pentland then distinguish the generalized idea of a routine from particular performances, explaining how the two can reproduce or modify each other. These arguments put agency inside repeated activity. The 2003 paper develops theory from existing evidence; it should not be counted as a second independent field study. Feldman, 2000, methods, routine descriptions and performative model; Feldman and Pentland, 2003, abstract and pp. 110–112.

Zollo and Winter focus on mechanisms that develop or adapt operating routines: accumulated experience, articulation and codification. Their proposition about deliberate learning is compatible with attention to routine performance, but its analytical focus differs. Our comparison should ask whether a particular change emerges within ordinary performance or through a recurrent process devoted to modifying operations. It should avoid labeling every adjustment a dynamic capability. Zollo and Winter, 2002, p. 339, learning mechanisms and knowledge-evolution cycle.

Seminar question: What observations would identify a dynamic capability separately from ordinary trial and error, an operating routine, or a one-time managerial intervention?

5. Useful knowledge and better coordination have outcome-specific value

Haas and Hansen distinguish knowledge use from its benefits. Their 2005 study reports that existing knowledge can hurt competitive proposals under particular experience and differentiation conditions. Their 2007 study separates time savings, work quality and competence signaling: documents and personal advice deliver different benefits. These two papers share an empirical setting and sample structure, so they should be used as complementary analyses rather than independent replications. Haas and Hansen, 2005, abstract, measures and results; Haas and Hansen, 2007, pp. 1133, 1141–1144 and results.

Macher makes the same measurement lesson visible in a governance setting. Specialized semiconductor manufacturers solved well-structured problems faster and retained a speed advantage across complexity levels; integrated manufacturers had an initial-yield advantage for complex problems. Therefore, the claim that complexity makes integration better must identify the performance dimension and acknowledge the mixed results. Macher, 2006, Tables 4–5 and pp. 839–841.

March’s simulation supplies a separate theoretical reason to distinguish outcomes: improving average performance or reliability can change the probability of competitive primacy differently. It is a model result, not an empirical finding about a population of firms. March, 1991, pp. 71–73, 77, 84–86.

Seminar question: Does the theory predict faster execution, higher quality, successful innovation, contract wins, persistent rents, or survival? Which outcome did the study actually measure?

Six empirical contrasts

These are cross-paper interpretations based on the source sections documented in empirical.json. The studies test particular mechanisms and outcomes; their results do not constitute a single general test of RBV or dynamic capabilities. AI-assisted synthesis was checked against the indicated primary-text excerpts. Full-text availability and actual reading coverage are reported separately for every record.

1. A resource's value depends on the environment and its complements

Miller and Shamsie (1996) connect property-based resources with performance in the relatively stable studio era and knowledge-based indicators with performance in the later, less predictable era. Helfat (1997) shows how related technological knowledge and complementary physical assets shape coal-conversion research investment, while the later decline of oil prices undermines that research opportunity. Together they support a conditional account: inherited resources shape the opportunities firms can pursue, and external demand and prices help determine whether pursuit remains attractive. Neither paper establishes an invariant resource ranking. Miller and Shamsie; Helfat.

The designs answer different questions. Miller and Shamsie relate historical resource indicators to performance across two eras; multiple legal and technological changes accompany the era shift. Helfat models capability-building expenditure rather than realized innovation or profits. The seminar question is therefore: Which environmental conditions make a resource useful, and at which step—from investment to output to appropriated return—is that usefulness observed?

2. Measuring capabilities requires a level of analysis and an outcome

Henderson and Cockburn (1994) relate pharmaceutical research productivity to organizational practices and prior research success, while explicitly confronting unobserved firm heterogeneity. Ray, Barney, and Muhanna (2004) show why resources associated with a customer-service process need not significantly predict overall return on assets. Ethiraj et al. (2005) examine project contribution inside one software firm: project-management measures are informative, but the client-specific capability effect is sensitive to specification. These studies make different portions of the resource–activity–performance sequence visible. Henderson and Cockburn; Ray et al.; Ethiraj et al..

Their joint methodological contribution is stronger than a claim that “capabilities improve performance.” Define the capability independently of success; measure the activity it should affect; then investigate whether process gains survive costs elsewhere and the distribution of returns among stakeholders. Project measures can reveal mechanisms hidden in firm-level profitability, while a single-firm design limits between-firm generalization. None of these observational designs completely isolates an exogenous capability investment.

3. Organizational change has costs, forms and uncertain outcomes

Drnevich and Kriauciunas (2011) report different dynamic-capability associations at process and firm levels, with only partial support for environmental moderation. Girod and Whittington (2017) distinguish broad restructuring from smaller unit reconfigurations: their relative performance implications change with environmental dynamism. Danneels (2011) documents repeated renewal efforts at Smith Corona and examines managerial beliefs about the applicability of existing resources. An organization can actively change while choosing unproductive applications or incurring costs that offset local improvements. Drnevich and Kriauciunas; Girod and Whittington; Danneels.

The evidence spans retrospective surveys, archival panel models, and a historical case. The panel provides broader conditional associations; the case makes resource interpretation and unrealized alternatives visible. They cannot be pooled as identical treatments. Use Winter (2003) to ask which changes reflect recurring learned routines and which reflect ad hoc problem solving, then assess the costs and time horizon of the response.

4. Prior experience can enable entry and constrain adaptation

King and Tucci (2002) find that production and sales experience in disk drives predicts entry into new niches; prior transition experience does not show the expected independent positive entry association. Leonard-Barton (1992) identifies knowledge systems whose skills, technical systems, management arrangements and values simultaneously support established activities and obstruct unfamiliar project requirements. Tripsas (2009) adds an identity mechanism: opportunities that challenge a firm's identity face attention and action barriers. King and Tucci; Leonard-Barton; Tripsas.

The apparent disagreement becomes a sharper question about the kind of experience, the locus of the constraint, and the population studied. Population-level entry advantages can coexist with project-level rigidity and the failure of particular incumbents. The disk-drive study does not directly observe managerial cognition; the cases trace mechanisms but cannot estimate their population frequency. Ask which parts of experience transfer and which organizational commitments block their application.

5. Governance shapes capability use, while capability histories shape governance

Leiblein and Miller (2003) find that firm-level experience and transaction attributes both help explain semiconductor make-or-buy choices. Leiblein, Reuer, and Dalsace (2002) examine technological performance after modeling selection into governance; the direct mode effect becomes nonsignificant, while inadequate safeguards remain associated with weaker outcomes. Dyer (1996) links specialized supplier networks with several performance dimensions, although not every specificity prediction is supported. Argyres and Bigelow (2007) find that governance misalignment's mortality penalty appears during the automobile-industry shakeout rather than uniformly across their sample. Leiblein and Miller; Leiblein et al.; Dyer; Argyres and Bigelow.

These results favor models that specify selection, safeguards and competitive context. They do not establish that ownership or outsourcing always dominates. Historical capability accumulation complicates a clean division between capability and transaction-cost explanations: yesterday's governance can generate today's comparative capability. Argyres and Zenger (2012) formalize that interpretive concern, while Jacobides and Winter (2005) extend it to industry evolution.

6. Employee departure has destination-dependent consequences

Campbell et al. (2012) distinguish moves to established firms from employee entrepreneurship in legal services and find stronger adverse source-firm associations for spin-outs. Somaya, Williamson, and Lorinkova (2008) distinguish competitor-bound attorney departures from moves to potential clients; the former are associated with lost business and the latter with gains. Human capital mobility can transfer capabilities, alter competition and create relationship channels, with different implications across destinations. Campbell et al.; Somaya et al..

The studies observe different outcomes: compensation-based firm-performance proxies versus client-specific business flows. Their mechanisms are interpreted from mobility patterns rather than experimentally isolated. Destination coding therefore matters as much as a simple turnover count. Link this evidence to the question of resource control: firms can organize human capital and relationships, but the benefits of those resources may shift when people move or renegotiate their role.
