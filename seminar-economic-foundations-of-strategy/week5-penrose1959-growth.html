The Theory of the Growth of the Firm — Chapters III and V

How do a firm’s existing resources and entrepreneurial services shape the opportunities it perceives and the direction in which it grows?

The problem Penrose sets herself

Author’s argument. The object of explanation is an enterprising firm’s growth: how it perceives possibilities, develops productive services and expands into activities it can organize profitably. Chapter III calls the set of possibilities management sees and can act upon the firm’s productive opportunity. This makes opportunity partly dependent on the firm’s experience and judgment. Two firms facing the same market can therefore confront different effective opportunities. Penrose expressly narrows her account to firms with competent, enterprising management; the argument does not explain every business’s willingness to grow. (Chapter III, pp. 28–38.)

Chapter V supplies an internal mechanism. A firm assembles resources for its current activities, but these resources can yield services beyond those presently used. Experience changes what personnel know, specialization changes what they can do, and combinations suggest further uses. Growth therefore changes the conditions of subsequent growth. An external market opportunity still matters: internally available services have to meet sufficiently remunerative demand. (Chapter V, pp. 58–77.)

Resources, services and knowledge

Concept Meaning in the assigned chapters Consequence for analysis
Resource A physical or human input available to the firm An inventory alone leaves its productive contribution unspecified.
Productive service What a resource actually contributes in a particular use and combination The same resource can support different activities in different firms.
Productive opportunity Possibilities entrepreneurs perceive and can act upon Demand conditions and the firm’s interpretation jointly shape expansion.
Unused service A potential contribution not absorbed by current activity It can induce search for further uses, subject to other constraints.

Author’s argument. The resource–service distinction makes knowledge economically consequential. Learning can reveal additional services of existing resources without a corresponding purchase of additional resource units. Productive uses also emerge through combinations: the service supplied by a machine or an experienced employee depends on the other resources with which it works. This is why a homogeneous list of inputs is an incomplete account of productive capacity. (Chapter V, especially pp. 60–70.)

Our interpretation. A useful presentation example is an engineering team that develops troubleshooting knowledge while running a production line. Its accumulated knowledge may permit a related product or a process-improvement service. This is an illustration of the mechanism, not an empirical case reported by Penrose. The analytical question is which services were created through experience, which remain underused, and what complementary resources expansion requires.

How the growth mechanism works

Existing resourcesExperience, specialization and new combinationsAdditional or unused servicesPerceived profitable opportunitiesExpansion and further learning

Penrose identifies several sources of unused services. Indivisibility can make the scale of a purchased resource exceed its current use. Increasing specialization can free services as particular people or assets become more effective in narrower tasks. New knowledge can reveal possibilities that were unavailable to management when the resource was first obtained. These processes recur as the firm operates and grows; their economic significance depends on the cost and feasibility of putting the resulting services to work. (Chapter V, pp. 60–70.)

The chain contains several points at which expansion can fail. A service may exist without management recognizing a use. A perceived use may lack demand. A commercially attractive use may require complementary services that the firm cannot obtain or coordinate. Chapter V therefore joins internal inducements with external opportunities and obstacles. Presenting the process as automatic expansion would remove essential conditions from the argument. (Chapter V, pp. 71–77.)

Entrepreneurship and management

Chapter III differentiates entrepreneurial services associated with ideas, judgment and willingness to pursue opportunities from managerial services involved in executing and administering activity. The same person can supply both. The distinction concerns functions rather than fixed categories of employees. Ambition, imagination and judgment help explain why available resources do not generate the same growth path in every firm. (Chapter III, pp. 28–38.)

Our interpretation. This is useful for separating three questions in discussion: what a firm could potentially do, what its decision makers recognize, and what it can currently organize. A model that treats the investment menu as given starts after much of Penrose’s explanatory work has already taken place. The assigned chapters support that comparison; they do not constitute an empirical estimate of the size of a managerial growth constraint.

The link to Wernerfelt is textually acknowledged in his introduction. The other rows identify comparisons for this seminar; conceptual agreement alone is insufficient to establish historical influence. Han and Tong’s claim that knowledge resources were downplayed in Penrose is especially productive to discuss alongside Chapter V’s explicit treatment of learning and resource services. Their extension foregrounds cross-boundary knowledge exchange and IPR institutions; the assigned Penrose text already makes knowledge part of endogenous growth.

Evidence, limits and presentation questions

Evidence. These chapters offer conceptual reasoning and business illustrations. Their force lies in the explanation of how operating a firm can alter its opportunity set. They provide no original statistical test, no estimated effect of unused resources, and no general prediction that diversification improves performance.

Author’s scope. The firm is enterprising, management is competent, and productive opportunities interact with external conditions. Our assessment. Empirical work needs measures of resource services and perceived opportunities that precede growth. Otherwise successful expansion can be used retrospectively to infer the very capabilities proposed to explain it.

For the individual presentation, reconstruct one resource–service example and ask where the next opportunity came from. For integration, ask how resource acquisition, productive coordination, protection from imitation and the distribution of returns enter at different points in the process. These questions connect Penrose to the week’s later theories without making them interchangeable.
