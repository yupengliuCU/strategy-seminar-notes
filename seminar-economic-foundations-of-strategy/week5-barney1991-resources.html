Firm Resources and Sustained Competitive Advantage

Which attributes allow firm resources to support advantages that competitors cannot duplicate?

From heterogeneity to sustained advantage

Author’s argument. Barney begins with heterogeneous resources and imperfect resource mobility. Firms can pursue different strategies because they control different bundles, and those differences need not disappear immediately through factor-market exchange. He then examines attributes that indicate whether a resource can support sustained competitive advantage. (pp. 99–105.)

Competitive advantage is defined relative to competing firms’ simultaneous implementation of a value-creating strategy. Sustainability concerns competitors’ inability to duplicate the benefits of that strategy. It is not defined by a fixed number of calendar years. Changes in the competitive environment can remove the value of previously advantageous resources. These definitions matter when deciding what an empirical study is actually testing. (pp. 102–103.)

Reconstructing the argument

The homogeneous-resource counterfactual clarifies the role of the assumptions. If competitors possess equivalent strategically relevant resources and those resources move freely, they can implement similar strategies. Persistent differentiation requires an explanation for why that equalization fails. The article develops resource attributes that help investigate such failures. (pp. 103–105.)

Attribute What it asks What would need to be observed
Value Does the resource enable responses to opportunities or threats that improve efficiency or effectiveness? A specified productive or demand-side contribution in a defined environment.
Rarity How many current or potential competitors possess the relevant resource? A defensible comparison set, not the focal firm alone.
Imperfect imitability Why can others not obtain or develop an equivalent resource at comparable cost? Historical, informational or social mechanisms that obstruct duplication.
Lack of substitutes Can a different resource support a strategically equivalent strategy? Alternative ways of delivering the same relevant benefit.

The table translates the conceptual framework into questions for research; the third column is our operational interpretation. The original attributes are developed on pp. 105–112. A resource can be useful for survival or competitive parity even when it does not distinguish a firm from its rivals. The framework therefore requires specifying the outcome being explained.

Why imitation may be difficult

Barney develops three broad mechanisms. Unique historical conditions mean that access depends on a time-and-place combination that competitors cannot reproduce at will. Causal ambiguity concerns uncertainty about the link between a resource and sustained advantage. Social complexity concerns relationships and arrangements that cannot simply be engineered by deciding to copy them. (pp. 107–110.)

These mechanisms require different evidence. A historical account should establish what was acquired or developed and why the opportunity cannot readily be recreated. A causal-ambiguity argument should identify whose causal understanding is limited. A social-complexity argument should name the relevant relationships and explain why purchasing individual components does not reproduce their effects. Merely labeling successful organizations “complex” leaves the causal claim untested. This is our assessment of research requirements, not an empirical result in Barney’s paper.

Substitution is a separate route around imitation barriers. A rival may achieve a similar strategic benefit without copying the original resource. This distinction is useful when comparing the article with Kogut–Zander’s discussion of competitors reproducing a technology’s function through a different underlying implementation. (Barney, pp. 111–112; Kogut–Zander, pp. 392–393.)

Applications and the unit that creates value

The article applies its framework to strategic planning, information-processing systems and reputation. These examples show that an observable formal system may be widely available while its embedding in informal relationships or organizational processes differs. The source of advantage may therefore be a combination of technology, knowledge and organization. (pp. 112–115.)

The first and third rows are our cross-paper synthesis. They identify work that the 1991 framework leaves for a broader account of growth and value capture. The remaining distinctions track the article’s own definitions and analysis.

Position in the literature and the main critique

Wernerfelt analyzes resource positions and product-market strategy. Barney formalizes attributes relevant to sustained advantage. Peteraf later organizes rent conditions around heterogeneity, limits to competition and mobility. The factor-market and asset-accumulation papers ask how resources are acquired or built. Read together, they separate origins, productive use, competitive protection and appropriation.

The main empirical challenge is to identify value, rarity and imitation barriers independently of observed success. Priem and Butler’s critique and Barney’s response belong beside the framework because they dispute its explanatory content and testability. The library records preserve that exchange. Our assessment: a design that names a resource in advance, measures the proposed mechanism and examines the conditions under which it loses value offers more discriminating evidence than a retrospective list of traits found in successful firms.

Evidence and presentation use

This is a theoretical article. It offers deductive reasoning and illustrative applications, then proposes directions for empirical work. It does not establish the average performance effect of “VRIN resources,” and it does not supply a measurement instrument that can be applied without contextual judgment.

For the presentation, define the outcome before introducing the four attributes. Then take one candidate resource through value, rarity, imitation and substitution separately. End with a potentially disconfirming observation: what would show that the resource is common, replaceable, no longer valuable, or purchased at a price that absorbs its prospective return? This makes the framework a basis for inquiry rather than an after-the-fact explanation of success.
