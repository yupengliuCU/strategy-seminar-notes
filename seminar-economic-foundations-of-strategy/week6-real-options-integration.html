Real Options Theory:
from staged investment to option-generating organizations

How does a firm pay for the right to respond later, and what must its organization make possible for that right to have value? This guide traces the question from finance to joint ventures, multinational networks, capabilities, and modular platforms.

English-first, source-grounded notes based on Tony’s Session 6 teaching plan and the 11 supplied PDFs. Yupeng presents Kogut & Kulatilaka (2001) and Baldwin (2019), up to 10 minutes each. Chunyang is the class integrator.

1. The option logic

Small initial commitment→Information arrives→Exercise, switch, expand, or abandon→Payoffs depend on the state

A real option is a right to take a future action involving a real asset, usually created or preserved by a current investment. Analysis should name the asset or opportunity, the cost of obtaining the right, the future exercise cost, the information that will arrive, the time window, and who controls the decision. Uncertainty and irreversibility make the timing of commitment consequential; exclusivity, competition, learning, and organizational frictions change the result. Trigeorgis & Reuer (2017, pp. 43–47) provide the organizing distinctions.

Why ordinary NPV can miss value

Myers’ two-stage example has a first investment that buys access to a second. The firm takes stage two only if the market remains attractive. Discounting an unconditional average of both stages overlooks that contingent choice; a first-stage project with negative standalone NPV can be justified by the option it creates. This is a specific failure of a passive cash-flow forecast, not a rejection of discounted cash-flow analysis for assets in place. Myers (1984, pp. 133–136).

A numerical intuition

Illustration, not a result from any assigned paper. Pay 10 today to learn demand. Next year a project costs 90 and yields 150 if demand is high or 30 if low, each with probability ½. With zero discounting, staged investment yields ½(150−90)+½(0)−10 = 20. Committing 100 before learning yields ½(150)+½(30)−100 = −10. The difference comes from declining the low-state investment.

Boundary condition. The illustration presumes the firm retains the future right and can avoid the second-stage cost. If a rival can preempt the opportunity, waiting may destroy value; if abandoning is politically or contractually difficult, the downside is not fully truncated. See Adner & Levinthal (2004) and Trigeorgis & Reuer (2017).

3. What each article actually contributes

Foundation · conceptual

Myers (1984): finance meets strategy

Main claim. Strategic planning should distinguish assets in place from growth opportunities. In a sequential project, the first outlay can purchase a call option on later investment. Myers also notes that firms can hold abandonment options, whose value rises when a secondary market makes salvage possible.

How the argument works. He first separates problems of misapplied DCF from genuine limits of passive DCF. The stage-two investment is discretionary: the firm pays only after seeing whether stage one and the market have worked out. He then links firm value to existing earnings and the present value of growth opportunities.

Use in this week. Ask what future decision the current investment buys. Identify the exercise cost before using the label “option.”

Myers 1984, pp. 133–136; Myers 1977 on growth options and debt-induced underinvestment.

Strategy process · conceptual

Bowman & Hurry (1993): the option chain

Main claim. Strategy develops through a sequence of resource investments and choices. Existing resources contain shadow options that managers may recognize; initial investments activate opportunities, and later exercise can create further options.

How the argument works. The authors bring resource allocation together with sensemaking, learning, and incremental choice. Their option chain explains why a small commitment can be strategically important even before the ultimate project is chosen. The framework offers propositions; it is not a new empirical test.

Use in this week. It supplies the bridge from Myers’ project-level option to Kogut’s JV and the later capability and architecture papers.

Bowman & Hurry 1993, pp. 760–782, especially the option-chain discussion.

Organizational boundary · empirical

Kogut (1991): joint venture to acquisition

Main claim. A JV can be an initial foothold that gives partners a chance to expand and possibly acquire the venture after favorable market information arrives. Acquisition is treated as a possible exercise event, with the important caveat that partner bargaining also matters.

Evidence. Kogut reconstructs the histories of 92 manufacturing JVs: 37 end in acquisition, 27 in dissolution, and 28 were still operating at observation and thus censored. A hazard model links unexpected product-market growth to a greater likelihood of acquisition. Unexpected shipment shortfalls do not produce a symmetric rise in dissolution. This pattern is consistent with an expansion-option interpretation; it does not show that every JV was formed for that reason.

Use in this week. The empirical move is to specify a trigger signal and an asymmetric action, then test timing rather than merely labeling JVs “flexible.”

Kogut 1991, pp. 19–33, especially sample and results.

Operating flexibility · formal model

Kogut & Kulatilaka (1994): multinational network

Main claim. A multinational network can gain value from shifting production between plants as real exchange rates change. The benefit comes from coordinated use of locations, beyond any static comparison of current production costs.

How the model works. A stochastic dynamic-programming model considers two plants in different countries, exchange-rate uncertainty, and switching costs. Once switching is costly, the firm uses different thresholds for moving production in opposite directions: an exchange-rate change may be insufficient to reverse a prior switch. The paper also considers within-country growth options and implications for transfer pricing and performance evaluation.

Use in this week. Physical dispersion only creates potential flexibility. Coordination routines, installed capacity, and switching costs determine whether the option can be exercised.

Kogut & Kulatilaka 1994, pp. 123–139.

Synthesis · review

Trigeorgis & Reuer (2017): ROT in strategic management

Main claim. Real-options work in strategy must account for option rights, competition, learning, organization, and interactions among options. The review organizes a life cycle: identify, create or acquire, manage, then exercise.

Three approaches. Real-options reasoning uses the logic without formal pricing. Real-options valuation models assumptions and comparative statics explicitly. Behavioral perspectives study organizational implementation. The authors seek stronger links among these streams.

Key qualification. Waiting is attractive under uncertainty and irreversibility when the option is sufficiently proprietary. Shared opportunities, preemption, or learning from early entry can favor commitment instead. Their two larger strategy tensions are commitment versus flexibility and competition versus cooperation.

Trigeorgis & Reuer 2017, pp. 42–63; life cycle pp. 46–49.

Optional critique

Adner & Levinthal (2004): what counts as an option?

Main claim. Sequential investment by itself does not establish a real option. If exploration changes the possible actions and the project is difficult to abandon, the clean exercise/abandon boundary of an option model becomes hard to identify.

Why it matters. An apparent “small bet” may become an open-ended search process. Organizational rules can make abandonment more credible, but strict rules may also stop a promising discovery. Use this paper to state the boundary of the option metaphor when discussing capabilities and platform experiments.

Adner & Levinthal 2004, pp. 74–85; conceptual critique.

Optional finance extension

Trigeorgis (1993): operating and financial flexibility

Main claim. Expanded NPV combines the passive value of expected cash flows with the value of active managerial options. Deferral, expansion, contraction, abandonment, and switching can interact rather than add independently.

Distinct contribution. The paper extends operating-option analysis to staged venture-capital financing. Financing in installments can give a lender an abandonment choice and create opportunities to renegotiate or revalue later. This brings capital structure and operating flexibility into one analysis.

Use in this week. Ask who finances the option, who can stop funding, and whether one option changes another's payoff.

Trigeorgis 1993, pp. 202–224; review and valuation illustrations.

Optional strategy review

Tong & Reuer (2007): setting the research agenda

Main claim. Real-options work contributes to strategy by making resource allocation dynamic, emphasizing asymmetric payoffs, and connecting financial analysis with strategic decisions.

How to use it. The chapter introduces a research volume and maps streams including international investment, governance, capabilities, and strategy processes. It is useful as an earlier field map before the more developed 2017 synthesis; it is not a standalone empirical test of these streams.

Tong & Reuer 2007, pp. 3–28; review and agenda-setting chapter.

4. Yupeng’s two papers: closer readings

Organization Science · 2001 · pp. 744–758

Kogut & Kulatilaka — “Capabilities as Real Options”

Question. How can a firm assess the future value of a capability when the market it will serve is uncertain and the organization cannot change instantaneously?

Argument in three conceptual pairs.

  1. Scarce factor ↔ underlying asset. A capability has strategic value because of the market opportunities it permits the firm to address. Internal uniqueness alone is insufficient. The authors recover Barney’s scarce-factor-market insight and propose using the price dynamics of correlated market assets to discipline claims about a “core” competence.
  2. Inertia ↔ irreversibility. Technology and organizational arrangements are coupled. Changing one may require changing the other, so switching costs create a band of inaction, or hysteresis. Persistence can therefore be a rational response to uncertainty and adjustment costs, though behavioral inertia may also exist.
  3. Rugged landscape ↔ option value. Capabilities occupy positions in a space with local and distant performance peaks. Search within the current configuration differs from architectural change. Exploratory investments can create paths across that space and reduce later switching costs.

Formal move. Their dynamic model makes current capability a state variable and compares current profit, switching costs, and expected future value in a Bellman equation. The model clarifies how exploitation can improve today’s technique while making transition to a new one more costly. Exploration sacrifices some immediate learning-by-doing but can lower the cost of future reconfiguration.

Concrete insight. A firm may remain with a mature technique despite an attractive alternative because the cost of changing both technology and organization is high. A more competent incumbent can even face stronger inertia when experience raises the payoff to its current path. This is a theoretical implication of the model, not an empirical estimate in the article.

Question for the room. What observable market asset could proxy the payoff to an organizational capability? If no reasonable proxy exists, how far can the valuation claim go?

Source: Kogut & Kulatilaka 2001, abstract and pp. 744–756; formal model and figures in pp. 753–756. The paper combines theory and modeling; it does not estimate these propositions on a new firm sample.

HBS Working Paper 19-073 · 2019 · draft chapter

Baldwin — “Platform Systems vs. Step Processes”

Question. How does a technical architecture change the value of experimentation and, through that value structure, the organization of work?

Two architectures. A step process needs each stage to function; variability at a step can create bottlenecks, favoring coordination and unified governance. A platform has an essential core plus optional complements. With a functioning core, a weak complement can be rejected while a successful one is retained. The option payoff therefore depends on separability and selective adoption.

PropositionMechanismImportant qualification
P-1 More optionsEach feasible optional complement has a nonnegative exercise value.The platform must function and the option must be genuinely selectable.
P-2 Users × optionsMore users raise the aggregate value of options; more options raise user value.Complementors and users must be able to access the system.
P-3 Risk in optionsHigher outcome variance can raise option value because poor results are rejected.Losses from experiments and system-wide spillovers still matter.
P-4 ModularityIndependent modules permit good outcomes to be retained without accepting bad ones.Interfaces must manage dependencies; modularization has costs.
P-5 Risk × modularityGreater uncertainty increases the value of having separate module-level choices.The benefit is conditional on viable modular boundaries.

Worked example. Baldwin imagines four computer components. Two redesigned components improve value by $20 each, while two worsen it by $20 each. An integral redesign must be accepted or rejected as a whole and adds zero in this state. A modular redesign retains the two improvements and adds $40. The example illustrates selective adoption; it is not a field estimate.

Design trade-off. Platform modularity requires design rules, interfaces, and tests. Prematurely separating poorly understood dependencies can cause failure. A step process can also be modularized with inventory or time buffers, but buffering reduces flow efficiency. Baldwin’s comparative model predicts more modules as the exogenous rate of technical change rises, subject to the cost of boundaries. Her numerical figures illustrate model assumptions, not a universal optimum.

Governance implication. Thin, well-specified module boundaries can support distributed decisions and separate firms supplying complements. Dense dependencies favor closer coordination. The paper also asks when a platform sponsor should open the system and how it can capture value when third parties supply options.

Question for the room. When does adding modularity create genuine independent options, and when does it merely shift interdependence into the platform core?

Source: Baldwin 2019, working-paper pp. 4–25 (PDF pp. 6–27); propositions pp. 5–8, design trade-off pp. 11–22. This is a draft chapter with theoretical propositions and illustrative calculations, not a new causal empirical study.

Three connections worth making in class

Option creation versus exercise

Bowman & Hurry’s shadow options, Kogut’s JV, and Baldwin’s modular complements emphasize how options arise. Kogut & Kulatilaka (2001) emphasize the capability to recognize and act on them. Trigeorgis & Reuer’s four-stage life cycle makes the distinction explicit. Our synthesis: counting possible actions overstates strategic flexibility unless the firm can fund, govern, and implement them.

Flexibility versus commitment

Myers’ staged project and Kogut’s JV retain a later choice. Baldwin shows that a modular platform can multiply later choices but requires a committed platform core and interfaces. Our synthesis: the architecture that creates flexibility often rests on an earlier irreversible commitment; the research question is which commitments enable valuable later choice.

Uncertainty has different effects

Variance can raise the value of an optional complement in Baldwin’s model, while variability in an interdependent step can lower throughput. In competitive settings, more uncertainty can still induce early entry when preemption is likely. Our synthesis: “uncertainty increases option value” needs the option holder, payoff asymmetry, and competitive setting specified.

Links to Weeks 2–5

TCE and property rights: interfaces, switching costs, and who holds exercise rights shape whether flexibility is usable. Agency: managers may delay exercise, continue failing projects, or underinvest in options when rewards favor short-term performance. RBV and capabilities: routines make future responses possible, while accumulated competence can also make a change of course costly. These are course-level comparisons; the Week 6 papers do not jointly estimate them.

Possible research design (our extension). Following an exogenous technical or demand shock, compare firms with similar exposure but different pre-shock interface modularity and capability breadth. Measure the exercise event (entry, module replacement, capacity switch, or abandonment), its timing, and realized payoff separately. Alternative explanations include financing constraints, bargaining rights, and managerial inertia.

6. Questions for discussion and presentation

  1. For each paper, identify the underlying asset, initial option cost, exercise cost, trigger information, expiration, and holder of the exercise right. Which papers leave any of these implicit?
  2. Kogut (1991) interprets acquisitions after positive market signals as option exercise. What rival mechanisms could produce the same hazard pattern?
  3. In Kogut & Kulatilaka (1994), how do switching costs change the exchange-rate threshold for moving production? What organizational capability must accompany the two plants?
  4. Kogut & Kulatilaka (2001) argue that inertia can be rational. How could a study distinguish rational hysteresis from managerial bias or political resistance?
  5. Baldwin’s P-3 says risk can raise platform-option value. Which risks remain with the platform sponsor, users, or complementors?
  6. How might a highly modular platform create new system-level dependencies that its component-level option analysis misses?
  7. Adner & Levinthal warn that exploration can change the choice set itself. At what point would you stop calling a staged AI project an option and study it as open-ended search?
