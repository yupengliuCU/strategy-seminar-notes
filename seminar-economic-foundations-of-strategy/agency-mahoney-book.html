Reading Agency Theory with Mahoney:
Incentive Design, Organizational Reality, and Bridges between Theories

Joseph T. Mahoney · Economic Foundations of Strategy (2005) · Chapter 4 and relevant passages of Chapter 6 · Publication record

Version and pagination. This analysis uses the 330-page, author-manuscript-style PDF held in the local Zotero library. Chapter 4, pp. 200–236, was read in full; Chapter 6, pp. 299–306, was checked for the integration argument. All page references below refer to that file’s printed pagination, which matches its PDF sequence. They are not presented as SAGE edition page numbers. Discussions of Arrow, Levinthal, Pratt–Zeckhauser, and Wolfson are through Mahoney’s account, unless a separate original source is specified.
The central reading task: reconstruct the insurance–incentive trade-off, then ask what actual contracts, authority relationships, and organizational behavior require us to add. A useful explanation identifies the mechanism, its assumptions, and the complementary theory needed for the remaining problem.

1. Locate the chapter within the book

The book’s five main perspectives are the behavioral theory of the firm, transaction costs theory, property rights theory, agency theory, and dynamic resource-based theory. Chapter 4 asks how cooperation can be organized when interests differ and actions or information cannot be perfectly observed. Its position between property rights and resources is consequential: rights, rewards, and evaluation affect how resources are deployed and developed. Chapter 6 explicitly connects incentives with resource deployment and organizational capability development (pp. 299–305).

This sequence offers a method for studying an organizational problem. What can actors process cognitively? How does governance handle adjustment and conflict? Who holds rights over assets and returns? How do information and compensation change behavior? How do resource combinations generate performance differences? These questions concern different mechanisms. Mahoney proposes combining theoretical modules around a problem while retaining each perspective’s vocabulary and explanatory target (pp. 303–304).

“modular building blocks” — local manuscript, p. 304

The practical implication is to combine theories because the problem requires their mechanisms. Adding theoretical labels without identifying what each explains does not accomplish the proposed integration.

3. Read the two agency traditions separately

Positive agency theory explains the emergence of contracts and governance structures. “Positive” describes an explanatory ambition; it does not mean that causal identification has been established. Mahoney examines the argument that capital markets, managerial labor markets, monitoring, and bonding allow parties to anticipate conflicts and adjust transaction terms. Jensen–Meckling, Fama, and Fama–Jensen figure prominently in this discussion.

Formal principal–agent models specify preferences, information, available payments, and constraints, then solve for an optimal arrangement. Most of Chapter 4 develops this branch. Its contribution is precision about the assumptions behind a conclusion: when effort is unobserved, outcomes are noisy, and the agent is risk averse, incentives compete with insurance (pp. 201–203). The distinction helps locate Eisenhardt’s conceptual map, Jensen–Meckling’s ownership-structure argument, and the later organization-design readings.

Mahoney challenges the move from identifying governance mechanisms to concluding that agency problems have been adequately solved. He states this judgment explicitly on pp. 200 and 233. It is the book author’s critical assessment, which should be distinguished from a model’s equilibrium result and from an empirical test. Jensen–Meckling allow positive agency costs. Mahoney’s additional concern is how the tractable entrepreneurial setting extends to a complex corporation with widely dispersed ownership (pp. 204–205).

4. Insurance and incentives: why maximum effort need not be optimal

Let the agent choose action a; outcome x also depends on an external state. The principal pays s(x). When effort can be directly contracted and enforced, a risk-neutral principal can insure a risk-averse agent with a fixed wage. If effort cannot be contracted, a fixed wage supplies no incentive for greater effort in this model of monetary utility and effort costs. Making compensation depend on output induces effort by exposing the agent to some outcome risk (pp. 217–219).

Two requirements must hold simultaneously. The participation constraint gives the agent at least the utility of an outside opportunity. The incentive-compatibility constraint makes the intended action optimal for the agent. The principal therefore compares the net return to inducing different actions. Greater effort can raise revenue while requiring sufficiently expensive risk compensation to lower the principal’s net return.

“a second-best contract” — local manuscript, p. 201

The benchmark matters. An arrangement may retain lower effort or costly risk bearing while outperforming every available alternative under the same informational constraints.

5. Work through the Levinthal example

In Mahoney’s account, high, medium, and low effort produce revenue of 50,000 with probabilities 0.75, 0.50, and 0.25 respectively; otherwise revenue is 25,000. The agent’s utility from wages is √z, effort costs are 40, 20, and 5, and the outside opportunity yields utility 120. The principal is risk neutral. The following calculations reproduce the chapter’s model values (pp. 225–230); they are not empirical estimates.

Effort / 努力Expected revenue E[x]Hidden-action wages z50 / z25Expected wage E[z]Principal net E[x−z]
High43,75032,400 / 10,00026,80016,950
Medium37,50028,900 / 12,10020,50017,000
Low31,25015,625 / 15,62515,62515,625

At medium effort, expected agent utility is 0.5 × 170 + 0.5 × 110 − 20 = 120. The principal receives 37,500 − 20,500 = 17,000. Moving to high effort raises expected revenue by 6,250 but expected compensation by 6,300, reducing net income by 50. The magnitude is specific to these assumed parameters.

Remove the information constraint. When effort is observable and enforceable, high effort requires a fixed wage of (120 + 40)² = 25,600, leaving the principal 18,150. Medium effort yields 17,900 and low effort 15,625. High effort is now preferred. The comparison isolates two losses under hidden action: a lower chosen effort level and inefficient risk allocation to the risk-averse agent.

Technical qualification. The displayed contracts satisfy weak incentive compatibility. Some alternative actions tie in expected utility; under the medium contract, low effort also yields utility 120. Implementing the stated action requires favorable tie-breaking or an arbitrarily small payment perturbation. The manuscript writes strict inequalities while reporting boundary solutions, so this point should be made explicit in a seminar explanation.

6. Monitoring: incremental information and joint design

In the Arrow discussion, monitoring supplies a signal y in addition to outcome x. A costless signal can improve the second-best arrangement when it provides useful information about the action beyond what the outcome already reveals. The relevant property is incremental informativeness. A redundant metric and a signal that separates effort from luck have different contracting value (p. 219).

Levinthal’s appendix connects monitoring with compensation. Monitoring is costly; reliance on noisy outcomes also requires risk compensation. The two instruments should therefore be chosen jointly. Information about the external state can reduce how much the agent is held accountable for uncontrollable events. Repetition and relative performance evaluation add possibilities, but their usefulness depends on the information and common shocks involved (pp. 231–232).

Reader’s AI extension. A study of improved behavioral visibility should distinguish incremental information, removal of outcome noise, and a subsequent compensation adjustment. More monitoring or higher output alone does not establish the complete mechanism of reduced agency costs.

7. Oil and gas: too much of one task, too little of another

Mahoney summarizes Wolfson’s limited partnerships: limited partners provide much of the capital, while the general partner controls decisions. Distance and expertise create informational asymmetries. Tax treatment and cost allocation place immediately deductible drilling costs largely on limited partners, while completion costs are shared or fall more heavily on the general partner. The arrangement can encourage excessive drilling and insufficient completion (p. 222).

This example makes action composition central. Ask which project stage receives resources and why its costs and benefits fall on different parties. Altering stage-specific incentives may explain more than increasing a general bonus. Mahoney also reports evidence linking the operator’s record of investor success with more favorable financing terms (p. 223). This page relies on that secondary account; it does not reanalyze the underlying data or interpret the reported association as an independently established causal reputation effect.

8. Why are real contracts often simpler than model contracts?

Arrow’s assessment contrasts complex model-implied fee schedules with relatively simple sharing rules and professional compensation. Mahoney reports three explanations: the cost of specifying complex relationships, evaluative information that cannot be completely specified in advance, and social rewards or penalties. Professional responsibility, ethics, and reputation can shape behavior without operating through current monetary payments (pp. 220–221).

Levinthal also questions the modeled action. The salient conflict in senior management may concern decision quality, risk choice, or resource allocation rather than insufficient industriousness. An exclusive emphasis on effort aversion can miss these strategic choices. Mahoney invites a dialogue with March, Simon, and Cyert: formal reasoning traces a mechanism precisely, while behavioral perspectives help identify decision processes that the model omits (pp. 203, 232–233).

“social rather than monetary forms” — local manuscript, p. 221, Mahoney’s account of Arrow

This observation supplies an early connection to the subsociety discussion. It does not establish that the later theory is already fully contained in this chapter.

Reader’s synthesis. The upper branch follows the formal analysis; the lower branch follows Arrow’s and Levinthal’s critiques and Chapter 6’s agenda. The assigned papers develop the organizational implications.

11. Three concrete seminar and research questions

Why is observed pay–performance sensitivity weak? Compare outcome noise, measurement difficulties, risk preferences, task allocation, and social mechanisms. The contract itself is an organizational choice that requires explanation. This is a research-design implication of reading the model and its critiques together.

Does cheaper information through AI centralize or decentralize authority? A principal may become better able to evaluate suggestions and intervene; an employee may become better able to formulate proposals independently. Distinguish whose information changes, and measure formal approval rights, actual intervention, and independent decisions separately. This is an open question developed through the comparison with Aghion–Tirole.

Why can similar efficiency improvements elicit different cooperation responses? Compare compensation, membership changes, perceived justice, and exit opportunities. Connecting subsociety and subeconomy requires conditions under which their mechanisms imply different outcomes. Employee support or opposition by itself does not identify the mechanism.

12. Retain the level of analysis when returning to TCE and PRT

The comparison spans individual compensation and action, internal rules and authority, and firm boundaries or the institutional environment. Mahoney contrasts ex ante incentive alignment with ex post governance while also discussing the institutional property-rights tradition of Libecap and North. Applying that description to Grossman–Hart requires checking the unit of analysis again: institutional change and individual investment incentives are distinct objects (pp. 301–304).

A useful exercise fixes one organizational choice, such as outsourcing an activity, and separately records changes in compensation, information, asset control, adaptation authority, and hard-to-measure work. Only then identify which mechanism explains the choice most directly. This reader-generated exercise also clarifies why Gibbons requires a definition of integration together with its benefit and cost.

13. Evidence boundaries

Author claims: Chapter 4 explains principal–agent models and challenges optimistic interpretations; Chapter 6 supports linking content and process. Evidence used here: the local manuscript, reproduced calculations, and its reported examples. Reader inferences: the cross-text map, AI questions, and diagram. Open questions: the relative importance of these mechanisms in particular organizations and the evidence needed to discriminate among them.

Page 302 discusses bounded rationality in a broad agency tradition, whereas p. 303 explicitly describes the chapter’s mathematical model as assuming perfect rationality. Retain the branch and context when citing these passages. The manuscript also contains occasional title and year errors; bibliographic records should be checked against original publications. 从 Mahoney 读 Agency Theory：
激励设计、组织现实与理论之间的桥梁

Joseph T. Mahoney · Economic Foundations of Strategy (2005) · 第 4 章及第 6 章相关段落 · 出版物信息

版本与页码：本页依据本地 Zotero 中的 330 页作者稿式 PDF，完整阅读其第 4 章 pp. 200–236，并核对第 6 章 pp. 299–306。下文页码均指这份文件的印刷页码，与 PDF 页序一致；未将其冒充 SAGE 出版版的页码。对 Arrow、Levinthal、Pratt–Zeckhauser、Wolfson 的讨论是通过 Mahoney 的转述，除非另有原文依据，不表示已阅读这些作品全文。
主线：Mahoney 用风险分担与激励的权衡讲清代理模型，再用现实中的契约、权威和行为问题检验它的解释范围。读完这一章，应能说明一个激励安排为何成立、依赖哪些假设，以及仍需哪种组织理论补充。

1. 先定位：本章在整本书里做什么？

书的五个主体视角分别是企业行为理论（behavioral theory of the firm）、交易成本理论（transaction costs theory）、产权理论（property rights theory）、代理理论（agency theory）和动态资源基础理论（dynamic resource-based theory）。第 4 章的问题是，个体利益存在差异、行动与信息难以完全观察时，如何组织合作。它接在产权之后，并通向资源与能力：谁有权、谁获益、如何评价行为，会影响资源如何被使用和发展。Mahoney 在第 6 章明确将激励与资源部署、组织能力发展联系起来（pp. 299–305）。

这种编排提供了一种研究方法。面对企业内部的现象，可以依次追问：行动者在认知上能处理什么？治理安排怎样应对调整和冲突？资产与收益的权利归谁？报酬和信息如何改变行为？资源组合又怎样产生不同的绩效？这些问题的解释对象不同，因而需要保留各自的机制。Mahoney 在结尾强调，可以把它们作为模块组合，但每种理论仍有自己的词汇和研究问题（pp. 303–304）。

“modular building blocks” — 本地稿 p. 304

中文理解：理论可以围绕具体问题组合。组合的依据应是需要解释的机制，而非在论文中增加更多理论名称。

3. 两条代理研究线，需要分别阅读

描述性／实证代理理论（positive agency theory）主要解释现实契约与治理结构为何形成。这里的 positive 指提出解释性命题，并不等于已经完成因果识别。Mahoney 关注 Jensen–Meckling、Fama 及 Fama–Jensen 的思路：资本市场、经理人市场、监督与担保等机制，可以让相关方预期利益冲突并调整交易条件。

形式委托—代理模型（principal–agent model）则给定偏好、信息结构、可用报酬与约束，求解最优契约。第 4 章主要篇幅放在这一支。它的价值是明确展示推论依赖什么；当努力不可观察、结果受随机因素影响、代理人厌恶风险时，激励与保险会发生权衡（pp. 201–203）。把两条研究线分开，才能准确理解本周 Eisenhardt 的地图、Jensen–Meckling 的所有权结构解释，以及后续有关组织设计的论文。

Mahoney 对描述性代理研究的批评是：从存在治理机制，推到代理问题已经得到充分解决，中间仍缺少证明。他在 p. 200 和 p. 233 明确表达了自己的怀疑。这是书作者的理论判断，应与模型内的均衡结果、以及外部经验检验分开。Jensen–Meckling 承认代理成本可以为正；Mahoney 更关心的是，这套推理从简化创业者情境推广到复杂现代公司时，哪些环节尚未展开（pp. 204–205）。

4. 风险分担与激励：为什么“更努力”不是唯一目标？

设代理人选择努力 a，结果 x 还取决于外生环境，委托人只能根据观察到的结果支付 s(x)。如果双方能够直接约定和执行努力，风险中性的委托人可以给风险厌恶的代理人固定工资，承担结果波动。如果努力无法直接约定，固定工资在这个仅含金钱收益与努力成本的模型中缺少诱导较高努力的作用。于是报酬需要随结果变化，让代理人分担一部分风险（pp. 217–219）。

这里要同时满足两个条件。参与约束（participation constraint）要求代理人留在关系中至少不比外部选择差；激励相容约束（incentive compatibility）要求被期待的行动对代理人自身也是最优选择。委托人要比较的是诱导不同努力的净收益。高努力提高收入，也可能需要更昂贵的风险补偿，因此净收益最高的安排可能只诱导中等努力。

“a second-best contract” — 本地稿 p. 201

中文理解：信息约束下，合理的契约通常保留一部分低努力或风险承担成本。评价它时，应使用同样约束下可行的替代安排作为比较对象。

5. 把 Levinthal 的例子真正算一遍

Mahoney 转述的例子设置了高、中、低三种努力。产生 50,000 收入的概率依次为 0.75、0.50、0.25，否则收入为 25,000。代理人的工资效用为 √z，努力成本依次为 40、20、5，外部机会效用为 120；委托人风险中性。下表复核书中数字，金额仅是模型设定（pp. 225–230）。

努力 / Effort收入期望 / E[x]隐藏行动下的工资 z50 / z25期望工资 / E[z]委托人净收益 / E[x−z]
高 / High43,75032,400 / 10,00026,80016,950
中 / Medium37,50028,900 / 12,10020,50017,000
低 / Low31,25015,625 / 15,62515,62515,625

中等努力下，代理人的期望效用为 0.5 × 170 + 0.5 × 110 − 20 = 120。委托人的收入减工资为 37,500 − 20,500 = 17,000。提高到高努力增加 6,250 的期望收入，却增加 6,300 的期望工资，因此净收益减少 50。这个差距来自所给定的数值设定，并非一般经验规律。

再把信息约束移除。在努力可观察且可执行的比较情境中，给定高努力只需固定工资 (120 + 40)² = 25,600，委托人净收益为 18,150；中努力对应 17,900，低努力对应 15,625。这时最优选择变为高努力。书中模型展示了两种损失：努力低于完全信息下的水平，且风险厌恶的代理人承担了部分不必要的结果风险。

数学细节：表中工资满足弱激励相容，某些备选努力给代理人相同期望效用。例如中努力契约下，低努力也得到 120。按表中目标实现努力，需要有利的平局选择或极小的工资扰动。书中正文使用严格不等号，但列出的数值落在约束边界；课堂讲解时应说明这一点。

6. 监督的价值：信息、噪声与联合设计

Arrow 的讨论将监督（monitoring）理解为：除了结果 x，委托人还获得有关行动的额外信号 y。若信号成本为零，且在已有结果之外提供关于行动的有用信息，它可以改善次优契约。关键是增量信息，而非记录数量。一个与已有指标完全重复的信号，和一个能区分努力与运气的信号，在契约中的价值不同（p. 219）。

Levinthal 的附录把监督与报酬选择联系起来。监督有成本；依赖随机结果激励也有风险补偿成本，两者需要共同选择。新增信息还能帮助剥离共同环境因素，使代理人较少为自己无法控制的事件负责。Mahoney 同时讨论重复关系、相对绩效评价与锦标赛，但这些机制都有相应条件，不能仅凭“有同事可比较”就假定可以去除所有噪声（pp. 231–232）。

对 AI 的阅读者延伸：如果工具提升行为可见性，应分别测量它是否提供了新增信息、是否减少了结果噪声，以及组织是否随之调整了报酬。只观察监控增加或产出提高，还不足以确认“降低代理成本”这一完整机制。

7. 油气案例：同一个代理人可以过度做一件事、又不足地做另一件事

Mahoney 转述 Wolfson 的有限合伙油气项目。有限合伙人提供主要资本，普通合伙人决定项目行动；双方在经验、距离和信息上不对称。税收与成本分担安排让可立即抵扣的钻探成本主要落在有限合伙人身上，而完井成本由双方分担或更多落在普通合伙人身上。这种安排可能同时诱导过多钻探、过少完井（p. 222）。

阅读这个案例时，应该把任务拆开。问题不只是“代理人是否努力”，还包括他把努力和资金投向哪一个阶段。改变不同阶段的收益和成本承担，可能比简单提高总奖金更有解释力。Mahoney 还转述了声誉与融资条件的关系：曾为投资者创造较好结果的运营者能够获得更有利的条件（p. 223）。这属于书中报告的经验材料；本页未重新分析原始数据，不能把相关关系升级为已识别的声誉因果效应。

8. 模型为何预测复杂合同，现实却常使用简单规则？

Arrow 的讨论指出，模型可以对细致的结果分布给出复杂报酬函数，但现实中的分成和专业服务费用经常相当简单。Mahoney 转述的解释包括规定复杂关系的成本、无法提前完整写出的评价信息，以及社会性奖惩。职业责任、伦理和声誉都可能影响行为，却不一定通过当期金钱支付发生作用（pp. 220–221）。

Levinthal 的反思进一步改变了需要解释的行动。高层管理的关键问题可能是决策方向、风险选择和资源配置，而非工作强度不足。若把所有行为冲突都压缩成“厌恶努力”，研究可能遗漏策略性选择。Mahoney 因此邀请读者把代理模型与 March、Simon、Cyert 等行为传统放在同一问题上比较：形式推理提供了可追溯的逻辑，行为研究帮助判断模型遗漏了哪些真实决策过程（pp. 203、232–233）。

“social rather than monetary forms” — 本地稿 p. 221，Mahoney 转述 Arrow

中文理解：组织中的回报和惩罚也通过社会关系和规范起作用。这为本周的 subsociety 讨论提供了一个早期接点，但接点本身并不证明后来理论已完整包含在书中。

11. 用于课堂与研究的三个具体问题

问题一：为什么观察到的薪酬—绩效关系较弱？至少需要比较结果噪声、测量困难、风险偏好、多任务配置以及社会性机制。仅用“治理失败”或“员工缺少动力”概括，会跳过契约为何被如此选择的问题。这是从 Mahoney 的模型与批评推出的研究设计建议。

问题二：AI 降低信息成本后，权威会集中还是分散？一条可检验路径是管理者更容易核对建议，实际控制增加；另一条路径是员工更容易形成方案，主动性和局部决策能力增强。需要观察信息变化发生在哪一方，并区分正式审批权、实际干预和独立决策。这里是与 Aghion–Tirole 对照产生的开放问题。

问题三：同样的效率改善为何有不同的合作反应？可比较报酬变化、成员身份变化、公正感和退出机会。若要把 subsociety 与 subeconomy 接起来，就应提出两者给出不同预测的条件，再决定用何种证据区分；不能仅从员工支持或反对就判定某一个机制成立。

12. 接回 TCE 与 PRT 时，保留分析层次

从这门课的连续阅读看，Agency、TCE 与 PRT 的比较至少包含三个层次：个人的报酬与行动、组织内部的规则与权威，以及企业边界和制度环境。Mahoney 在结尾强调代理理论的事前激励安排与 TCE 的事后治理，但在讨论产权时同时涉及 Libecap、North 的制度变化传统。将这个概括用于 Grossman–Hart 的资产所有权模型时，需要重新核对分析单位，避免把制度层面的产权论证直接套在个体投资模型上（pp. 301–304）。

一个可行的比较练习是固定同一组织选择，例如把某项活动外包，再分别写清：谁的报酬变化，谁获取或失去信息，谁持有资产控制，发生意外时谁作调整，以及这些变化如何影响不可完全测量的工作。随后才判断哪一种机制提供了最直接的解释。这种问题驱动的拆解，也能帮助理解 Gibbons 为什么要求每种理论同时说明一体化的定义、收益与代价。此练习是阅读者的综合应用。

13. 本页的证据边界

作者主张：第 4 章提供代理模型并批评其部分乐观推论；第 6 章支持连接内容与过程研究。证据：本页使用该稿原文、模型计算与书中转述案例；它不构成新的经验检验。阅读者推断：与本周论文的对应关系、AI 研究问题及综合图。开放问题：这些机制在具体组织中的相对重要性，以及哪种证据可以区分它们。

书中 p. 302 用较宽泛的代理传统讨论有限理性，p. 303 又明确说明本章的数学模型假定完全理性。引用时应保留研究分支与段落背景。书稿还包含个别题名与年份笔误；参考文献应核对原始出版物，而不能直接复制书中每一处拼写。本文保留书作者的批评语境，同时不把它当作对所有代理理论的定论。
