Property Rights Theory, Transaction Costs Theory, and Agency Theory: An Organizational Economics Approach to Strategic Management

Authors: Jongwook Kim (Western Washington University) & Joseph T. Mahoney (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
Journal: Managerial and Decision Economics, Vol. 26, No. 4, pp. 223–242
Year: 2005
DOI: 10.1002/mde.1218
Nature: theory-integration essay / conceptual survey — a comparative application of the three branches of organizational economics to strategic management, illustrated through the oil-field unitization case
Keywords: property rights theory; transaction costs theory; agency theory; organizational economics; strategic management; oil-field unitization; residual control rights; shared ownership; resource-based theory

One-sentence summary

Kim and Mahoney (2005) show that property rights theory shares its Coasean–Alchian–Demsetzian roots with, yet is distinct from, transaction costs and agency theories, and demonstrate how a property-rights lens uniquely explains persistent inefficiency and shared-ownership phenomena that the other two treat as given, using oil-field unitization as the worked case.

Reading note. This paper occupies the application-to-strategy node of the Week-3 arc (classical PRT → modern PRT → application to strategy → ownership competence → empirical test). It does not itself build the classical metering/monitoring model (that is Alchian & Demsetz 1972) or the formal residual-control-rights model (Grossman & Hart 1986; Hart & Moore 1990); it translates both strands into the language of strategic management — firm boundaries, joint ventures, appropriation of knowledge, vertical integration — and reads the classical and modern PRT streams as complementary to transaction costs theory (TCE) and agency theory (AT). Written the same year as Mahoney's (2005) book Economic Foundations of Strategy, which anchors this seminar, the paper supplies the comparative, three-theory template that the book's organizational-economics chapters develop at book length: it is the bridge that turns economics formalism into strategy constructs such as shared ownership, residual claimancy, and appropriability, and it prefigures the ownership-competence debates by insisting that ownership is a multidimensional bundle of partitioned rights rather than a single indivisible residual.

1. Background and motivation

Kim and Mahoney attack a specific target: neoclassical economics' treatment of firm behavior. In the neoclassical world, the price mechanism — Adam Smith's "invisible hand" — efficiently and costlessly coordinates economic activity, so the very existence of the firm is a paradox (Coase 1937). The paper's opening move is to define the intellectual coalition that grew out of dissatisfaction with this picture. Property rights theory (产权理论), transaction costs theory (交易成本理论), and agency theory (代理理论) are the three branches of what Barney and Ouchi (1986) call "organizational economics," and Williamson's (1985, p. 24) "cognitive map of contract" places all three in the efficiency branch of organizational economics, set against the monopoly branch (their Figure 1, p. 224). These theories, the authors note, "form part of the theoretical core of the discipline of strategic management" (p. 223), and they have already earned their keep in strategy: transaction costs theory illuminates vertical integration (Mahoney 1992b) and corporate diversification (Teece 1982); agency theory has been applied across accounting, finance, marketing, political science, and strategy (Eisenhardt 1989).

The gap the paper attacks is property rights theory's neglect in strategy. Despite its potential value for explaining business decisions over knowledge-based resources and intellectual property rights, property rights theory had received "relatively little theoretical or empirical attention by strategic management researchers" (p. 223). The paper's stated objectives follow directly: to analyze extant property rights theory, to connect it to the more mainstream strategic-management perspectives of TCE and AT, and to illustrate the fundamental principles of all three by analyzing the business case of oil-field unitization in the United States — a case of "contracting failures" where persistent inefficiency is the observed outcome rather than the exception. The abstract states the paper's program in one breath:

Property rights theory has common antecedents with contractual theories of the firm such as transaction costs and agency theories, and is yet distinct from these theories. We illustrate fundamental theoretical principles derived from these three theories by analyzing the business case of oil field unitization.
— Abstract, p. 223

The methodological bet is that a worked business case "breathes some life into these otherwise abstract theoretical principles" (p. 229) and lets the reader see how each theory's unit of analysis, focal costs, and assumptions change the diagnosis of the same contractual situation. The deeper claim — carried through the whole paper — is that the three theories are complementary rather than rival: they share common intellectual antecedents, yet each supplies what the others omit, and property rights theory, far from being a latecomer, provides "the theoretical background" for TCE and AT (p. 237).

2. Core argument / framework

2.1 The classical theory: from Coase to the partitioning of rights

The paper's history begins with Coase (1937, 1959, 1960). The "Nature of the Firm" (1937) and "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960) are read as two sides of one insight: in a world of positive transaction costs, alternative modes of organizing must be compared. Where the 1937 paper says governance structures matter for efficiency outcomes, the 1960 paper says legal rules matter; property rights theory inherits the second claim. Two further planks complete the classical platform. First, institutions respond dynamically to new contracting situations — early property-rights scholars argued that economic institutions tend to move toward more efficient solutions through negotiation among interested parties, but that with positive, non-negligible transaction costs the process is gradual and "in some economic cases may result in failure to reach contractual agreement" (p. 226). North (1990) is invoked to sharpen the point: no student of economic history can deny that efficient economic outcomes are the exception rather than the rule. Vested interests and existing political, social, and economic positions of contracting parties can lock in inefficient property-rights regimes (Libecap 1989; Alston et al. 1999).

Second, and more subtly, the classical theory conceptualizes property rights as multidimensional. Property rights are "the rights of individuals to the use of resources … supported by the force of etiquette, social custom, ostracism, and formal legally enacted laws supported by the states' power of violence or punishment" (Alchian 1965, p. 129, as quoted on p. 226) — a deliberately broad definition that spans law, culture, and reputation. Because rights to any asset can be partitioned, different people can hold rights to different facets of a single resource: one person grazes livestock on a plot of land, another walks across it, a third flies over it. All economic activity, in Furubotn and Pejovich's (1972) phrase quoted approvingly here, is the exchange of bundles of property rights. Economic logic drives these partitions to be aggregated into bundles assigned to whichever transacting party can use them most productively — the standard efficiency criterion of the classical school. Crucially, the transfer of control over some attributes of a resource is "the very definition of a transaction: reallocating rights among transacting parties" (Barzel 1997, as rendered on p. 227). The classical position on institutional dynamics is summarized in a passage that anchors the whole paper's view of why inefficiency can persist:

The convergence to an optimal institutional arrangement is always attainable in the absence of transaction costs. Because of positive transaction costs, however, institutions that optimally suit the given contractual situation only gradually emerge, at best.
— p. 227, "Key Ideas in Property Rights Theory"

Team production enters through Alchian and Demsetz (1972): when more than two contracting parties affect the income flow from a set of rights, delineating each party's rights becomes difficult, and the distribution of income generated by collective effort — team production — becomes the critical economic issue. Alchian and Demsetz's monitoring solution (a central party monitors each member's contribution) poses the question "Who will monitor the monitor?" — answered by making the monitor the residual claimant. This residual-claimant logic returns at the end of the paper as one half of the definition of ownership.

2.2 The modern theory (GHM) and its discontents

The "modern property rights theory," also called the GHM model after Grossman and Hart (1986) and Hart and Moore (1990), asks the strategic question "who should own what assets." In these stylized models, contracts are incomplete because of bounded rationality — parties cannot contemplate all future contingencies or measure performance under each one — so ownership is identified with residual control rights: the right to use an asset in any way not forbidden by contract, law, or social norm. Where relation-specific assets create the hazard of ex post opportunism (hold-up), residual control rights over a bundle of relation-specific assets must be concentrated in one owner, and common ownership (integration) safeguards the parties. The owner is the party with the most to gain from bundling. Hart (1995) sharpens the definitional claim: control rights are not divisible, while residual rights to income are, so residual control is the stronger concept of ownership.

The paper then stages the central theoretical confrontation of its first half: the modern theory is "not an unambiguous scientific advance over classical property rights theories" (p. 227). Three criticisms are assembled. Demsetz (1998) attacks the equation of ownership with residual control as tenuous: residual rights are by definition hard to specify, yet the model assumes courts will uphold ownership claims based on them; in practice ownership is recognized as belonging to the holder of the most "important" portion of the bundle, which need not be the residual. Foss and Foss (1999, 2001) add a logical inconsistency — residual control rights fully enforceable in court sit uneasily with the incomplete contracts the model needs — and show that even well-specified, non-residual rights can be appropriated when monitoring and measurement costs are positive. The authors locate the source of these difficulties in the model's thin conception of ownership:

It is our argument that much of this ambiguity and logical inconsistency in the GHM model can be traced to its simple, uni-dimensional conception of property rights (ownership rights) to assets, perhaps necessitated by the practical limitations of analytical modeling.
— p. 228

Because residual control rights cannot be divided, the GHM model precludes shared ownership altogether. A 50–50 equity joint venture makes the point: firm A invests in R&D, firm B in manufacturing and marketing, and each should obviously control its own critical assets — but how the partners should share the unanticipated gains when one finds a new commercial use for a by-product is exactly what a residual-control conception of ownership cannot answer. Holmstrom's (1999) criticism is that the model explains why individuals own assets but not why firms own assets. A rental-contract example completes the case against uni-dimensionality: even if the renter's rights are perfectly specified in advance, significant costs of monitoring compliance (asymmetric information) and measuring the degree of compliance (measurement costs; Barzel 1982) let the renter appropriate economic benefits despite perfect specification. The conclusion of section 2.2 is therefore a two-part corrective: a comprehensive property rights concept must admit (1) multiple partitions of rights to any asset, since assets have multiple attributes, and (2) the possibility that even non-residual rights are not perfectly secure.

2.3 The worked case: oil-field unitization

Oil-field unitization occurs when a single firm is designated unit operator to develop an oil reservoir as a whole. Its economics follow from geology plus law. Oil is migratory — it moves within the reservoir toward any point of reduced pressure — and under the common-law rule of capture, property rights to oil are recognized only upon extraction, so a landowner may drill and drain oil from beneath a neighbor's land without liability. Multiple landowners over one reservoir, the rule of capture, and migratory oil jointly produce a common-pool problem: each leaseholder maximizes the value of his own lease by competitive drilling ("a race to produce"), dissipating reservoir rents through excessive capital, too-rapid production, and lost total recovery. The efficiency ideal is unitization — a private contractual arrangement, typically 10–20 years (near the natural life of the field), that is a once-and-for-all contract: sharing rules must be fixed in advance with no renegotiation, because unitization irrevocably changes the geological characteristics of the field, so individual lease shares cannot be inferred from post-unitization production. A single unit operator — normally the firm with the most to gain and the most to lose — extracts oil and distributes proceeds per the predetermined sharing rules (their Table 1, p. 230). The striking empirical fact that motivates the case is the gap between potential and realized value:

Despite the potential for substantial economic value creation, the actual rate of oil field unitization is surprisingly low.
— p. 230

The case is well chosen precisely because it is a situation of persistent inefficiency: substantial gains from consolidation exist, competitive drilling dissipates them, and voluntary contracting nevertheless often fails.

Unit of analysis. Agency theory's unit is the principal–agent contractual relationship; it takes the productive resources as the oil leases (not drilling capabilities, which are assumed homogeneous) and reads the case from the perspective of the individual oil firm. Once unitization is in place, the oil firms are principals and the lone unit operator is the agent, monitored through voting rules, notification requirements, grievance and arbitration procedures, reporting and accounting practices, and a supervisory committee (Libecap and Smith 1999, 2001). TCE's unit is the transaction — the unitization contract itself — and explains organizational form as the matching of transactional characteristics to governance mechanisms (Hennart 1993; Williamson 1996). Property rights theory's unit is the institution of unitization — contracting for unitization in general, not any single contract — so the political environment and public policy enter alongside the micro-analytic contract; this is why the authors can say the property-rights literature on unitization has implications for "why the government is also ineffective in resolving economic inefficiencies" (p. 232, citing Libecap and Wiggins 1985 and Kim and Mahoney 2002).

Focal dimensions and cost concerns. Agency theory concentrates on aligning the agent's incentives with the principals' and minimizing residual loss through ex ante contractual design; residual loss remains because monitoring is costly. Its cost concept is defined by Jensen and Meckling (1976, p. 308):

Agency costs are comprised of '(1) the monitoring expenditures of the principal, (2) the bonding expenditures by the agent, and (3) the residual loss,' (Jensen and Meckling, 1976, p. 308).
— p. 233, quoting Jensen & Meckling (1976, p. 308)

TCE sees the interdependence of tracts as asset specificity. Forming a unit and assigning production to a single operator "effectively eliminates the holdup problem" (p. 233) — but the oil firms that would have had holdup incentives instead engage in strategic holdout during the contracting phase, refusing to join unless compensated, because tracts over geologically advantaged parts of the reservoir are pivotal for efficient extraction. TCE's focal costs are the ex post costs of maladaptation, haggling, the setup and running costs of governance structures, and bonding costs (Williamson 1996, p. 176, as quoted on p. 234). Property rights theory generalizes the interdependence problem as an externality: insecure rights under the rule of capture mean each firm's drilling imposes negative externalities on the others' productivity by weakening reservoir pressure. Rent-seeking — "vested economic interests" channeled into political activity to influence unitization statutes — is a property-rights cost category with no TCE or AT analogue. Because unitization failures often involve large numbers of contracting parties — sometimes hundreds of independent owners — and heterogeneous interests between large and small firms, consensus is hard to reach (Libecap 1989).

Contractual focus and theoretical orientation. Agency theory works in a complete-contracting setup: getting incentives right ex ante delivers efficient contracting because ex post enforcement problems are assumed away, and its orientation is constrained optimization — the theory of the second best, the optimal contract under information asymmetry and non-observability. TCE assumes incomplete contracts and therefore attends to ex post governance; its orientation is comparative assessment among discrete structural forms (Williamson 1991) — "the better governance choice among (imperfect) available alternatives" (p. 234), not the optimal mix. Property rights theory is also comparative, but it compares real-world institutions including path-dependent ones and evaluates them against improvements in social welfare (Coase 1960), whereas TCE focuses on reducing costs (Coase 1937). The deepest asymmetry: agency theory and, to a lesser extent, TCE assume equilibrium outcomes — "getting the economic incentives right at the outset of the contract does not guarantee the optimal unitization contract" under TCE, but neither theory asks whether unitization happens at all. Property rights theory alone asks why efficient arrangements may never be reached:

An inefficient initial allocation of property right and path-dependence, even if decision-makers are intendedly rational, may result in fixed bargaining positions that are vastly divergent and hence difficult to reconcile, thus leading to persistent suboptimal contracting outcomes.
— p. 234

Strategic intent and sources of frictions. Agency theory and TCE adopt the shareholder view — maximize principals' payoffs, or minimize transaction costs to create shareholder value. Property rights theory adopts the stakeholder view: it weighs public policy, various interest groups, and the contracting parties, because initial endowments (e.g., the initial assignment of land to settlers), antitrust traditions, and vote-maximizing legislatures shape the rights structure within which firms compete. Its catalogue of market frictions — unclearly defined and insecure rights, externalities, vested interests — overlaps and contains the frictions emphasized by the other two. The paper closes its comparison with two summary observations, the second of which is its boldest theoretical claim:

To sum up, there are at least two noteworthy theoretical features here. First is that agency theory is not concerned with the process of how a unitization agreement actually comes about. That is, agency theory focuses on the principal–agent relationship once a team production setting, with principal–agent relationship, is put in place. But why unitization (despite its potential for aggregate economic gain) is formed, or even more important, why it will not be formed, falls outside the analytical framework of agency theory. Second, both agency and transaction cost concepts of market frictions can be largely subsumed under the property rights concept of (negative) externalities.
— p. 236

3. Evidence / examples

This is a theory paper, so its "evidence" is a worked case plus secondary empirical material, not a new dataset. The paper's evidentiary core has three layers.

First, the mechanism evidence. The unitization case establishes that the inefficiency is real and structural: because oil is migratory and the rule of capture assigns rights only upon extraction, individual lease-maximization implies competitive drilling, which dissipates the pressure needed for efficient extraction; the prisoners'-dilemma logic is documented through the literature on oil-field unitization (Wiggins and Libecap 1985; Libecap 1998; Libecap and Wiggins 1984, 1985; Libecap and Smith 1999, 2001). The once-and-for-all nature of the contract — sharing rules fixed in advance, no renegotiation, lease shares uninferable after the field's geology is irrevocably altered — makes the case analytically clean: the parties must agree on the division of a joint surplus before it is produced, under severe information asymmetry about each tract's productivity, with no market test afterward.

Second, the quantitative evidence. The paper's sharpest numbers come from note 7 (p. 238): after New Mexico added a compulsory unitization statute in 1977, production increased by 280 million barrels of oil from 33 statutory unitizations over a 20-year span (Oil and Gas Journal, May 5, 1997). Projecting New Mexico's experience onto Texas, it was predicted that 165 state-assisted (compulsory) unitizations would yield 1.4 billion barrels over 20 years — against a baseline of approximately 1.95 billion barrels of total U.S. crude-oil production in 1999 (U.S. Dept. of Energy 2000) and roughly 400 million barrels produced in Texas alone in 2000 (Railroad Commission of Texas 2001). The scale of the forgone gains is thus enormous relative to aggregate national output, which is precisely why the low voluntary unitization rate is a puzzle for equilibrium theories and grist for the property-rights mill.

Third, the worked thought examples that carry the conceptual weight. (i) The plot-of-land partitioning example (one person grazes, another crosses, a third overflies) establishes that ownership of an asset is not ownership of the physical entity but ownership of specific partitioned rights to it. (ii) The 50–50 joint venture example (firm A contributes R&D, firm B manufacturing and marketing) shows that residual-control ownership cannot adjudicate how partners share unanticipated gains from by-product discoveries — the shared-ownership phenomenon the GHM model cannot represent. (iii) The rental-contract example shows that even perfectly specified, non-residual rights are appropriable when monitoring and measurement costs are positive. (iv) Franchising extends the same logic to strategy: brand-name capital is a shared intangible resource — a common pool — into which the franchisor invests heavily while franchisees do not bear the full costs of degrading quality standards (Michael 2000).

Commentary on the evidence

The case is an excellent illustration but a weak test. The paper's own empirical claims are drawn from secondary sources — the oil-and-gas economics literature (Libecap and Wiggins 1985; Wiggins and Libecap 1985) and trade-press statistics — and are used to motivate, not estimate, the magnitude of contracting failure. Three cautions follow. First, the New Mexico and Texas figures concern compulsory (state-assisted) unitizations: they demonstrate what regulators could achieve when voluntary contracting fails, and the Texas figure is a forecast projected from New Mexico experience, not an observed outcome. Second, the underlying counterfactual — how much value voluntary unitization would have created had it succeeded — is never directly measured, so the "surprisingly low" rate is documented but its welfare cost is only bracketed. Third, the case is a single industry in a single institutional environment, so its external validity for, say, knowledge-based or intangible assets rests on the franchising example and the reader's willingness to accept the common-pool analogy. For a conceptual survey these are acceptable limitations of evidence; they mean the paper's contribution is diagnostic and programmatic rather than confirmatory.

4. Position in the literature

The paper converses explicitly with every major line in organizational economics. It extends the classical property-rights line of Coase (1959, 1960), Alchian (1965, 1969), Demsetz (1964, 1966, 1967, 1988, 1998), Alchian and Demsetz (1972, 1973), Cheung (1968–1983), and Furubotn and Pejovich (1972–1974), and their institutional-change successors North (1981, 1990), Libecap (1989), Barzel (1982, 1997), Eggertsson (1990), and Alston et al. (1996, 1999). It engages the modern GHM stream of Grossman and Hart (1986), Hart and Moore (1990), and Hart (1995) — plus the large extension-and-criticism literature listed in its note 2 (Aghion–Tirole, Baker–Gibbons–Murphy, Holmstrom–Milgrom, Rajan–Zingales, Maskin–Tirole, Whinston, and others) — and it adjudicates between the streams via the critics Demsetz (1998) and Foss and Foss (1999, 2001). It draws the agency line from Jensen and Meckling (1976) and Jensen (1983) through Fama (1980), Fama and Jensen (1983), Holmstrom (1979, 1982), and Eisenhardt's (1989) assessment; and the TCE line from Williamson (1975, 1985, 1991, 1996). The umbrella is Barney and Ouchi's (1986) organizational economics and Mahoney's (1992a) mapping of organizational economics "within the conversation of strategic management" — the same program that Mahoney (2005) develops in the book Economic Foundations of Strategy, the anchor text of this seminar, which presents TCE, property rights theory, and agency theory as complementary theoretical foundations for strategy rather than as rival candidates.

Within the property-rights line specifically, the paper performs three moves. First, it refuses to let the GHM model stand as the whole of property rights theory: it reinstates the classical conception — ownership as a multidimensional bundle of partitioned, interdependent rights — as the corrective to the modern model's uni-dimensional residual-control definition, while preserving GHM's genuine contribution (ownership concepts in an incomplete-contract setting, relation-specific assets, and the clarification of what integration means). Second, it positions property rights theory as the theoretical foundation of the other two: "some of the theoretical underpinnings of transaction costs theory and agency theory … are rooted in property rights theory" (p. 225). Third, it forges the bridge to resource-based theory: property rights theory offers "a clear theoretical base for conceptualizing resources and capabilities" (p. 237, citing Liebeskind 1996 and Kim and Mahoney 2002), and organizational integration can be theorized as common ownership. The direct antecedent is the authors' own companion paper, Kim and Mahoney (2002), which applies resource-based and property-rights perspectives to the very same oil-field unitization case; the present paper broadens the comparison from two to three theories and generalizes the argument to strategic management.

The result is a distinctive position in the literature: a synthesis paper that sides with the classical tradition on the substance of ownership while acknowledging the modern model's formal power, that reads the three organizational-economics theories as complementary, and that re-orients property rights theory from a theory of the firm in economics to a theory of strategy — applicable to vertical integration (Grossman and Hart 1986), shared ownership in joint ventures and alliances (Hennart 1993), franchising (Michael 2000), commercialization policy in biotechnology (Argyres and Liebeskind 1998), business culture (Jones 1983), the value of knowledge and intellectual property (Liebeskind 1996; Miller and Shamsie 1996), the structure of interfirm alliances (Oxley 1999), international collaborative ventures (Chi and Roehl 1997), and value creation in transition economies (Braguinsky 1999). In the Week-3 reading arc, this paper is the hinge between the formal PRT models and the later strategy-specific developments: it supplies the comparative template that ownership-competence work (the capability view) and empirical tests inherit, and it is the natural companion chapter-source to Mahoney (2005).

5. Limitations

(a) The authors' own stated limitations

  • The paper is explicitly an overview-and-illustration exercise, not a new formal or empirical contribution: "The purpose of the current paper is to contribute to a better understanding of property rights theory, and to explain how property rights theory can be applied to strategic management theory and business phenomena" (p. 224). It does not claim to resolve the classical–modern debate.
  • The authors concede that the GHM model's simplifications are "perhaps necessitated by the practical limitations of analytical modeling" (p. 228), and they present their two-part corrective (partitioning plus appropriation of non-residual rights) as a direction for future work rather than as a completed model.
  • They acknowledge that the empirical regularities they invoke are others' findings (Libecap and Wiggins 1985; Wiggins and Libecap 1985; Libecap and Smith 1999, 2001), and they flag that property rights theory itself had received little empirical attention from strategy researchers.
  • Their forward-looking claims are explicitly conjectural — e.g., "We conjecture that with advances in coordination enabled by information technology, intellectual property rights research will be a growth industry for at least the next decade in the discipline of strategic management" (p. 237).

(b) Independent judgment

  • [paper claim] The central thesis — that agency theory and TCE assume equilibrium outcomes while property rights theory is "well suited to explain business situations where inefficient economic outcomes persist" (abstract, p. 223) — is asserted at the level of research-program characterization, and it is fair as far as the oil case goes, but the paper does not supply a formal or empirical demonstration that the three theories generate different predictions in a testable way.
  • [evidence] The supporting material is one industry case plus secondary econometric and historical studies; the headline numbers (New Mexico's 280 million barrels from 33 statutory unitizations; the 1.4 billion-barrel Texas projection) concern compulsory, state-assisted unitizations, whereas the theory's distinctive claim is about the failure of voluntary contracting — so the evidence documents regulatory rescue more directly than it isolates the voluntary-failure mechanism.
  • [inference] The complementarity argument is programmatic: the paper shows that each theory highlights different contractual features of one case, but it never specifies decision rules for when a strategy researcher should reach for PRT rather than TCE or AT — e.g., conditions under which TCE would prescribe hierarchy while PRT predicts persistent inefficient non-contracting. The "complementarity" claim therefore describes a division of intellectual labor, not a unified model with cross-theory comparative statics.
  • [inference] The classical correction to GHM (multipartitioned rights, appropriable non-residual rights) is argued through examples — the land plot, the 50–50 joint venture, rental contracts, franchising — rather than through a formal framework, so the paper does not establish how a partitioned-rights model would handle the incentive problems (holdup on relation-specific investment) that motivate GHM in the first place.
  • [open question] Ownership competence — the later capability view that firms differ in their ability to delineate and enforce ownership arrangements productively — is gestured at through the multidimensional conception of rights, but the paper does not explain where that competence comes from, how it is built, or how it connects to the RBV link it draws (a gap that subsequent work in the ownership-competence and empirical-test nodes of this course's arc addresses).
  • [open question] Its key constructs — "unclearly defined and/or insecure property rights," "weak appropriability," "vested interests" — are never operationalized, so the paper offers no measurement guidance for empirical strategy research on property rights.
  • [inference] The "subsumption" claim that agency and transaction-cost frictions reduce to the property-rights concept of negative externalities is provocative but one-directional: the paper does not show the converse, that externality-based reasoning can recover the governance, adaptation, and incentive prescriptions that TCE and AT generate — which would be the real test of whether PRT is the general theory and the others special cases.

6. Conclusion

The paper establishes three things. First, the three organizational-economics theories share common antecedents in Coase, Alchian, and Demsetz and yet ask different questions, so a fair reading of any strategy situation requires all three lenses — with property rights theory supplying what the others assume away: why inefficient contractual outcomes persist, and how shared ownership works. Second, ownership itself is a two-sided concept: the appropriate allocation of residual control rights mitigates ex post contractual problems (holdup), while the appropriate alignment of residual claims mitigates ex ante contractual problems (incentives); "both residual control and residual claimancy (ex ante and ex post contractual) issues are at the heart of a definition of ownership" (p. 237). Third, property rights theory grounds the resource-based view — it offers the clear theoretical base for conceptualizing resources and capabilities, and theorizes organizational integration as common ownership.

The one-line takeaway for strategy: treat ownership architecture — who holds residual control over which partitions of which assets, and who bears residual income — as a first-order strategic choice made under incomplete contracts and insecure rights, and treat the institutional and legal environment (rule of capture, antitrust policy, enforcement) as an endogenous part of strategy rather than as background. In an economic world of positive transaction costs, closing the gap between potential and realized value creation is exactly the strategist's job, and property rights theory names the friction.

"In an economic world of positive transaction costs, there are frictions in the development of property rights that can drive a persistent wedge between potential value and realized value creation."
— p. 235

Strategy, on this reading, is the sustained effort to design and re-draw the ownership arrangements that narrow that wedge — the firm's boundaries, its shared assets, and the rights partitions behind every resource it controls.
