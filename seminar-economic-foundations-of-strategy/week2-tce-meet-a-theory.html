2 · The moderator's opening: an X-ray of TCE

Nuno takes ten minutes to take TCE apart. The unit of analysis is the transaction — a good or service transferred across a technological interface; the transaction attributes of interest are asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency; the governance modes are market / hybrid / hierarchy; the two behavioral assumptions are bounded rationality and opportunism. The core prediction is clean: as asset specificity rises, the transaction costs of market governance rise, and hierarchy becomes more likely. (These are the exact building blocks spelled out in Williamson 1985 and Williamson 1991.)

On the "current state" he offers a glass-half-full/half-empty frame: TCE is at once "alive and prolific" (an enormous number of empirical tests) and "continuously criticized." On the evidence side he flags the milestones: David & Han (2004, SMJ) — a review of roughly 60 studies finding that asset specificity is the most reliable predictor while uncertainty gets weaker support; a Tilburg meta-analysis with 90,000+ observations giving broad support; and the most recent review, co-authored by Silverman himself — Cuypers, Hennart, Silverman & Ertug (2021, Academy of Management Annals). On the controversy side he names: the opportunism assumption caricatured as a "cartoon of human nature," construct-validity problems (Kyle Mayer's 2009 chapter), and the irony of practical implications — if you treat everyone as an opportunist, you may induce opportunistic behavior.

3.1 Brian Silverman: basic propositions, the empirical map, four future directions

Silverman positions himself as the bridge from Nuno's introduction to the other three. He first works through the first-order assumption: actors economize on transaction costs by assigning transactions — which differ in their attributes — to governance structures — which differ in their governance capabilities — in a discriminating way. To the familiar attribute triad (asset specificity, uncertainty, frequency) he adds a fourth that entered later: appropriability. He illustrates "asset specificity × uncertainty → hold-up" with an example: a Chicago furniture maker shipping down the Mississippi to New Orleans asks you, the railroad, to finance a 50-mile spur line into the plant; once built, the furniture maker can squeeze you — and the more uncertainty there is, the more you worry. So you either sign a safeguarded contract or integrate. He also defends the opportunism assumption: the famous Ghoshal & Moran (1996) critique is largely a straw man that "rewrites Williamson's words and attacks the rewrite"; quoting Joe Mahoney, safeguards are "another form of insurance," and business schools teaching accounting fraud are not teaching people how to commit fraud.

On the empirical map he traces a line from organizational form to performance consequences: Coase poses the question → Williamson fills it in (first vertical integration) → Teece (empirical tests + horizontal diversification + international) → Hennart (international) → Erin Anderson (marketing) → Gary Pisano (alliances as the midpoint between market and hierarchy) → Joanne Oxley (the first empirical work on appropriability — see Oxley 1997). The recent "performance consequences" literature asks: do firms that are "misaligned" by Williamson's standard suffer? The answer: lower profits, higher costs, faster death. The reach extends from vertical integration, diversification, alliances, entrepreneurship, innovation, and international business to supply chain, marketing, law — even the diffusion of diamond engagement rings (Margaret Brinig's paper). His four future directions: ① organizational change — why don't misaligned firms adjust? (adjustment costs); ② within-firm design — R&D centralization vs decentralization, and the finding that "firms use contracts internally too" (Katie Magelssen and co-authors); ③ cognition (handed to Weber); ④ culture and values — returning to an old 1996 debate.

3.2 Nick Argyres: informal organization — the 47-year atmosphere puzzle

Argyres picks the future direction he finds most interesting and drills into it: TCE and informal organization. His anchor is Williamson 1975's sentence — "atmosphere matters, but I don't know how to handle it" — and the fact that by 1991 Williamson had "forgotten atmosphere," returning to the purely formal market/hybrid/hierarchy matching. Argyres's challenge: formal structures are easier to imitate than informal ones, so if competitive advantage only came from "matching governance to transactions by the book," every firm would do it and there would be no advantage. His opening quote is Drucker's:

Culture eats strategy for breakfast.
Peter Drucker, as quoted by Argyres.

He breaks "informal organization" into four pieces, each of which TCE does not explicitly handle but which, extended, would explain organization and performance better: ① informal communication — communication not mandated by the organization, distinct from the formal-hierarchy information-processing literature (Nonaka noted organizations have a "special language" but did not compare); ② social networks — partly communication channels, and how they interact with formal governance structures (he, Silverman, and Luis Rios studied how R&D centralization shapes inventors' informal networks and thereby innovation); ③ informal incentives — relational contracts, payments/bonuses without legal enforcement, and Bob Gibbons's question of whether relational contracts can be observed inside firms (see Weber & Mayer 2011 for the formal side of this debate); ④ informal authority — people without high positions who nevertheless influence decisions, like the senior Boeing engineers of the 707/727 era who refused management roles and could "push back" against wrong design decisions (the Aghion & Tirole model). His practical challenge is data availability (emails, meeting records are hard to get), and his advice to students: some research with downloadable data, some "walking into organizations" for finer-grained casework and theory building.

3.3 Fabrice Lumineau: digital technology and blockchain

Lumineau connects TCE to digital technology, focusing on blockchain. His premise: TCE has been discussing technology since Coase 1937 (Coase already noticed the telephone and telegraph shaping firm boundaries) — the digital era just refreshes the question. Blockchain is a cryptography-based decentralized system resting on two pillars: decentralized consensus + machine automation, with smart contracts delivering immutability and traceability. His core judgment: blockchain offers a third enforcement mode beyond contractual and relational governance — contracts rely on legal remedies (damages, termination); relational governance relies on social norms and trust (the shadow of the future); blockchain relies on a self-contained set of formal rules (protocol + cryptographic enforcement).

Decomposing transaction costs by stage: search costs fall (entering a smart contract is a pre-commitment not to behave opportunistically); design/coding costs rise (transactions must be codifiable, constrained by verifiability); monitoring costs fall (real-time transparency); enforcement costs fall (simplified dispute resolution). The conclusion is two-sided: digital technology can both expand and contract firm boundaries; AI can relieve some limits of bounded rationality (learning, processing speed, memory, computation) while potentially amplifying some cognitive biases. Blockchain lowers behavioral uncertainty — and therefore directly interrogates one of TCE's central pillars: the incidence and form of opportunism. His closing line is the panel's lightest and heaviest:

TCE is dead maybe, but long live TCE.
Fabrice Lumineau.

Connections to the readings: this is the same "third enforcement mode" logic as the digital-governance agenda in Nagle, Seamans & Tadelis (2024) (reputation systems as substitutes for trust) and the expanded governance menu in Kretschmer et al. (2022).

3.4 Libby Weber: behavioral governance — prying at the assumptions with psychology

Weber calls herself a "TCE geek" (her dog is named Oliver) whose career has been poking at TCE's assumptions one by one. Her claim: strengthening TCE with psychology — not just cognition, but psychology more broadly — can do three things: answer the classic criticisms, reopen long-settled debates, and connect to areas TCE previously couldn't reach (like Argyres's atmosphere). Her methodological reminders: ① know the theory cold (she re-reads The Economic Institutions of Capitalism repeatedly, finding something new each time); ② target the assumptions — Williamson had specific reasons for each assumption, and you may change one (e.g., swap risk-neutrality for risk aversion and see what breaks); ③ dig out the hidden assumptions — e.g., "why do people think contracts damage/promote trust?"; ④ be explicit about the level of analysis (individual → aggregated to firm); ⑤ you must say something new (the best advice a reviewer ever gave her).

Two examples she develops. First, treating opportunism as a motive, not a single motive: with Foss & Lindenberg's work she links organizational form to goal frames — a normative goal frame drives collusive/status opportunism (e.g., hold-up), a hedonic goal frame drives shirking/instinctive opportunism (e.g., free-riding); matching Williamson 1986 Ch.10's 2×2 of simple hierarchy/U-form/M-form/project matrix against these motives, the diagonal (matched) pairs suppress opportunism, off-diagonal pairs do not — yielding two previously unknown kinds of "hierarchy failure." Second, reopening the old "contracts and trust: substitutes or complements" debate: dig out the hidden assumption that "contracts are only for prevention," then introduce prevention-framed vs promotion-framed contracts, and show experimentally (with Chris Bauman) that promotion-framed contracts complement trust while prevention-framed contracts substitute for it. New areas this connects to: job design, leadership, organizational learning. Connections to the readings: this is the behavioral governance program of Weber & Mayer (2011) and Schepker et al. (2014), and it answers the Ghoshal & Moran charge of a cynical human model from within — the same critique that motivated Verbeke & Greidanus (2009) to widen opportunism into the envelope concept of bounded reliability.

4 · Three highlights from the cross Q&A

  • The risk-neutrality assumption (audience question): TCE assumes risk neutrality, but it has barely been tested empirically. Silverman's judgment: it is not a load-bearing assumption but a simplification that focuses attention; it can be relaxed and formal models can trace the consequences — which is exactly where Weber's cognitive work bites.
  • How to measure transaction costs (audience question): Lumineau says almost nobody measures them directly; the key is first to decompose which stage and which level (firm / transaction / international), then talk about measurement. (The measurement agenda is also challenge #1 in Cuypers et al. 2021.)
  • Platform–complementor relations (Argyres answering "the big future question"): TCE usually studies buyer–supplier dyads or intra-firm units, but platform owners and complementors often have no explicit goods/services exchange at all — yet they stand in a transaction relationship with ample opportunism space on both sides. This is a large domain TCE has not explicitly modeled.

The readings give TCE's canonical skeleton (question → operationalization → formalization → entry into strategy — see the four-reading integration page); the panel gives four working scholars' assessment of where that skeleton is strong and where it is missing pieces.
