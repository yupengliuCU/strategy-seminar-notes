In one sentence

TCE's grammar — discriminating alignment, matching governance to transaction attributes — is sharper than ever in the AI era; but its two-term vocabulary (market ↔ hierarchy) and its assumption that specificity and bounded rationality are fixed exogenous inputs are being rewritten in the field by AI companies' cross-investments, acqui-hires, and open-sourcing.

How to read this page: three threads woven into one — ① are TCE's predictions still accurate (§1–2); ② how to understand and fuse TCE in this era (§3); ③ what AI companies' cross-investments and M&A say back to TCE (§4–6); plus one emerging extension: codified skills × human asset specificity (§7). 1 · The core judgment

AI simultaneously changes both sides of transaction costs, relaxes cognitive constraints, and turns the core assets into non-rival and modular ones — thereby producing governance forms beyond market and hierarchy. The right statement is: TCE's diagnostic framework remains reliable, but its specific predictions need layer-by-layer revision, and it must be fused with the knowledge-based view, platform theory, and the cognitive perspective to explain organizational boundaries in the AI era.

2 · Are TCE's predictions still accurate? A layered answer

Conclusion: the master proposition of discriminating alignment holds; the specific mapping "high specificity → hierarchy" frequently fails in the AI era.

2.1 What still holds (the core logic is not obsolete)

  • Specificity → hold-up → governance is everywhere in the AI stack: chips (NVIDIA's CUDA ecosystem), data pipelines, RLHF alignment, and fine-tuned models tied to a specific business are all highly specific, hard to specify in contracts, and hold-up-prone assets.
  • Incomplete contracts + residual control rights (Grossman–Hart–Moore: GH 1986, HM 1990) play out live in AI alliances — the OpenAI–Microsoft equity/compute/IP structure is essentially "quasi-ownership" solving a transaction that cannot be written down ex ante.
  • TCE's diagnostic function is amplified: AI raises the cost of drawing the boundary in the wrong place (platform lock-in, digital cruft), which makes careful diagnosis of transaction attributes more valuable, not less.

2.2 Predictions that frequently fail

Classic predictionWhy it fails in the AI era
High specificity → hierarchy (integration/M&A)Hold-up requires two premises that the AI era's most valuable assets both violate. Premise A — the asset is ownable: integration protects by transferring ownership, but ① inalienable human capital (Hart–Moore) — researchers, mission, culture — cannot be owned, only employed, so acquisition buys nothing and destroys the asset (it is why acqui-hires buy "weights + employment" instead). Premise B — the asset's value is locked into the bilateral relation: ② digital assets' non-rivalry + modularity keep even specific investments reusable through standard interfaces, so the outside option stays good and there is little quasi-rent to expropriate. Where both premises hold — chips, compute, CUDA ecosystems — the classic prediction still runs (labs backward-integrate into compute, §5.5); where they fail, governance shifts to minority equity, quasi-integration, or market/API
IT lowers transaction costs → everything moves to the marketThe direction is two-way and heterogeneous: the internet pushed dis-integration in manufacturing (Forman & McElheran 2025), but data-driven technologies push "insourcing without reshoring" (Kim 2026); AI even internalizes tasks previously governed by the market (Stevens 2026)
Make-or-buy is a binary choiceEmpirically, plural sourcing is the norm (Parmigiani 2007; Hoffreumon et al. 2024), and AI makes complementary sourcing even more common
The governance menu is "market–hybrid–hierarchy"Forms beyond market and hierarchy appeared: open-source commons (Benkler), platforms/ecosystems (Kretschmer et al. 2022), algorithmic governance (Uber), protocol-coordinated "headless firms" (Klein & Wieczorek), cognitive-fusion networks (Wan 2026)
Bounded rationality is a fixed constraintGenAI directly relaxes bounded rationality (Wan 2026) — the foundation of TCE's behavioral assumptions is being shaken

In one line: TCE's sense of direction is still right, but its coordinate system (the one-dimensional market↔hierarchy spectrum) and its engine (only "economize on transaction costs," never "create/learn") need replacing. It remains the best debugging tool (where will hold-up occur, where should we integrate), but it is no longer a complete design tool (what capabilities to build, which ecosystem to enter).

3 · How to understand and fuse TCE in this era

3.1 Treat TCE as a diagnostic layer, not a verdict layer

TCE anchorTranslation into the AI era
Transaction costBecomes a composite of coordination cost + governance cost + lock-in cost. AI changes internal coordination costs (agents make internal coordination cheaper and messier) and external transaction costs (APIs make market procurement cheaper; platform lock-in makes exit more expensive)
Asset specificityDistinguish two specificities: traditional relational specificity (site/physical/human/dedicated — hold-up-prone) vs the digital era's within-module specificity + standard cross-module interfaces (specific, yet reusable inside the ecosystem; hold-up weakened)
Governance structureThe menu expands from "market–hybrid–hierarchy" to a multi-dimensional space: market — contracts/alliances — hierarchy — platform — ecosystem — commons — algorithmic governance

3.2 What TCE answers and what it does not (the precondition for fusion)

TCE answers: given a transaction, market or internalization, so as to avoid hold-up and minimize governance costs (the "value protection / economizing" lens).

TCE does not answer: ① which capabilities to accumulate and how to learn and innovate (→ knowledge-based view: Nickerson & Zenger 2004; Argyres & Zenger 2012); ② how much value to capture and through which complementary assets (→ Teece 1986); ③ which ecosystem to enter and how to dance with the platform owner / complementors (→ Kretschmer et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2022; Zhu & Liu 2018); ④ what organizations become once cognitive constraints relax (→ Wan 2026; Klein & Wieczorek).

3.3 An operational fusion framework: four questions in order

When an AI-related activity faces a boundary decision, ask in sequence: ① the TCE layer — what are the transaction attributes, where is the hold-up risk? (should we internalize to protect?) ② the capabilities layer — is this a hard-to-imitate core capability to accumulate over time? (should we internalize to learn?) ③ the complementary-assets layer — are there co-specialized assets (data, channels, trust, ecosystem position) for value capture? (Teece 1986) ④ the ecosystem layer — is industry architecture evolving toward platforms/ecosystems/commons? (Jacobides et al. 2021)

5 · How TCE reads these deals

5.1 Cross-investment = quasi-integration through mutual hostages

Compute-for-equity is a co-specialized exchange: the lab's training needs are assets specialized to a particular cloud architecture, and the cloud's compute commitments to the lab are equally specific investments — a bilateral dependency. Equity + compute commitments = symmetric credible commitments (Williamson 1983): each side's specific investment is a hostage that curbs opportunism. This is exactly the core prediction of Oxley (1997): when appropriability hazards are severe (frontier weights would leak under a pure market contract), governance shifts toward equity alliances.

transaction cost economics regards vertical integration that is not attended by transaction cost economies as a source, not of power, but of weakness. That is because internal organization always experiences a loss of incentive intensity and added bureaucratic costs as compared with markets and hybrids.
Williamson (1991, SMJ, p. 79) — the warning label on every AI mega-acquisition that is not clearly transaction-cost-economizing.

5.2 Why minority equity + licenses, not full acquisition?

  • Inalienable human capital (Hart & Moore 1990): the core asset is researchers' tacit frontier knowledge — unbuyable. Acquisition would destroy the equity upside and mission that attract talent. So Microsoft takes only a minority stake; acqui-hires unbundle the transaction: the alienable part (weights) is bought via license, the inalienable part (talent) is ring-fenced with employment contracts, and no equity changes hands.
  • High uncertainty → real option: the technology trajectory is unknown; a minority stake is an option on an unknown technology rather than an all-in bet (TCE's adaptation argument).
  • Institutional constraints (North): full acquisitions trigger antitrust review; minority equity + licenses + hiring is the "stealth merger."

5.3 Circular deals = a new form TCE can analyze but never presupposed

"I invest in you → you buy his chips → he buys my cloud" is a bundled, multi-party, quasi-barter transaction whose price is not cash but symmetric specific commitments. TCE's market↔hierarchy menu has no clean cell for it — asset specificity is distributed across a network rather than a bilateral pair, and systemic risk follows: each party's "asset" is another party's revenue; the whole web holds itself hostage.

5.4 Open/closed source = deliberately de-specifying one layer to protect another (Teece 1986)

TCE predicts firms will protect specific assets (integrate to prevent expropriation); but Meta open-sourcing Llama and NVIDIA giving away software is deliberately abandoning appropriability at one layer — to commoditize rivals' core layer (free Llama destroys Anthropic/OpenAI's pricing power) and to protect one's own complementary-asset layer. This is Teece (1986) "profiting from innovation" played across a multi-layer AI stack. The implication: open source is a specificity-destroying weapon that turns a rival's specific model into a commodity — TCE takes specificity as exogenously given; here it is a manipulated strategic variable. (This is outlook point #1 in the literature review's research agenda.)

7 · AI layers & the one-person company

7.1 The AI stack is a specificity gradient, not a uniform wall

One clean way to read the AI industry through TCE is to treat the stack's four layers — hardware, infrastructure, data/cloud, software — as a gradient of asset specificity, each with its own hold-up hazard and therefore its own predicted governance.

LayerAsset specificityHold-up hazardTCE predictsObserved 2024–26
Hardware (chips/silicon)Highest — co-specialized with software (CUDA), fab-specific, massive dedicated investmentHighestHierarchy / deep controlNVIDIA forward-integrates into CUDA & DGX Cloud; labs backward-integrate (Broadcom, in-house silicon, Stargate); TSMC as a bilateral foundry
Infrastructure (compute/datacenters)High — capacity dedicated to training, huge sunk capitalHighLong-term contracts / quasi-integrationcompute-for-equity (Microsoft–OpenAI; Amazon–Anthropic), the $300B Oracle deal, the circular "perfect loop"

Software (models/apps)Low at the generic level (foundation models commoditizing); app-layer specificity lives in data + domain + distribution, not the codeLow for generic models; shifts to ecosystem lock-inMarket / APIModels bought via API; app/agent layer internalized with proprietary data; open weights (Llama) commoditize the model layer

The takeaway: specificity is not uniformly high across the stack — it has two peaks (hardware at the bottom, proprietary data at the top) and a commoditizing middle (generic models). Firms integrate where the peaks are; the modern firm is increasingly the hardware-adjacent control bundle or the data + orchestration + judgment bundle. This is exactly the pattern the cross-investment map in §4 shows — every layer integrates toward its own peak and contracts across the trough.

7.2 The one-person company (OPC) is TCE's limit case

By 2026 the "one-person company" is no longer a thought experiment — it is a policy-recognized form. Media and reports describe "1 person + N agents" startups in which a solo founder plus AI agents runs what used to take a whole firm, with profit margins reportedly up 20%+ and the four-stage lifecycle compressing from ~3 years to ~10 months (Anthropic × Tsinghua report); Beijing has made OPCs an official policy category. Wan (2026) opens with the canonical anecdote:

A solo entrepreneur armed with ChatGPT, Claude, and Midjourney recently delivered a comprehensive brand strategy, complete with market analysis, creative assets, and implementation roadmap, outcompeting a traditional 20-person agency in both speed and quality.
Wan (2026), "Beyond markets and hierarchies," Electronic Markets 36:41, opening illustration.

The TCE reading has three moves. (1) Why the firm existed — and why it shrinks: Coase's firm economized on the costs of using the price mechanism (discovery, negotiation, enforcement); agents collapse those costs from inside, so the minimal governance unit shrinks toward one person. (2) What remains specific: the OPC keeps in-house exactly the peaks from §7.1 — proprietary data, domain judgment, orchestration — and contracts everything else through APIs and agents (market governance). (3) Where TCE struggles: with zero employees there is no employment relation to safeguard, no bilateral dependency to hold up, and no internal hierarchy to be the "firm" — the OPC is a bundle of one person's inalienable human capital wrapped in algorithmic governance, which is precisely the "beyond markets and hierarchies" form the literature review's outlook flags as an open frontier.

8 · Extension: agent/skills and human asset specificity

The frontier question the stack raises for the Week 2 readings: human asset specificity is endogenous to technology. When a task is codified into a model, the human capital specialized to that task loses its specificity — and with it its hold-up protection (Kanazawa, Kawaguchi & Shigeoka 2026 shows technology changing skill returns among taxi drivers; Stevens 2026 shows firms swapping market-contracted labor for AI at a steep discount). Williamson's four types of specificity were static; in the AI era the interesting variable is the rate at which technology de-specifies human capital — which determines whether the income that used to flow to firm-specific skills flows to the platform, the model owner, or the orchestrator. This is the "codified skills × human asset specificity" extension named in the page's original title line, and it connects directly to Warin (2025)'s platform-gatekeeper argument and the headless-firm hypothesis of Klein & Wieczorek.

9 · Research hooks worth writing up

  1. Network-level TCE: formalize hold-up when hostages are distributed across a circular web (the OpenAI–Oracle–NVIDIA loop) rather than a dyad. What are the comparative statics of "mutual hostage" in an N-party network?
  2. Specificity as a choice: model open-sourcing as deliberate de-specification (Teece 1986 across layers) — when does destroying your own layer's appropriability maximize the stack's total capture?
  3. Endogenous human-asset specificity: measure how AI codification changes the quasi-rents of specific human capital (a natural experiment in the gig economy, extending Kanazawa et al. and Stevens).
  4. Governance of the one-person company: what does "discriminating alignment" predict when the firm has no employees — and what replaces the employment relation as the safeguard?
TCE's grammar — "match governance to the hazards of the transaction" — has outlived every technology that was supposed to kill it.
What AI changes is not the grammar; it is the vocabulary:
new assets (non-rival weights), new hazards (network-level hold-up),
and a governance menu that no longer fits on one line.
