Knowledge of the Firm, Combinative Capabilities, and the Replication of Technology

Why are firms comparatively effective at creating and transferring knowledge, and how does this affect growth and organizational boundaries?

What the firm knows how to do

Author’s argument. Kogut and Zander explain capabilities through knowledge held by people and expressed in the organizing principles of a social community. These principles structure how individuals and groups cooperate. Replacing employees does not automatically replace the organization’s capabilities, because its productive knowledge also depends on the relationships and practices through which people work together. (pp. 383–385.)

The paper distinguishes information from know-how. Information communicates facts and propositions through an understood code; know-how concerns the accumulated practical ability to perform an activity. Both can be difficult to communicate. The distinction therefore should not be mechanically equated with “explicit information versus tacit skill.” The authors discuss codifiability and complexity as dimensions that shape the transfer of both kinds of knowledge. (pp. 386–388.)

The table paraphrases Figure 2, p. 388. Shared language helps communication within a group, but communication across professional groups can be harder. Higher-order organizing principles connect those groups. This produces a layered account of firm knowledge: what individual members know, how small groups communicate, and how the wider organization combines their contributions. The authors leave a fuller integration of individual and organizational knowledge as an unresolved task. (pp. 388–390.)

The replication paradox

Author’s argument. Codifying knowledge and simplifying its use can lower the cost of internal transfer. That supports expansion beyond the physical limits of the original expert or small team. Yet making knowledge easier to reproduce can also lower outsiders’ imitation costs. Growth and protection can therefore pull in different directions. (pp. 390–391.)

Codification and shared organizing principlesFaster internal replication and growthPotentially easier external imitationPressure to develop new knowledge

The authors also distinguish copying a technology’s underlying implementation from reproducing its function. Competitors may deliver similar benefits through another technical route. Competitive protection therefore depends on what rivals can do and what buyers value, not solely on whether the original code or process remains hidden. (pp. 392–393.)

Combinative capabilities and path dependence

Combinative capabilities support new applications through recombination of existing and acquired knowledge. Learning is local because new activity must connect with the organization’s current information, relationships and ways of organizing work. The authors explicitly invoke Schumpeter’s new combinations and behavioral/evolutionary accounts of local search. They also examine how survival pressure can crowd out investments whose value lies in future applications. (pp. 391–394.)

Our interpretation. This gives a process account of resource heterogeneity: firms do not simply start different; their current ways of learning can reproduce and extend those differences. The mechanism also creates a boundary condition. Prior competence helps when a new activity fits existing organizing principles and can hinder when successful performance requires a different relational structure.

Firm boundaries and the comparison with TCE

The make-or-buy discussion asks how good a firm is at performing an activity, how good it is at learning the relevant capabilities, and how valuable those capabilities may become as platforms for other markets. The authors reinterpret earlier studies and propose four boundary propositions. They expressly acknowledge that transaction hazards can matter while arguing that opportunism is unnecessary for their knowledge-transfer explanation. (pp. 394–396.)

Evidence limitation. Reinterpreting a firm dummy or a production-cost advantage does not isolate the knowledge mechanism. Differences may also reflect past governance choices, selection, demand or unmeasured contractual problems. The empirical library’s capability–governance studies are useful precisely because they attempt to specify and compare these mechanisms more closely.

Compare with Grant and Nelson–Winter

Why does history matter? Learning builds on current relationships and knowledge Nelson–Winter explain persistence through memory, routines and internal truces.

Our synthesis. The most useful classroom disagreement concerns the explanatory work done by organization itself. If a coordination mechanism can operate in an alliance, when does it require a single firm? The paper provides propositions and a social account of productive competence; a comparative governance argument still needs to explain when markets, collaborations and common ownership differ in the relevant respect.
