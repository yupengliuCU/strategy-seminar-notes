Dynamic Capabilities and Strategic Management

How do firms build, renew and protect competitive advantage under rapid technological change?

The question of renewal

Author’s argument. Teece, Pisano and Shuen ask how firms create and protect wealth under rapid technological change. Possessing valuable assets is insufficient if a firm cannot coordinate, develop and reconfigure the competences needed in a changing environment. The paper defines dynamic capabilities through those activities and presents a preliminary framework around processes, positions and paths. (pp. 509–518.)

The framework’s overview explicitly identifies Schumpeter, Penrose, Nelson–Winter and other contributors as foundations. Williamson appears among the acknowledged theoretical foundations, and governance considerations enter the substantive argument. A genealogy that depicts dynamic capabilities simply as a rejection of organizational economics would lose this textual connection. (pp. 510, 515, 517, 522.)

Processes Ways of coordinating, learning and transforming Which observable practices perform these functions?
Positions Technological, complementary, financial, reputational, structural and other assets What does the firm have access to, and which assets are difficult to trade?
Paths Historically shaped possibilities and constraints Which alternatives are feasible given prior investments and knowledge?

The first two columns summarize pp. 518–524; the third is our operational interpretation. The framework makes capability development dependent on a firm’s current organization and history. It also allows external relationships, institutions and complementary assets to shape what it can do.

Three roles of organizational processes

Coordination and integration concern the combination of activities within and across firms. Learning concerns improved performance and the discovery of further opportunities through repetition and experimentation. Reconfiguration and transformation concern changing the asset structure and the organization when conditions call for it. (pp. 518–521.)

The authors emphasize complementarity among practices and incentives. Partial copying of a coherent system may yield little benefit when the copied practice relies on others that remain absent. Change can require adjustments across multiple organizational layers or external relationships. Their discussion of high- and low-powered incentives also shows why productive knowledge and governance should be studied together. (pp. 517, 519–520.)

Inherited assets and pathsCoordination, learning and reconfigurationCompetences and product performanceDemand, imitation and appropriabilityEconomic returns

Our synthesis. Each link should be measured separately where possible. A successful product can reflect favorable demand, complementary assets or a protected position as well as an effective change process. Calling every success a dynamic capability would make those alternatives difficult to distinguish.

Replication, imitation and value

Replication transfers or redeploys competence in another setting. It can require moving people because knowledge is embodied in practices and relationships. Imitation is replication by a competitor; emulation can reproduce functionality through another means. Sustainability depends on these processes together with whether the competence remains valuable to customers. (pp. 524–526.)

The paper’s appropriability discussion joins technical replication difficulty with intellectual-property protection. This supplies a connection to Week 3: legal control can affect the returns to productive knowledge, while the difficulty of transferring that knowledge remains a distinct consideration.

This paraphrases the visually inspected Table 1 on p. 527. It is the authors’ characterization of those approaches, not a neutral replacement for reading each tradition. Their conclusion allows both complementarities and disagreements among frameworks and argues that the appropriate tool depends on the problem. (pp. 526–530.)

Later debates and empirical limits

The 1997 article is a conceptual framework supported by prior research and illustrations. The authors call for theoretical refinement and empirical research. Eisenhardt and Martin later emphasize identifiable processes and commonalities; Winter distinguishes ordinary capabilities, dynamic capabilities and ad hoc problem solving; Helfat and Peteraf examine capability lifecycles. The library keeps these refinements separate so that their definitions can be compared.

Our assessment. Useful tests should identify a change process before observing its consequences, account for the cost of maintaining it, and specify the environmental conditions under which it helps. Process-level and firm-level outcomes need not move together. This is why the evidence page preserves mixed results and does not treat every positive capability–performance association as a test of the full framework.

For discussion, trace one change from the inherited asset base through the process that modifies it to the relevant outcome. Use the 1997 vocabulary when reconstructing this article; later formulations such as sensing and seizing belong to later developments and should be attributed separately.
