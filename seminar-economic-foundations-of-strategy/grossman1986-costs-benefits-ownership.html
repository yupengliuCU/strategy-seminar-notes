The Costs and Benefits of Ownership: A Theory of Vertical and Lateral Integration

作者： Sanford J. Grossman (Princeton University) & Oliver D. Hart (Massachusetts Institute of Technology)
期刊： Journal of Political Economy, Vol. 94, No. 4, pp. 691–719
年份： 1986
DOI： 10.1086/261404
性质： Formal two-period game-theoretic model（两期博弈论正式模型：不完全契约 incomplete contracts、现代企业产权理论 modern property-rights theory of the firm），并附对保险分销（insurance distribution）的非正式行业应用
关键词： residual rights of control; specific vs. residual contractual rights; incomplete contracts; relationship-specific (ex ante) investment; hold-up; vertical & lateral integration; ownership allocation

一句话总结

所有权是对资产剩余控制权（residual control rights）的购买，因此一体化（integration）只是把控制权从一方再配置给另一方；它应当给予关系专用性投资（relationship-specific investment）最重要的一方——用该方更强的激励换取另一方更弱的激励。

本文是课程第三周线索中现代产权理论（modern property-rights theory, PRT）的奠基性正式表述：它接续关注监督与计量（monitoring and metering）的古典产权理论（Alchian & Demsetz 1972），把"谁是监督团队生产（team production）的剩余索取者（residual claimant）？"换成"契约不完全（incomplete contracts）时谁应拥有哪些资产？"。它提供了剩余控制权（residual control rights）框架——战略管理应用由此推出企业边界（firm-boundary）与所有权能力（ownership-competence）的论证（Kim & Mahoney 2005 及所有权能力文献流），也产生了后来实证工作（如 Magelssen 2020）检验的配置规则。把它读作交易成本经济学（transaction-cost economics）与产权方法之间的枢纽：一体化既非免费，也非自动有益——它是对控制权的一次再配置，且成本在两侧都存在。

1. 背景与动机（Background and motivation）

本文攻击的问题是交易成本经济学（transaction-cost economics，TCE）提出过、但在 Grossman 和 Hart 看来从未回答的问题：企业是什么？其活动的纵向或横向一体化程度由什么决定？两位作者明确立足于 Coase (1937)、Klein, Crawford, and Alchian (1978) 和 Williamson (1979)——这些文献都强调，当完全契约（complete contracts）成本高昂或无法写定时"控制（control）"的收益。他们的诊断是：这一传统留有两个未解决的缺口。

第一个缺口在于：交易成本论证解释了独立企业之间的市场缔约为何成本高昂，却没有解释一体化为何能修复它。如果一体化总能降低交易成本，任何买方与卖方都能靠一种机械式的重组改善处境：买下对方，把原有契约当作内部转移价格（transfer price）保留，再让被收购方的经理拿子公司的利润作为报酬。TCE 中没有解释这种"复制"为何行不通——这其实就是"什么限制了企业规模"这一问题的另一种问法。

"Furthermore, if vertical integration always reduces transaction costs, any buyer A and seller B that have a contractual relationship should be able to make themselves better off as follows: (i) A buys B and makes the previous owner of B the manager of a new subsidiary; (ii) A sets a transfer price between the subsidiary and itself equal to the contract price that existed when the firms were separate enterprises; and (iii) A gives the manager of B a compensation package equal to the profit of the subsidiary. Given this, however, how can integration ever be strictly worse than nonintegration; that is, what limits the size of the firm?"

— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 693, §I.A（对一体化交易成本论点的批评；另见 Evans & Grossman 1983, fn. 1）

中文复述：进一步说，如果纵向一体化总能降低交易成本，任何存在契约关系的买方 A 与卖方 B 都应能按以下方式改善自身处境：(i) A 买下 B，让 B 的原所有者出任新子公司的经理；(ii) A 在子公司与自身之间设定一个转移价格，等于两家企业独立时的原契约价格；(iii) A 给 B 的经理一份等于子公司利润的报酬包。既然如此，一体化怎么可能严格劣于非一体化？也就是说，是什么限制了企业规模？

第二个缺口是定义性的。"一体化"没有被清楚地定义：把零售队伍称作"雇员（employees）"的企业，是否比使用"独立但专属的销售代理人（independent but exclusive sales agents）"的企业更一体化？Grossman 和 Hart 用一个资产检验越过这些标签——重要的是谁拥有生产性资产，而不是各方被称作什么。在保险零售业中，雇员型代理人（employee-agent）与独立代理人（independent agent）的差别只在于：雇员不拥有自己的客户名单，独立代理人则拥有；一家"直接承保人（direct writer）"只要拥有客户名单和代理人的其他重要资产，就和雇用自己销售队伍的公司一样一体化。Chandler (1977) 描述的制鞋业从"发料外包"（putting-out）转向工厂制的转变说明的是同一点：即使计件工资完全相同，工厂也更一体化，因为工厂主拥有机器。界定企业的是所有权（ownership），而不是报酬方式。

2. 核心论证与框架（Core argument and framework）

2.1 特定权利与剩余权利（Specific rights versus residual rights）

核心的概念动作是把契约权利分成两类。特定权利（specific rights）是契约授予的、针对另一方资产或行动逐项列明的权利。剩余控制权则是其余的一切：决定资产一切未被契约明确让渡之用途的权利。由于逐条列出覆盖每种未来偶发事件的特定权利成本高昂，一方可以改买"契约特别提及者之外的一切权利"——而这笔购买就是所有权。

"This is the simplest possible illustration of our assumption that the owner of an asset has the residual rights of control of that asset, that is, the right to control all aspects of the asset that have not been explicitly given away by contract."

— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 695, §I.B（出版商—印刷商例子：当一次未预见的二次加印变得有利可图时，决定权属于印刷机的所有者）

中文复述：这是对我们假设的最简单说明：资产所有者拥有该资产的剩余控制权，即控制该资产一切未经契约明确让渡之方面的权利。

剩余权利正是一体化并不免费的原因。所有权权利是零和的：一方购入剩余权利，另一方就失去它们——论文开篇即说这"必然造成扭曲（inevitably creates distortions）"。失去控制权的一方也失去了对事后剩余的议价能力，因此每一笔收购都带有一侧的成本，这正是论文其余部分要精确化的内容。

2.2 模型（The model，论文 Section II）

正式模型有两个企业、两个时期。在标准的纵向解读中，企业 1 在下游、企业 2 在上游（企业 2 向企业 1 供应一种投入品）；同样的结构也涵盖横向（lateral）关系，例如两家相邻的零售店。每个企业由一名经理经营，经理获得其企业活动的全部回报——一种私人的、不可验证（nonverifiable）的收益 $B_i[a_i, \phi_i(q_1,q_2)]$（管理者的在职消费或努力），它从不进入账目，因此无法写进契约。

时间线承载了理论的全部分量：

  • 日期 0（Date 0）。 契约只能做一件实质性的事：配置两家企业资产的所有权（剩余控制权），外加一笔一次性转移支付（lump-sum transfer）。$a_i$、$q_i$、$B_i$ 中任何一项都无法事前（ex ante）缔约——投资要么过于复杂而难以描述，要么是不可验证的管理努力；各 $q$ 也无法针对每一种世界状态事先写定。
  • 日期 0（缔约之后）。 经理 1 与经理 2 同时、非合作地选择各自的关系专用性投资 $a_1, a_2$；到日期 1 投资即沉没（sunk）。
  • 日期 1。 状态实现，相关的生产选择变得清晰，$q_1, q_2$ 变为事后（ex post）可缔约。在默认（不重谈 renegotiation）结果中，每项资产的所有者选择他所控制的 $q$。由于信息对称且重谈无成本，双方总会重谈至一个事后有效（ex post efficient）的配置，并按 50:50 分割增益（纳什谈判 Nash bargaining）。

两个选择值得强调。其一，谈判中的分歧点（disagreement point）是非合作结果——每个所有者最大化自己的 $\phi_i$；所有权只经由这个分歧点、从而经由剩余的分割产生影响。其二，在无成本重谈与对称信息下根本不存在事后无效率：给定投资，任何所有权结构都产生有效配置，因此全部福利损失都是事前（ex ante）的投资扭曲。这就是论文所说的"套牢（hold-up）"的含义：每个投资者预期只能从自己沉没投资的边际剩余中拿回谈判所得的那一份，于是投资低于第一最优（first best）——这不是因为一件已完成的资产被攫取（Klein, Crawford, and Alchian 1978 的可占用准租金 appropriable quasi-rents 故事），而是因为剩余将在日后被分割。

"The inefficiency arises, then, because manager i puts 50 percent weight on the noncooperative outcome q, which is generally ex post inefficient, instead of all the weight on the cooperative outcome, which is ex post efficient; this is in spite of the fact that the noncooperative outcome never occurs!"

— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 703, §III.A（投资不足机制；参 Grout 1984）

中文复述：无效率之所以产生，是因为经理 i 把 50% 的权重放在一般事后无效率的非合作结果 q 上，而不是把全部权重放在事后有效的合作结果上——尽管非合作结果从未真正出现。

不完全性假设是承重的。如果各 $q$ 事前可缔约，那么即使投资不可缔约，任何所有权结构下都能达到第一最优（固定 $q_i = q_i^*$，每个经理都会正确投资）；如果收益 $B_i$ 可转移（transferable），一切利益冲突都会消失。所有权之所以有效率内容，仅仅因为缔约是不完全的——这正是结论部分追问"Coase 定理的哪条假设被放弃"时所指出的要点。

2.3 三种所有权结构与主要结果（The three ownership structures and the main results，论文 Section III）

既然日期 0 的契约只是所有权配置加一笔转移支付，三种结构就值得关注：非一体化（nonintegration，即每个经理拥有并控制自己企业的资产）、企业 1 控制（firm 1 control，经理 1 拥有两家企业的资产）与企业 2 控制（firm 2 control，镜像情形）——在纵向解读中即第一类与第二类一体化：买方拥有其供应商，或供应商拥有买方。第四种情形——交叉持股（cross-ownership）以及随信息而定的"选择权式拥有（option to own）"契约——被搁置为次优方案（fn. 13, p. 701）。

所有权经由一条单调性渠道影响激励。给定假设 5（Assumption 5：平均收益高时边际收益也高），一位因所有权改善了分歧点、从而在事后剩余中占有更大份额的经理会相对第一最优过度投资（overinvest），而失去控制权的经理则投资不足（underinvest）。于是企业 1 控制使经理 1 过度投资、经理 2 投资不足；企业 2 控制则相反；非一体化让双方都保有一定权力，产生温和的——通常偏低的——投资。有效的结构使这些扭曲造成的总损失最小。

"Firm 1 control will be desirable when firm 1's ex ante investment is much more important than firm 2's (so that firm 2's underinvestment under firm 1 control is relatively unimportant) and when overinvestment by firm 1 under firm 1 control is a less severe problem than underinvestment by firm 1 as in, for example, the nonintegrated solution. … Finally, nonintegration is desirable if a1 and a2 are both "important" in some sense, so that it is preferable to have both of them at a medium level than to have one very high and the other very low as under integration."

— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 708, §III（核心权衡的口头表述）

中文复述：当企业 1 的事前投资远比企业 2 的重要（于是企业 1 控制下企业 2 的投资不足相对无足轻重），且企业 1 控制下经理 1 的过度投资不像非一体化等方案中经理 1 的投资不足那样严重时，企业 1 控制是可取的。……最后，若 $a_1$ 与 $a_2$ 在某种意义上都"重要"，让两者都处于中等水平就优于一方极高、另一方极低（如一体化之下那样），此时非一体化是可取的。

正式表述是命题 1（Proposition 1）。若企业 1 的收益主要取决于自己的 $q_1$，企业 2 的收益主要取决于 $q_2$，非一体化就接近第一最优，因为每个所有者的单边选择几乎事后有效。若企业 2 几乎不在乎那些不可缔约变量（其 $\phi_2$ 在 $q_1, q_2$ 上近乎常数），企业 1 控制接近第一最优；对称地，当企业 1 的收益几乎不依赖各 $q$ 时，企业 2 控制接近第一最优。组织原则是：如果不可缔约变量对企业 j 无关紧要，j 失去对它们的控制也无妨——j 的投资保持无扭曲，而若由 j 拥有它们，反而会扭曲真正在乎它们那一方的投资。 注记 1（Remark 1）处理一名经理无效率损失地经营两家企业的情形（$B_2 \approx 0$：互补性活动与闲置产能，见 Aron 1984 与 Mann & Wissink 1984）；此时企业 1 控制占优。注记 2（Remark 2）陈述人力资本边界：当控制的一个子向量必须留在被收购的经理手中（只有他能行使）时——买下律师事务所的律师客户便是如此——所有权的价值甚微。

过度投资效应使模型真正具有两面性。即使企业 2 的投资无关紧要（$\partial B_2 / \partial a_2 = 0$），企业 1 控制也不是自动第一最优的，因为控制可能使企业 1 过度投资。只有当企业 2 的收益完全可让渡（alienable）——经理 1 可在日期 1 解雇并替换经理 2，从而取走 $B_2$——过度投资才消失，企业 1 控制才达到第一最优。不可让渡的私人收益（inalienable private benefits）是所有权的成本一面的来源。

2.4 为什么"更大可行集"论证会失败（Why the "larger feasible set" argument fails）

受过标准契约理论训练的读者可能反驳：收购一个企业只会扩大可行集（feasible set），因此一体化应当弱占优（weakly dominate）。模型说明了这为何失败。收购方的所有者获得了以种种方式干预子公司、从而扭曲子公司经理激励的权力，而且——关键地——所有者无法承诺有选择地干预，因为剩余权利按定义就是无法事先以可执行的细节加以规定的权力。因此一体化既带来收益也施加成本；只要双方的投资与收益都随控制权的配置而变化，第一最优就不可企及。

3. 证据与推演实例（Evidence and worked examples）

3.1 模型内的推演实例（Worked examples inside the model）

作为一篇理论论文，第三部分（Section III）的"证据"是固定机制含义的推演性案例。出版商—印刷商（publisher–printer）案例定义剩余权利：命令一次未预见的加印的权利属于印刷机的所有者。坑口电厂（mine-mouth power plant）案例（沿 Joskow 1985 对长期煤炭契约的研究）说明不可缔约者如何到事后才变得可缔约：煤的潜在杂质多到无法事前逐一列出，但一旦实现，相关的那一种（高灰分 high ash）就清楚了——拥有煤矿使电厂得以指定低灰煤，拥有电厂则使煤矿得以指定锅炉改造。解雇与替换（firing-and-replacement）案例（p. 709）说明可让渡性（alienability）：拥有资产使收购方能够通过替换把目标经理的私人收益取走，让一体化逼近"一个拥有社会目标函数的经理"。律师事务所（law firm）例子（注记 2, p. 709）划定理论的边界：当一项资产的价值寓于某个人身上时，买下资产并不能买下那个人的裁量权，所有权权利的价值甚微。

3.2 应用：保险业中谁拥有客户名单（Application: who owns the client list in insurance，论文 Section IV）

第四部分（Section IV）把框架应用于单一资产的场景，在其中非一体化没有意义：选择恰恰是保单持有人名单归公司控制还是归代理人控制。在财产—意外险与寿险中，"直接承保人"（雇员或专属代理人 captive agents）不拥有保单持有人名单；独立代理人（independent agents）拥有。名单所有权决定关系终止时谁能接触客户、谁能拿到续保（renewal）。模型的逻辑经由佣金结构运行：由于代理人"带来持久客户"的努力不可验证，有效的佣金必须后置（back-loaded）——首期佣金低于获客成本，续期佣金高于服务成本。只有没有任何其他因素能干扰续保现金流时，后置才无害；有两类不可缔约因素可以干扰：

  • 第一类——公司拥有名单时损害代理人的行动。 公司可以降低自身产品的竞争力（提价、在某地区削减服务质量、改变广告），使客户流失，毁掉代理人后置的续保租金。若代理人拥有名单，他可以把客户带到匹配更好的公司；若公司拥有名单，代理人事前生产持久客户的努力就被钝化。
  • 第二类——代理人拥有名单时损害公司的行动。 公司向代理人的客户推介新开发的产品前必须征得代理人同意；在把客户转给竞争对手只是把剩余从公司转移到代理人的状态里，代理人会这么做。这扭曲了公司在名单建设、产品开发与保单持有人服务上的事前投资。
"Our analysis therefore predicts that, in products in which the renewal is not guaranteed and is sensitive to the agent's actions, the agent will be more likely to own the list, whereas in products in which the renewal is more certain and is less sensitive to the agent's actions, the company will be more likely to own the list."

— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 714, §IV（把预测带到保险数据上）

中文复述：我们的分析因此预测：在续保没有保证、且对代理人行动敏感的险种中，代理人更可能拥有名单；而在续保更确定、对代理人行动不那么敏感的险种中，公司更可能拥有名单。

3.3 对证据的评注（Commentary on the evidence）

保险材料刻意是非正式的——模型被"相当宽松地（with considerable latitude）"应用，因为现实中有些变量可缔约；理论也"缺乏定量完备性（lacks quantitative completeness）"（pp. 697, 710）。证据是相关性的、产品层面的：保费份额、经纪销售比例与 Marvel 的跨产品相关性——没有回归、没有控制变量、没有识别策略来把激励渠道与竞争性解释分开。这些数字与理论一致——论文也只主张这些；它们并没有在统计意义上让理论与 TCE 对检。在论文自身的话语内，证据是一种可信性演示（plausibility demonstration）：随着续保确定性与努力敏感性在不同产品间变化，客户名单所有权的归属沿投资激励逻辑所要求的方向移动。

4. 在文献中的位置（Position in the literature）

Grossman and Hart (1986) 是现代产权理论的奠基论文，其地位体现在它与之对话的文献上。它拓展了 Coase (1937)，给"企业的性质"一个 TCE 所缺乏的定义——企业是一个所有权单位所控制的一组资产；它也拓展了 Coase (1960)，说明即使事后谈判被允许且无成本，只要契约不完全，产权的事前配置仍有效率后果。它精炼了 Klein, Crawford, and Alchian (1978)：套牢仍源于关系专用性投资，但对象从"事后被攫取的准租金"变为"因预期到剩余分割而产生的事前投资扭曲"。它回应 Williamson (1971, 1979, 1983) 及周边 TCE 线索（Williamson, Wachter, and Harris 1975; Teece 1980），否定了"内部组织近似完全契约结果"这一隐含前提。相对把企业定位于"计量团队生产的剩余索取者"的古典产权理论（Alchian & Demsetz 1972），Grossman 和 Hart 把戏份移到两位拥有不可让渡私人收益的所有者—经理（owner-manager）之间、围绕资产的剩余控制之上。

6. 结论（Conclusion）

论文确立了三点。第一，一个定义：企业是它所拥有的一组资产，所有权是对剩余控制权的购买——决定资产一切未被契约明确让渡之用途的权利。第二，一个机制：因为契约不完全，剩余权利决定各方在日期 1 的谈判地位，并经由它决定各方在日期 0 做关系专用性投资的激励；因此即便事后谈判是完美的，所有权配置仍有真实的效率后果。第三，一条内含权衡的规范规则：当一方投资具有关键性时一体化——集中剩余控制会强化该方的激励，同时削弱另一方的；当双方投资都重要、都应保持在中等水平时，保持非一体化。

"We argue that the relevant comparison is not between the nonintegrated outcome and the complete contract outcome but instead between a contract that allocates residual rights to one party and a contract that allocates them to another. … Integration shifts the incentives for opportunistic and distortionary behavior, but it does not remove these incentives."

— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 716, §V（结论 Conclusions）

The Costs and Benefits of Ownership: A Theory of Vertical and Lateral Integration

Authors: Sanford J. Grossman (Princeton University) & Oliver D. Hart (Massachusetts Institute of Technology)
Journal: Journal of Political Economy, Vol. 94, No. 4, pp. 691–719
Year: 1986
DOI: 10.1086/261404
Nature: Formal two-period game-theoretic model (incomplete contracts; modern property-rights theory of the firm), with an informal industry application to insurance distribution
Keywords: residual rights of control; specific vs. residual contractual rights; incomplete contracts; relationship-specific (ex ante) investment; hold-up; vertical & lateral integration; ownership allocation

One-sentence summary

Ownership is the purchase of residual rights of control over assets, so integration reallocates control from one party to another and should be assigned to the party whose relationship-specific investment matters most, trading that party's stronger incentive against the other party's weaker one.

Reading note. This paper is the foundational formal statement of modern property-rights theory (PRT) in the course's Week-3 arc: it follows the classical PRT of monitoring and metering (Alchian & Demsetz 1972) by replacing the question "who is the residual claimant that monitors team production?" with "who should own which assets when contracts are incomplete?" It supplies the residual-control-rights framework that strategic-management applications translate into firm-boundary and ownership-competence arguments (Kim & Mahoney 2005 and the ownership-competence stream), and it generates the allocation rule that later empirical work (e.g., Magelssen 2020) tests. Read it as the hinge between transaction-cost economics and the property-rights approach: integration is neither free nor automatically beneficial — it is a reallocation of control with costs on both sides.

1. Background and motivation

The paper attacks a question that transaction-cost economics (TCE) had posed but, in Grossman and Hart's judgment, never answered: what is a firm, and what determines how vertically or laterally integrated its activities are? The authors build explicitly on Coase (1937), Klein, Crawford, and Alchian (1978), and Williamson (1979), all of which emphasize the benefit of "control" when complete contracts are costly or impossible to write. Their diagnosis is that this tradition has two unresolved gaps.

The first gap is that transactions-cost arguments explain why market contracting between separately owned firms can be costly, but not why integration fixes it. If integration always reduced transaction costs, any buyer and seller could improve their position by a mechanical reorganization: buy the partner, keep the old contract as an internal transfer price, and pay the acquired manager the subsidiary's profit. Nothing in TCE explains why this replication fails — which is another way of asking what limits the size of the firm.

"Furthermore, if vertical integration always reduces transaction costs, any buyer A and seller B that have a contractual relationship should be able to make themselves better off as follows: (i) A buys B and makes the previous owner of B the manager of a new subsidiary; (ii) A sets a transfer price between the subsidiary and itself equal to the contract price that existed when the firms were separate enterprises; and (iii) A gives the manager of B a compensation package equal to the profit of the subsidiary. Given this, however, how can integration ever be strictly worse than nonintegration; that is, what limits the size of the firm?"
— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 693, §I.A (critique of transactions-cost arguments for integration; see also Evans & Grossman 1983, fn. 1)

The second gap is definitional. "Integration" is not clearly defined: is a firm that calls its retail force "employees" more integrated than one that uses "independent but exclusive sales agents"? Grossman and Hart cut through the labels with an asset test — what matters is who owns the productive assets, not what the parties are called. In insurance retailing, an employee-agent differs from an independent agent only because the employee does not own his list of clients while the independent agent does; a "direct writer" that owned the list and the agents' other important assets would be exactly as integrated as one employing its sales force. Chandler's (1977) putting-out-to-factory shift in shoemaking makes the same point: even under identical piece rates, the factory is more integrated because the factory owner owns the machines. Ownership, not compensation method, defines the firm.

2. Core argument and framework

2.1 Specific rights versus residual rights

The central conceptual move is to divide contractual rights into two types. Specific rights are the particular, enumerated rights a contract grants over another party's assets or actions. Residual rights of control are everything left over: the right to decide all uses of an asset that the contract has not explicitly assigned away. Because it is costly to write a long list of specific rights covering every future contingency, a party may instead buy "all the rights except those specifically mentioned in the contract" — and that purchase is ownership.

"This is the simplest possible illustration of our assumption that the owner of an asset has the residual rights of control of that asset, that is, the right to control all aspects of the asset that have not been explicitly given away by contract."
— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 695, §I.B (the publisher–printer example: when an unanticipated second print run becomes profitable, the right to decide belongs to the owner of the printing press)

Residual rights are why integration is not free. Ownership rights are zero-sum: when one party purchases residual rights, the other loses them — the paper's opening words are that this "inevitably creates distortions." The party who loses control also loses bargaining power over the ex post surplus, so every acquisition carries a cost side that the rest of the paper makes precise.

2.2 The model (Section II)

The formal model has two firms and two periods. Firm 1 is downstream and firm 2 upstream in the standard vertical reading (firm 2 supplies an input to firm 1); the same structure covers a lateral relationship such as two adjacent retail stores. Each firm is run by a single manager who receives the full return of his firm's activities — a private, nonverifiable benefit $B_i[a_i, \phi_i(q_1,q_2)]$ (managerial perquisites or effort) that never appears in the accounts and hence cannot be contracted over.

The timeline carries all of the theory's weight:

  • Date 0. The contract can do only one substantive thing: allocate ownership (residual rights of control) over the two firms' assets, plus make a lump-sum transfer. None of $a_i$, $q_i$, or $B_i$ is ex ante contractible — the investments are too complex to describe or are unverifiable managerial effort, and the $q$'s cannot be specified in advance for every state of the world.
  • Date 0 (after contracting). Managers 1 and 2 choose their relationship-specific investments $a_1, a_2$ simultaneously and noncooperatively; the investments are sunk by date 1.
  • Date 1. The state is realized, the relevant production choices become clear, and $q_1, q_2$ become ex post contractible. In the default (no renegotiation) outcome, each asset's owner chooses the $q$'s he controls. Because information is symmetric and renegotiation is costless, the parties always renegotiate to an ex post efficient allocation, splitting the gains 50:50 (Nash bargaining).

Two choices deserve emphasis. First, the disagreement point in bargaining is the noncooperative outcome in which each owner maximizes his own $\phi_i$; ownership matters only through this disagreement point and hence the division of surplus. Second, with costless renegotiation and symmetric information there is no ex post inefficiency at all: conditional on investments, every ownership structure yields an efficient allocation, so all welfare loss is an ex ante investment distortion. That is the paper's meaning of "hold-up": each investor anticipates capturing only his bargaining share of the marginal surplus from his own sunk investment, so investment falls short of the first best — not because a finished asset is expropriated (the appropriable-quasi-rents story of Klein, Crawford, and Alchian 1978), but because the surplus will be divided later.

"The inefficiency arises, then, because manager i puts 50 percent weight on the noncooperative outcome q, which is generally ex post inefficient, instead of all the weight on the cooperative outcome, which is ex post efficient; this is in spite of the fact that the noncooperative outcome never occurs!"
— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 703, §III.A (the underinvestment mechanism; cf. Grout 1984)

The incompleteness assumptions are load-bearing. If the $q$'s were ex ante contractible, the first best would be reachable under any ownership structure even with noncontractible investments (fix $q_i = q_i^*$ and each manager invests correctly); if the benefits $B_i$ were transferable, all conflicts of interest would vanish. Ownership has efficiency content only because contracting is incomplete — the point the conclusion presses when it asks which Coase-theorem assumption has been dropped.

2.3 The three ownership structures and the main results (Section III)

Given that the date-0 contract is just an ownership allocation plus a transfer, three configurations are interesting: nonintegration (each manager owns and controls his own firm's assets), firm 1 control (manager 1 owns both firms' assets), and firm 2 control (the mirror image) — in the vertical reading, type-1 and type-2 integration: the buyer owns its supplier, or the supplier owns the buyer. A fourth case of cross-ownership, and message-contingent "option to own" contracts, are set aside as inferior (fn. 13, p. 701).

Ownership affects incentives through a monotonicity channel. Given Assumption 5 (marginal benefit is high when average benefit is high), a manager who commands a larger share of ex post surplus because ownership improves his disagreement point overinvests relative to the first best, while the manager who lost control underinvests. Firm 1 control thus makes manager 1 overinvest and manager 2 underinvest; firm 2 control does the reverse; nonintegration leaves both parties some power and produces moderate — typically too low — investment by each. The efficient structure minimizes the total loss from these distortions.

"Firm 1 control will be desirable when firm 1's ex ante investment is much more important than firm 2's (so that firm 2's underinvestment under firm 1 control is relatively unimportant) and when overinvestment by firm 1 under firm 1 control is a less severe problem than underinvestment by firm 1 as in, for example, the nonintegrated solution. … Finally, nonintegration is desirable if a1 and a2 are both "important" in some sense, so that it is preferable to have both of them at a medium level than to have one very high and the other very low as under integration."
— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 708, §III (the central trade-off, stated verbally)

The formal statement is Proposition 1. If firm 1's benefit depends primarily on its own $q_1$ and firm 2's primarily on $q_2$, nonintegration approximates the first best, since each owner's unilateral choice is nearly ex post efficient. If firm 2 barely cares about the noncontractibles (its $\phi_2$ is nearly constant in $q_1, q_2$), firm 1 control approximates the first best; symmetrically, firm 2 control does so when firm 1's benefit hardly depends on the $q$'s. The organizing principle: if the noncontractibles matter little to firm j, j can safely lose control of them — j's investment stays undistorted, while j's ownership would distort the investment of the party who cares. Remark 1 covers one manager running both firms with no loss of efficiency ($B_2 \approx 0$: complementary activities and spare capacity, per Aron 1984 and Mann & Wissink 1984); firm 1 control then dominates. Remark 2 states the human-capital limit: when a subvector of control stays with the acquired manager because only he can exercise it — the lawyer whose client buys the law firm — ownership is worth little.

The overinvestment effect makes the model genuinely two-sided. Even when firm 2's investment is irrelevant ($\partial B_2 / \partial a_2 = 0$), firm 1 control is not automatically first-best, because control may make firm 1 overinvest. Only if firm 2's benefits are perfectly alienable — manager 1 can fire and replace manager 2 at date 1, capturing $B_2$ — does overinvestment vanish and firm 1 control attain the first best. Inalienable private benefits are the source of the cost side of ownership.

2.4 Why the "larger feasible set" argument fails

A reader trained on standard contract theory might object that acquiring a firm can only enlarge the feasible set, so integration should weakly dominate. The model shows why this fails. The acquiring owner gains the power to intervene in the subsidiary in ways that distort the subsidiary manager's incentives, and — critically — the owner cannot commit to intervene selectively, because residual rights are by definition powers that cannot be specified in advance in enforceable detail. Integration therefore imposes costs as well as benefits, and the first-best is unattainable whenever both parties' investments and benefits respond to the allocation of control.

3. Evidence and worked examples

3.1 Worked examples inside the model

As a theory paper, Section III's "evidence" is worked cases that fix the mechanism's meaning. The publisher–printer case defines residual rights: the right to order an unanticipated extra print run belongs to the owner of the press. The mine-mouth power plant case (following Joskow 1985 on long-term coal contracts) shows the noncontractible becoming contractible ex post: too many potential coal impurities to list ex ante, but once realized the relevant one (high ash) is clear — owning the mine lets the plant direct low-ash coal; owning the plant lets the mine direct boiler modification. The firing-and-replacement case (p. 709) illustrates alienability: owning assets lets the acquirer transfer the target manager's private benefit by replacement, making integration approach "one manager with the social objective function." The law firm example (Remark 2, p. 709) bounds the theory: when an asset's value lives in a person, buying the asset does not buy the person's discretion, and ownership rights are worth little.

3.2 Application: who owns the client list in insurance (Section IV)

Section IV applies the framework to a single-asset setting in which nonintegration has no meaning: the choice is exactly company control versus agent control of the policyholder list. In property-casualty and life insurance, "direct writers" (employees or captive agents) do not own the list of policyholders; independent agents do. List ownership determines who can sell to the client if the relationship ends and who captures the renewal. The model's logic runs through the commission structure: because the agent's effort to deliver persistent clients is nonverifiable, the efficient commission must be back-loaded — initial commissions below acquisition cost, renewal commissions above servicing cost. Back-loading is harmless only if nothing else can interfere with the renewal stream; two families of noncontractibles can:

  • Type 1 — actions that hurt the agent when the company owns the list. The company can make its product less competitive (raise price, cut service quality in a region, change advertising), making clients switch and destroying the agent's back-loaded renewal rents. If the agent owned the list he could move the client to a better-matched company; if the company owns it, the agent's ex ante effort to produce persistent clients is blunted.
  • Type 2 — actions that hurt the company when the agent owns the list. The agent must consent before the company can solicit his clients for a newly developed product, and the agent can switch clients to competitors in states where the switch merely transfers surplus from company to agent. This distorts the company's ex ante investments in list building, product development, and policyholder services.
"Our analysis therefore predicts that, in products in which the renewal is not guaranteed and is sensitive to the agent's actions, the agent will be more likely to own the list, whereas in products in which the renewal is more certain and is less sensitive to the agent's actions, the company will be more likely to own the list."
— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 714, §IV (prediction taken to the insurance data)

The same pattern holds across property-casualty lines: independent (list-owning) agents hold 47 percent of private passenger automobile liability but 96 percent of surety insurance (Webb et al. 1984), and Marvel (1982) reports a positive correlation across products between the importance of independent agents and the size of the agent's client-acquisition costs. Automobile insurance is short-term with effort-sensitive renewals; surety is idiosyncratic and match-sensitive — both push ownership to the agent. The authors rebut Marvel's rival exclusive-dealing-and-advertising account (fn. 20, pp. 714–715): exclusive dealing is better read as an enforcement device for list ownership, and Marvel neither separates ownership of the renewal from exclusive dealing nor explains why life insurance uses captive agents far more than property-casualty insurance.

3.3 Commentary on the evidence

The insurance material is deliberately informal — the model is applied "with considerable latitude" because some variables are contractible in reality, and the theory "lacks quantitative completeness" (pp. 697, 710). The evidence is correlational and product-level: premium shares, brokerage-placement rates, and Marvel's cross-product correlation, with no regression, controls, or identification strategy to separate the incentive channel from rivals. The numbers are consistent with the theory, which is all the paper claims; they do not test it against TCE in any statistical sense. Within the paper's terms the evidence is a plausibility demonstration: ownership of the client list moves in the direction the investment-incentive logic requires as renewal certainty and effort sensitivity vary across products.

4. Position in the literature

Grossman and Hart (1986) is the founding paper of modern property-rights theory, and its place shows in the conversation it conducts. It extends Coase (1937) by giving "the nature of the firm" a definition TCE lacked — the firm is the set of assets one ownership unit controls — and Coase (1960) by showing that even with permitted, costless ex post bargaining, the ex ante allocation of property rights has efficiency consequences when contracts are incomplete. It refines Klein, Crawford, and Alchian (1978): hold-up still originates in relationship-specific investment, but the object shifts from quasi-rents expropriated ex post to the ex ante investment distortion from anticipated surplus division. It answers Williamson (1971, 1979, 1983) and the surrounding TCE line (Williamson, Wachter, and Harris 1975; Teece 1980) by denying the implicit premise that internal organization approximates the complete-contract outcome. Against classical PRT (Alchian & Demsetz 1972), which locates the firm in a residual claimant who meters team production, Grossman and Hart relocate the action to residual control over assets between two owner-managers with inalienable private benefits.

Within the PRT line, the paper's immediate predecessor is the authors' own 1984 mimeograph, "Vertical Integration and the Distribution of Property Rights," which this article extensively revises — and which, with Farrell (1985), develops the ex post-inefficiency variant (barriers to renegotiation). The direct descendants are Hart and Moore (1990), generalizing ownership to multi-asset, multi-agent settings, and Hart (1995), consolidating the property-rights approach and pushing it into financial structure — the investor-control agenda the conclusion sketches (p. 717). In strategy, the rule "give ownership to the party whose investment matters most" underlies applications to co-specialized assets and firm boundaries (Kim & Mahoney 2005), the ownership-competence idea that exercising residual control is itself a capability, and empirical tests such as Magelssen (2020). The symmetry claim is the contrast with Williamson that later literature quotes most: TCE says integration protects specific investments by removing opportunism; GH (1986) says integration shifts the incentives for opportunistic behavior from one party to the other.

5. Limitations

(a) The author's own stated limitations

The paper is unusually explicit about what it does not cover. The starkest admission concerns the very assumptions that make ownership matter:

"It is nonetheless very strong to assume that no aspects of these variables are contractible. In any realistic situation, some parts of a firm's performance will be reflected in verifiable shareholders' profit, even if other parts, such as managerial well-being, are not. … While we are confident that some version of our results will continue to hold when a1, q1, and B1 are partly contractible, the formal extension of our analysis to this case is by no means straightforward."
— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 700, end of §II

The remaining author-stated limits, all traceable to the text: (i) Assumption 7 — the ex post efficient choice of the $q$'s independent of the investments — is "a strong assumption," unreasonable when benefits are differentiable in the $q$'s, though it can hold with discrete $q$'s (pp. 706–707). (ii) If renegotiation were blocked by transaction costs or asymmetric information, residual rights would affect the size as well as the distribution of ex post surplus; the authors defer that analysis to Grossman and Hart (1984) and Farrell (1985) (p. 717). (iii) The relationship is once-and-for-all; repetition and reputation (Telser 1980; Kreps 1984) are set aside, with no clear implication of reputation for asset ownership (fn. 5, pp. 695–696). (iv) The manager-as-full-residual-claimant and inalienability assumptions are extreme; alienability is explored only via the special replacement case (p. 709). (v) Only simple allocations are considered — cross-ownership and option-to-own contracts are ignored, and the analysis covers two firms and assets whose control can transfer (fn. 13, p. 701; Remark 2, p. 709). (vi) The theory "lacks quantitative completeness," so the application is qualitative (p. 697); risk aversion and financing motives are acknowledged as additional unmodeled reasons residual rights matter (p. 717).

(b) Independent judgment

  • [paper claim] Under the model's assumptions (in particular monotonicity — Assumption 5, that marginal and average benefits of investment move together), firm i control makes firm i overinvest and firm j underinvest, while nonintegration produces moderate investment by both; the optimal structure minimizes the total ex ante investment distortion, and ownership should go to the party whose investment matters most. This is the paper's central assertion, not a commentator's gloss.
  • [paper claim] Because renegotiation is costless and information is symmetric, every ownership structure yields an ex post efficient allocation given investments; all welfare loss is ex ante, so ownership matters only through investment incentives. The authors state this repeatedly (pp. 703, 717) — but it describes an assumed environment, not a theorem about real transactions.
  • [evidence] The insurance patterns (65 vs. 12 percent list-owning-agent premium shares; Table 1's brokerage-placement rates; 47 vs. 96 percent agency shares; Marvel's correlation) are informal, cross-sectional, and correlational — consistent with the model's comparative statics but unable to rule out rivals such as Marvel's advertising/exclusive-dealing account, which the authors rebut on plausibility grounds rather than with data (fn. 20, pp. 714–715).
  • [evidence] The worked examples inside Sections I–III (publisher–printer, mine-mouth plant, firing-and-replacement, the law firm) function as plausibility demonstrations of the mechanism, not empirical evidence about real ownership patterns.
  • [inference] The model's welfare accounting excludes the ex post transaction costs that motivated TCE. Once renegotiation is costly or information asymmetric, integration can generate benefits — avoiding haggling and maladaptation — that lie outside the ex ante-investment metric, so the Grossman–Hart comparison understates what hierarchy can buy and what market contracting can lose. The authors gesture at this (p. 717) without bringing it into the formal model.
  • [inference] The sign of the investment response rests on monotonicity (marginal return rising with average return). If returns were non-monotone — control beyond some point breeding complacency, or marginal returns peaking at intermediate surplus shares — the "overinvest under control, underinvest without it" prescriptions could weaken or reverse, so the empirical content of "whose investment matters most" depends on a functional-form property the paper does not defend with evidence.
  • [open question] The two-firm, two-manager, single-relationship setting leaves unanswered the questions its successors took up: which party should own complementary assets in multi-asset settings (Hart & Moore 1990), and how residual rights operate over people and actions — the employer–employee versus contractor–contractee distinction the authors themselves flag as future work (p. 717) — rather than over assets alone.
  • [open question] How to observe residual rights empirically — what "ownership" means for intangibles, data, and human capital — and how to test the allocation rule against TCE in data remain unresolved; the authors offer no measurement strategy, and the task fell to later applied work.

6. Conclusion

The paper establishes three things. First, a definition: a firm is the set of assets it owns, and ownership is the purchase of residual rights of control — the right to decide all uses of an asset not explicitly assigned away in a contract. Second, a mechanism: because contracts are incomplete, residual rights determine each party's bargaining position at date 1, and through it each party's incentive to make relationship-specific investments at date 0; hence the allocation of ownership has real efficiency consequences even though ex post bargaining is perfect. Third, a normative rule with a built-in trade-off: integrate when one party's investment is pivotal, because concentrating residual control strengthens that party's incentive while weakening the other's; stay nonintegrated when both investments matter and should be kept at moderate levels.

"We argue that the relevant comparison is not between the nonintegrated outcome and the complete contract outcome but instead between a contract that allocates residual rights to one party and a contract that allocates them to another. … Integration shifts the incentives for opportunistic and distortionary behavior, but it does not remove these incentives."
— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 716, §V (Conclusions)
"The impossibility of ex ante bargaining over all aspects of the product to be delivered, that is, the incompleteness of the contract, is the source of our conclusion that the distribution of property rights has efficiency consequences."
— Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 718, §V (which Coase-theorem assumption is dropped)

One-line normative takeaway for strategy: identify whose relationship-specific investment creates the surplus, and put residual control — ownership — with that party, accepting that every act of integration buys one party's incentive at the price of the other's.

"When residual rights are purchased by one party, they are lost by a second party, and this inevitably creates distortions."
Grossman & Hart (1986), p. 691 — the sentence that turns integration from a one-way gain into a trade-off, and the reason this theory is called the costs and benefits of ownership.
