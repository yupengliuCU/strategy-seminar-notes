The search result in one paragraph

Within the strict 2015–2025 window, Academy of Management Annals contains exactly one TCE review — Cuypers, Hennart, Silverman & Ertug (2021). Journal of Management contains no review devoted to TCE in that window; its closest TCE-grounded review is Sørensen, Hoetker, Leiblein & Mellewigt (2023) on plural governance. Schepker, Oh, Martynov & Poppo (2014) — the standard JoM TCE-anchored contracts review — is included with an explicit flag: it appeared in January 2014, just outside the window.

3 · English analyses

3.1 Cuypers, Hennart, Silverman & Ertug (2021) — Academy of Management Annals, 15(1), 111–150

Positioning. The flagship modern review of transaction cost theory (TCT). Unlike earlier stock-taking (David & Han 2004, SMJ; Geyskens, Steenkamp & Kumar 2006, AMJ; Zhao, Luo & Suh 2004, JIBS), which summarized the broad empirical state of the field or single phenomena, this essay reviews the distinctive features of TCT research across conceptual areas and charts a deliberately pluralistic path forward, drawing mainly on strategy and international business (IB). It was written as Oliver Williamson passed away, and serves partly as a tribute to his program.

Core thesis. Restates Williamson's discriminating alignment hypothesis: transactions that differ in their attributes are assigned to governance structures with differing adaptive capacities and costs, in a discriminating (transaction-cost-economizing) way. Key transaction attributes: asset specificity, uncertainty, frequency; governance mechanisms: markets, hierarchies, hybrids; behavioral assumptions: bounded rationality and opportunism.

  • Behavioral assumptions revisited. Clarifies that opportunism means "some actors behave opportunistically some of the time" — rebutting the Ghoshal–Moran (1996) and Perrow (1986) critiques that TCT presumes universal guile. Introduces bounded reliability (Verbeke & Greidanus 2009) as a broader envelope concept (strategic opportunism plus non-strategic preference reversal). Argues for importing behavioral economics and psychology (prospect theory, endowment effects, self-serving fairness biases, e.g., Loewenstein et al. 1993) to explain perceived opportunism without assuming guile, and to explain deviations from TCT-prescribed governance.
  • Constructs revisited. Asset specificity: reviews the four canonical Williamson types plus temporal (Masten, Meehan & Snyder 1991) and procedural (Zaheer & Venkatraman 1994) specificity. Uncertainty: traces Williamson's oscillation between environmental and behavioral uncertainty and the primary/secondary distinction (Koopmans 1957); highlights interpretive uncertainty (Weber & Mayer 2014).
  • Measurement critique. Documents three recurring empirical weaknesses: (1) R&D/marketing-intensity proxies for asset specificity, often at industry level; (2) survey measures of investment amount rather than of specificity (residual value / quasi-rents); (3) one-sided measurement that ignores the exchange partner's specific investments — an omitted-variable problem. Calls for transaction-level, direct measurement.
  • Governance and performance. Discusses hybrids: as a distinct governance mode (Williamson 1991) versus institutions combining price and hierarchy mechanisms (Hennart 1993, 2013). Reviews evidence that firms "get governance right" on average, and highlights the open puzzle of systematic deviations (Sampson 2004; Nickerson & Silverman 2003).

Research agenda. Three conceptual directions: (1) deeper integration across strategy, IB, and institutional economics; (2) engagement with sociology — trust as substitute/complement for governance and the formal–informal organization literature; (3) psychology and behavioral economics to unpack how decision makers perceive behavioral uncertainty. Three empirical frontiers: platform governance and two-sided markets (market tipping can create small-numbers situations without asset specificity), artificial intelligence and machine learning in governance decisions, and nonpecuniary objectives (nationalism, CSR, grand challenges).

Takeaway for strategy. The authoritative current roadmap of TCT: what is settled, what is contested, and where the theory can plausibly go in the digital economy.

3.2 Sørensen, Hoetker, Leiblein & Mellewigt (2023) — Journal of Management, 49(8), 2831–2862

Positioning. A review-and-agenda article in Journal of Management on plural governance: organizing an economic activity through multiple governance modes at the same time. It synthesizes three research streams that descend from Bradach & Eccles (1989) but developed in isolation: franchising, dual distribution, and concurrent sourcing.

Transaction cost economics connection. Plural governance is the empirical frontier of TCE. The Williamsonian ideal type is a discriminating choice of one mode per transaction (make, buy, or ally); plural forms deliberately combine modes — taper integration, dual distribution, simultaneous make-and-buy — and the TCE logic is central to explaining when such combinations are efficient. The paper builds on TCE, agency theory, and capability/learning explanations (e.g., safeguarding, benchmarking, symbiosis, information advantages).

Core argument. Each plural-governance stream has its own characteristics, conceptual foundations, and dominant methodologies, with little cross-stream exchange. The authors take stock of all three, then show that four governance challenges are common to all streams — monitoring, cooperation, learning, and competition — and use these challenges to isolate theoretical and methodological blind spots in each stream.

  • Monitoring: how a focal firm observes performance and behavior across simultaneous modes.
  • Cooperation: how simultaneous modes shape (and are shaped by) relational and contractual cooperation.
  • Learning: how operating multiple modes generates knowledge that feeds governance adaptation.
  • Competition: how modes compete for resources, and how mixing modes affects competitive positioning.

Research agenda. Proposes importing theoretical predictions, causal mechanisms, and methods across streams in theoretically appropriate ways, and outlines specific theory-development avenues within each stream while connecting them to the intellectual core of plural governance.

Takeaway for strategy. The natural JoM companion to Cuypers et al. (2021): where Cuypers et al. review the single-mode discriminating-alignment logic, Sørensen et al. review what happens when firms run several governance modes simultaneously — the most common empirical setting in franchise and distribution systems.

3.3 Schepker, Oh, Martynov & Poppo (2014) — Journal of Management, 40(1), 193–225 · January 2014 — just outside a strict 2015–2025 window

Positioning. A review of the interfirm-contracting literature that organizes the field around the functions contracts serve. It is anchored in transaction cost economics (TCE), which is the most prominent perspective on the "optimal governance" and "safeguarding" function of contracts.

Core argument. Safeguarding alone cannot explain how contracts are structured. The review integrates four additional perspectives: relational capabilities (building cooperation and trust), firm capabilities, relational contracts, and the real-option value of a contract. It documents that contract research has moved from a narrow focus on contract structure and its safeguarding function toward a broader view emphasizing adaptation and coordination — a shift it ties to the economics of incomplete contracts and relational exchange (Macneil 1974, 1978).

  • TCE's role: contracts as safeguards against ex post opportunism under asset specificity, bounded rationality, and uncertainty — the reference point against which other functions are measured.
  • Beyond TCE: coordination and adaptation functions of contracts (assigning roles, decision rights, and adjustment routines), their role as relational and capability-building devices, and their option value under uncertainty.

Research gaps identified. (1) Consequences of contracting, especially outcome assessment; (2) strategic options, decision rights, and the evolution of dynamic capabilities; (3) contextual constraints of relational capabilities; (4) contextual constraints of contracting capabilities; (5) complements, substitutes, and bundles of governance mechanisms; (6) contract structure and social process.

Takeaway for strategy. The cleanest JoM statement of how TCE-based contract research absorbed relational, capability, and options perspectives — useful as the conceptual bridge between Williamsonian safeguarding logic and modern contract-design research.
