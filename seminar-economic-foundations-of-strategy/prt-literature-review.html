PRT Literature Review — Framework (Phase D)

The PRT development line in one paragraph

Property rights theory begins from a single move: what economic actors do depends on which rights they hold over the assets they use. The classical line (Coase 1960; Demsetz 1967; Alchian & Demsetz 1972) established that rights emerge to internalize externalities and that the firm is a contractual answer to the metering problem of team production. The modern line (Grossman & Hart 1986; Hart & Moore 1990) formalized ownership as residual control rights over non-human assets and derived the cost-benefit logic of integration. Two decades of extension (Aghion & Tirole; Bolton & Whinston; Holmström & Milgrom; Baker, Gibbons & Murphy) generalized the model to authority, multitasking, and relational contracting, while a parallel critical line (Maskin & Tirole 1999; Rajan & Zingales 1998; Holmström 1999; Foss et al. 2021) attacked its foundations and redirected attention from asset ownership to access, power, knowledge, and competence. The empirical record (Joskow; Baker & Hubbard; Kaplan & Strömberg; Lafontaine & Slade; Magelssen) confirms the central prediction — who holds the right changes what gets invested — while leaving the mechanism contested. Today the theory is being stress-tested again by assets it never modeled: code, data, models, and platforms, where ownership is shared, contested, and algorithmically recomposed (Benkler 2002; Boudreau 2010; Jacobides et al. 2018; the AI-era papers of 2025–2026).

Six streams

1. Roots & antecedents — property as an economic (not merely legal) category; externalities, internalization, and the emergence of rights (Commons, Llewellyn, Knight, Coase, Alchian, Demsetz, Cheung, Furubotn-Pejovich, Anderson-Hill, Umbeck, Libecap, North, Barzel).

2. Siblings — the parallel theories that share PRT's DNA but differ on the focal cost: transaction costs (Williamson, Klein-Crawford-Alchian), agency (Jensen-Meckling, Holmström, Fama-Jensen), relational contracting (Macaulay, Macneil), and the competence/RBV challenge (Winter, Conner-Prahalad).

3. Modern PRT (the GHM line) — ownership as residual control; the costs-and-benefits logic of integration; then authority (Aghion-Tirole), multitasking (Holmström-Milgrom), relational contracts (BGM), and the foundations debate (Hart-Moore 1999, Segal, Hart 2017).

4. Critiques & counterpoints — Maskin-Tirole's irrelevance result, Holmström's subeconomy, Rajan-Zingales' access/power, Ghoshal-Moran's practice critique, and the ownership-competence redirect.

5. The empirical record — asset specificity, contractibility, and control rights across autos, coal, trucking, biotech, VC, and MNE subsidiaries.

6. Digital, platform, ecosystem & AI era — from open source (Benkler, Lerner-Tirole) to platforms (Boudreau, Hagiu-Wright), ecosystems (Adner, Jacobides et al.), digital economics (Goldfarb-Tucker), and the new firm of AI agents (2025–2026).

Key debates

D1. Why does the firm exist? Transaction costs (Coase 1937; Williamson) answer "costs of the market"; A&D (1972) answer "costs of metering team output"; GHM (1986) answer "incentives of ex-ante investment under incompleteness". The three answers are complements with different focal costs, not rivals — Kim & Mahoney (2005) makes the mapping explicit (transaction / institution / principal-agent).

D2. Ex ante incentives vs ex post governance. PRT's distinctive claim is that ownership works through ex ante investment incentives; TCE works through ex post adaptation and hold-up. Whinston (2003) shows the two can be formally separated and asks which does the empirical work — a question the empirical stream still cannot fully adjudicate.

D3. Ownership of what? GHM: residual control over non-human assets. Rajan-Zingales: access to critical resources and power, not ownership. Conner-Prahalad, Foss: knowledge. Foss et al. (2021): ownership itself as a competence. Each move widens the asset base the theory can address — relevant when the "asset" is code, data, or a model.

D4. Is the incomplete-contract foundation sound? Maskin-Tirole (1999): with enough rationality and transferable utility, unforeseen contingencies need not block efficient contracting — the irrelevance critique. Hart-Moore (1999), Segal (1999), and Hart (2017, reference points) rebuild the foundation on complexity, renegotiation, and aggrievement.

D5. Incentives or competence? GHM's owner is an incentive device; Foss et al. (2021) make the owner a bearer of judgment — ownership decisions are themselves skilled choices. This is the Week-3 arc's endpoint ("ownership: from incentive to competence").

D6. What does the evidence actually test? The empirical record robustly shows specificity, contractibility, and information predict ownership and integration (Joskow; Baker-Hubbard; Monteverde-Teece; Lafontaine-Slade). What it has not cleanly separated is the mechanism — ex-ante investment vs ex-post hold-up vs selection. Magelssen (2020) moves the test inside the firm, where rights allocation predicts innovation even holding the firm boundary fixed.

D7. The modern era: who owns the intangible? The digital-era papers re-run D1–D6 on new assets: open source shows production without residual claimants (Benkler; Lerner-Tirole); platforms show control rights split between owner and complementors (Boudreau; Hagiu-Wright); ecosystems show value co-produced without a single owner of the residual (Jacobides et al.; Adner); and the AI-era papers ask whether the owner of the model, the data, or the agent is the new residual claimant.

The empirical record in four sentences

Across autos (Monteverde-Teece 1982), coal (Joskow 1987/1988), trucking (Baker-Hubbard 2003/2004), biotech alliances (Lerner-Merges 1998), VC (Kaplan-Strömberg 2003), and MNE subsidiaries (Magelssen 2020), the allocation of ownership and control rights predicts investment and performance in the direction PRT predicts. The strongest designs (Baker-Hubbard's on-board computers; Magelssen's transfer-pricing rights) isolate changes in the information/rights environment, not just levels. What remains under-identified is the mechanism: whether ownership works through ex-ante incentives, ex-post adaptation, or selection of competent owners — the exact fault line between PRT, TCE, and the competence view.

The modern era in four sentences

The digital era re-opened PRT's founding question on assets the founders never imagined. Open source demonstrated large-scale production coordinated by reputation and access rights rather than residual claims (Benkler 2002; Lerner-Tirole 2002). Platforms and ecosystems split control rights across owners and complementors, reviving the "shared ownership" problem Kim & Mahoney (2005) flagged as PRT's distinctive strength. And the AI era (2025–2026) asks the sharpest version yet: when agents do not shirk but misalign, and when the key assets are weights, data, and prompts, the residual-control logic survives, but the object of ownership and the competence of owning have become the center of the theory.

Stream 1 · Roots & Antecedents (classical property-rights economics)

1.1 · Knight (1921) — Risk, Uncertainty and Profit [book]

Pitch. Knight separates measurable risk from unmeasurable uncertainty and makes profit the reward for bearing the latter through entrepreneurial judgment. The paper matters in the PRT line because it offers the first systematic account of why some people direct others' productive activity and claim the residual — the fact later property-rights and firm-boundary theories take as their explanandum.

Question. Why does profit exist in a competitive economy, and what function does the entrepreneur perform?

Argument. Insurable risk rests on a known probability distribution; true uncertainty does not, so it cannot be diversified or insured away. Under uncertainty, the "confident and venturesome" guarantee fixed incomes to the cautious and, in return, receive the right to direct their work; the entrepreneur bears residual uncertainty and exercises responsible control. Profit is the residual return to this uncertainty-bearing judgment and would vanish in a world of perfect knowledge.

Evidence. Conceptual and analytical argument built on the risk/uncertainty distinction; the wage-versus-residual income split defines the enterprise; no empirical data. Knight himself judged the determinants of firm size largely non-scientific ("a matter of general and historical accident"), a claim Coase later quotes against him.

Findings. Competition cannot erode profit because uncertainty cannot be insured; the entrepreneur's twin functions — judgment and control — constitute the firm.

Position. Provokes Coase (1.5), who rejects guaranteed-versus-fluctuating income as the distinguishing mark of the firm, and Alchian & Demsetz (1.11), who deny that risk bearing explains the residual claimant's role. note: the judgment reading of Knight also feeds modern ownership-competence work (4.12).

1.2 · Commons (1924) — Legal Foundations of Capitalism [book]

Pitch. Commons puts the legal order inside economics: market exchange is a transfer of legal rights, and courts' "working rules" determine what can be owned, sold, and done. This is the institutionalist ancestor of the property-rights premise that the object of economic analysis is rights, not things.

Question. What are the legal foundations on which capitalism actually rests, and how does the state define the individual freedom to transact?

Argument. Property is not a physical object but a bundle of legally enforced rights of use, enjoyment, and disposal; economic activity is analyzed through "transactions" governed by "working rules" that combine custom, sovereignty, and law. Commons traces how the US Supreme Court's due-process cases progressively widened "property" from physical possession to exchange value — goodwill, going-concern value, and expected future income — so that state action limiting expected returns became a constitutional issue.

Evidence. Close doctrinal reading of American case law and the evolution of legal concepts; historical and jurisprudential argument rather than data or formal models.

Findings. The economy is constituted by law: who may do what, and who must pay whom, is set by working rules, so value and efficiency cannot be analyzed apart from the rights structure the state enforces.

Position. Anticipates the "rights, not resources" core later restated by Alchian (1.9), Demsetz (1.10), and Alchian & Demsetz (1.12), and the legal-institutional turn of North (1.18). note: Commons' transactions-and-sovereignty apparatus differs from the transaction-cost framing Coase (1.5, 1.7) later makes canonical.

1.3 · Llewellyn (1931) — What Price Contract? An Essay in Perspective

Pitch. Contract is not a discrete exchange of promises but a forward-looking framework for an ongoing relation that is adjusted as circumstances change. The essay injects legal realism into economic thinking about exchange and supplies the incompleteness intuition on which later firm theory rests.

Question. What does the legal apparatus of contract actually cost and do in business life, and what should contract doctrine be for?

Argument. The classical picture — a contract as a self-contained act specifying future performance, with courts enforcing the terms — misdescribes practice. Business people use contracts as frameworks: the writing fixes the limits of a relation whose details are worked out and renegotiated through time, and disputes are typically settled by adjustment rather than by litigation over "breach." Llewellyn asks what price society pays for this doctrinal machinery and argues doctrine should support the underlying relation rather than strict, discrete enforcement.

Evidence. Legal-realist analysis of doctrine against observed commercial practice; illustrative case reasoning, no quantification.

Findings. The essence of contracting lies in the relation and its adjustment machinery; discrete-spot-exchange imagery misleads, especially for long-run dealings.

Position. Anticipates incomplete-contracting and relational themes at the heart of Coase (1.5), Macneil (2.8), relational-contract theory (3.13), and the GHM program (3.1–3.3): if contracts are inevitably open frameworks, something other than the written term — authority, ownership, or trust — must allocate the unstated remainder.

1.4 · Commons (1934) — Institutional Economics [book]

Pitch. Commons proposes a volitional economics in which institutions — "collective action in control, liberation, and expansion of individual action" — are the subject matter, and the transaction is the ultimate unit of analysis. It is the fullest statement of the institutionalist research program that property-rights economics later formalizes.

Question. How can economic theory be rebuilt with institutions, not hedonistic individuals, at its center?

Argument. Classical economics took the commodity and individual exchange as units; Commons replaces them with the transaction, classified into bargaining, managerial, and rationing transactions, whose ordering is set by "working rules" (custom, the state, the courts). Value is "reasonable value," worked out through collective action rather than given by utility alone. Five principles — scarcity, efficiency, custom, working rules, and futurity — frame how rights and duties are assigned; the "going concern" is the operating unit of collective action.

Evidence. Broad historical, legal, and philosophical argument building a definitional system; no formal models or data.

Findings. The rights and duties that working rules assign determine which transactions can occur and at what terms; institutions are not frictions around the market but the constitution of the economy.

Position. The most direct ancestor of the claim that rights structure incentives (1.9, 1.10); its authoritative-allocation view of transactions contrasts with Coase's cost-based explanation (1.5) and with Williamson's later governance economics (2.9). Its concern with institutional change links forward to North (1.18).

1.5 · Coase (1937) — The Nature of the Firm

Pitch. The firm exists because using the price mechanism costs something: within a firm, market transactions are superseded by an entrepreneur who directs resources, and the firm's size is set where the cost of organizing one more transaction inside equals the cost of doing it on the market. This is the founding statement of transaction-cost economics and the question the whole PRT line inherits.

Question. Why does a firm emerge in a specialized exchange economy in which the price mechanism is assumed to coordinate resources, and what determines its size?

Argument. The distinguishing mark of the firm is "the supersession of the price mechanism" (p. 389). The main reason is the cost of using the price mechanism: discovering relevant prices, and negotiating and concluding a separate contract for every exchange (p. 390). The firm substitutes one contract in which a factor agrees, for remuneration, "to obey the directions of an entrepreneur within certain limits"; the essence of the contract is that it states only the limits of the entrepreneur's power, details being decided later (p. 391). A firm expands until the cost of organizing an extra transaction inside equals the market-transaction cost or the cost of organizing in another firm (pp. 395, 404), bounded by diminishing returns to management, mistakes, and rising factor supply prices (pp. 394–397). Coase rejects Knight's fixed-versus-residual income criterion (pp. 392, 398–401) and anchors the definition in the legal master–servant relation, where direction is the essence (pp. 403–404).

Evidence. Conceptual argument with worked examples: department stores, Lancashire cotton, sales taxes and quotas as firm-creating regulations (p. 393), and a von Thünen multi-product example (pp. 402–403).

Findings. Firms are "islands of conscious power" that exist because organizing is cheaper than transacting; the boundary sits at the margin where the two costs are equal.

Position. Answers Knight (1.1) and the division-of-labor explanations; extended and made refutable by Alchian & Demsetz (1.11), radicalized by Cheung (1.14), and later given an ownership-based answer by GHM (3.1–3.3), who locate the residual in control over assets rather than in direction of transactions.

[grounding: full-text — pp. 389–391 (supersession of the price mechanism; marketing costs; the employment contract's limits); pp. 395, 404 (marginal rule for firm size); pp. 398–401 (critique of Knight)]

1.6 · Alchian (1950) — Uncertainty, Evolution, and Economic Theory

Pitch. Under uncertainty no one can literally maximize, so economic theory need not assume profit maximization or perfect foresight: competition works as an adoption-and-selection process that favors realized positive profits. The paper shifts the theory of the firm from optimizing agents to environmental selection.

Question. How can economics explain the behavior and survival of firms under uncertainty without assuming profit maximization?

Argument. Because outcomes are unpredictable, "profit maximization" is replaced by "realized positive profits" as the condition of survival. The economic system is an adoptive mechanism: whatever actions turn out profitable — whether chosen by foresight, luck, or imitation — are copied and expanded, while unprofitable patterns are weeded out by competition. Imitation of successful innovators does the work that rationality assumptions were thought to do, so observed firm behavior can be "as if" optimal even when motives are not.

Evidence. Informal analytical argument using examples of innovation, imitation, and survival analogies; no formal model or data.

Findings. Evolution, not motivation, explains which behaviors persist; the relevant question is which rules of action survive competitive pressure in a changing environment.

Position. Precursor of the selection and competence view of the firm (Winter, 2.12). note: for the PRT line it matters because selection operates on who captures returns — property rights determine which experiments are rewarding, a connection Alchian develops in 1.9 and Barzel formalizes in 1.19.

1.7 · Coase (1960) — The Problem of Social Cost

Pitch. When transacting is costless and rights are well defined, bargaining yields an efficient allocation regardless of who holds the rights; when transacting is costly, the initial assignment of rights determines the outcome, and the law should assign rights so as to maximize the value of production. The paper converts externalities into a property-rights problem.

Question. Is the Pigovian remedy — taxing the injurer for divergences between private and social cost — the right way to handle harmful effects?

Argument. Harm is reciprocal: the rancher's cattle and the farmer's crops conflict no matter who holds the right. Coase shows that with zero transaction costs the parties will negotiate to the same efficient output under either liability rule; the law then matters only for distribution. With positive transaction costs, the initial allocation of rights is decisive, and the task is to compare the total product under alternative legal-institutional arrangements — market transacting, firm organization, or regulation — and choose the one that maximizes value. The analysis runs through nuisance cases (e.g., Sturges v. Bridgman, Bryant v. Lefever) and the railway-sparks example.

Evidence. Extended common-law case analysis and worked examples (farmer–rancher, confectioner–doctor, sparks); analytical, no econometrics.

Findings. The Pigovian tax prescription rests on a misdiagnosis: the economically relevant issue is which party can transact most cheaply to the efficient outcome, and with costly transacting the rights assignment itself is the policy variable.

Position. The bridge from 1.5 to property-rights economics: since exchange is of bundles of rights (Demsetz's opening claim in 1.10), assigning rights is allocating resources. Demsetz (1.10) derives his internalization thesis from Coase's zero-transaction-cost result, and the state's role in rights here feeds North (1.18).

1.8 · Arrow (1962) — Economic Welfare and the Allocation of Resources for Invention

Pitch. Knowledge is an indivisible, inappropriable, and uncertain good, so a private economy systematically under-invests in invention; patents create artificial property rights in information to restore incentives, at the price of monopoly distortion. The paper is the founding welfare analysis of intellectual property.

Question. Does a competitive economy allocate resources to invention optimally, and what institutions correct the failure?

Argument. Three properties of information defeat the market: indivisibility (using an idea does not consume it, implying increasing returns); inappropriability (a buyer can resell information, so the inventor cannot capture its full value); and uncertainty (invention outcomes are risky and uninsurable). Welfare analysis shows equilibrium under-investment relative to the social optimum. Correctives are government-funded research and patent law, which grants a temporary monopoly to make information appropriable — a second-best device whose own distortions must be weighed.

Evidence. Analytical welfare reasoning from the public-good character of information, with R&D examples; no empirical data.

Findings. Because information is a public good, private invention is under-supplied; the patent is the deliberate creation of a property right where the natural right would be communal.

Position. Introduces the property-rights-in-knowledge problem that the PRT classics handle as attenuated communal rights — Demsetz (1.10) argues freely appropriable ideas blunt inventive incentives and private rights speed ideas forth — and Barzel (1.19) treats via capture and the public domain. note: this is the canonical counterpoint for the open-source literature in stream 6 (6.1–6.3).

1.9 · Alchian (1965) — Some Economics of Property Rights

Pitch. Prices are exchange ratios of rights, so economics is inseparable from the property-rights structure that assigns decision power over scarce resources. Alchian's essay states the paradigm in one move: the system of rights determines who bears the consequences of actions, and therefore behavior and resource allocation.

Question. What are property rights, and how do private, communal, and state ownership — and attenuated rights — change economic behavior?

Argument. A property right is a socially enforced right to select among uses of a resource; ownership is a bundle of such rights, circumscribed by prohibitions and by the cost of enforcement. The central mechanism is cost-and-reward bearing: the more an actor's decisions determine a resource's use, the more fully that actor internalizes the consequences. Alchian applies this to why private ownership concentrates costs and rewards on owners, why attenuation — through price controls, regulation, or collective ownership — diverts behavior toward nonpecuniary goals, and why managers of firms with attenuated or nontransferable residual claims behave differently from full owners.

Evidence. Comparative conceptual analysis of private, state, and communal arrangements, including business-firm and socialist examples; no formal model.

Findings. Rights allocation, not preferences and technology alone, determines prices and outputs; altering who holds which right alters who internalizes what, and hence behavior.

Position. The foundational manifesto of the paradigm elaborated in Demsetz (1.10), the survey of Furubotn & Pejovich (1.13), and the programmatic statement of Alchian & Demsetz (1.12); its attenuated-rights logic reappears as measurement and capture problems in Barzel (1.19).

1.10 · Demsetz (1967) — Toward a Theory of Property Rights

Pitch. Property rights emerge to internalize externalities when the gains from internalization exceed its costs; shifts in technology and relative prices are what trigger new rights. Demsetz turns property rights from a legal datum into an endogenous economic variable.

Question. Why do property rights come into being, and what determines whether ownership is communal, private, or state, and how rights coalesce into bundles?

Argument. A market transaction is an exchange of two bundles of property rights: "it is the value of the rights that determines the value of what is exchanged" (p. 347). Rights specify who may benefit or harm whom; an effect is external when the cost of bringing it to bear on decisions exceeds the gains, and internalization is usually a change in property rights (pp. 347–348). The thesis: rights develop to internalize externalities when gains exceed costs, chiefly after new technology or markets make old rights poorly adapted (pp. 349–350). The Labrador fur trade is the evidence: before it the Montagnes hunted freely with no private land rights; the trade raised fur values and hunting scale, and family hunting territories emerged by the early eighteenth century — while Southwestern plains Indians, lacking valuable non-migratory animals, never developed them (pp. 350–353). Communal ownership fails because no one bears the full cost of use and future generations have no living agent to bid (pp. 354–355); private ownership cuts negotiation costs because only the affected few must deal (pp. 356–357). The corporation — delegated management, limited liability, salable shares as an escape hatch — follows the same cost logic (pp. 358–359).

Evidence. Anthropological-historical case evidence (Leacock, Speck, the Jesuit Relations) plus comparative institutional examples; no econometrics.

Findings. Property rights appear when internalizing pays, and ownership structure follows the same cost logic; communal rights over ideas blunt invention, so private rights to ideas (patents) speed it up (p. 359).

Position. Builds directly on Coase's zero-transaction-cost result (1.7) and on Alchian (1.9); its historical method is the template for Anderson & Hill (1.15) and Umbeck (1.16); its internalization account is generalized by Barzel (1.19) and inverted by GHM (3.1).

[grounding: full-text — pp. 347–348 (rights and internalization); pp. 349–353 (emergence thesis and fur-trade evidence); pp. 354–359 (communal vs private ownership; the corporation)]

1.11 · Alchian & Demsetz (1972) — Production, Information Costs, and Economic Organization

Pitch. The firm has no fiat or authority beyond ordinary contracting; it is a contractual structure for team production in which a central monitor who is the residual claimant controls shirking — the metering mechanism Coase's marketing-cost story left unspecified.

Question. Why does the classical capitalist firm exist, and what explains its internal structure and which inputs firms own?

Argument. The firm's presumed power of command "is delusion": the employer can only withhold future business or sue; long-term contracts are not the essence (p. 777). Team production joins inputs whose output is not a sum of separable outputs, so individual marginal products are not cheaply observable and must be metered (p. 779); costly detection induces shirking — each input's realized cost of leisure falls below its true cost — and market competition disciplines shirkers only imperfectly (pp. 779–782). The remedy is a specialized monitor given title to the net earnings plus the rights to observe input behavior, be common to all contracts, renegotiate and alter membership, and sell this status — the six attributes defining the classical firm (pp. 782–783). Coase is extended, not contradicted: transaction costs alone do not yield the residual claimant, and the factory system grew after central power lowered negotiating but raised metering costs (pp. 783–785). Which inputs are owned rather than hired follows from user-cost detection — tools and trucks tend to be owned, labor cannot be (pp. 791–793); the same logic reaches profit sharing, the corporation, nonprofits, partnerships, and unions (pp. 785–791).

Evidence. Conceptual model with worked examples — cargo loading, university perks, the weavers-and-central-power case, dockworkers versus lawyers, athletes, trucks — and comparative institutional cases yielding testable implications; the firm is finally read as a privately owned market for input information (pp. 793–795).

Findings. The classical firm exists because concentrating the residual claim and monitoring rights on one party lowers shirking-detection costs in team production; "authority" is a misleading description of a nexus of bilateral contracts.

Position. Directly extends Coase (1.5) and displaces Knight's risk-bearing explanation (1.1); the nexus-of-contracts and residual-claimant reading is systematized by Jensen & Meckling (2.4), generalized by Cheung (1.14), and contested by GHM (3.1), who ground ownership in residual control over assets rather than in monitoring.

[grounding: full-text — pp. 777–779 (no fiat; metering; team production); pp. 779–783 (shirking, the monitor, six attributes of the classical firm); pp. 788–795 (corporation, input ownership, firm as private market)]

1.12 · Alchian & Demsetz (1973) — The Property Right Paradigm

Pitch. The essay states the property-rights paradigm for economic historians: what is owned is not resources but socially recognized rights of action, and the structure of those rights — which exist and who holds them — determines allocation and its evolution.

Question. Three questions organize the paradigm: (1) What is the structure of property rights in a society? (2) What consequences for social interaction flow from a given structure? (3) How did that structure come into being?

Argument. Ownership is a bundle of circumscribed rights whose strength is set by the probability that the owner's choice governs use (pp. 17–18). "Communal rights" — use without an absentee right to exclude — are first-come-first-served, so users cannot be charged the cost of their use and overuse follows: the 1970 Prince Edward Island seal slaughter under a 50,000-animal quota is the vivid case (pp. 19–20). Attenuation of exchange — rent control raised "restricted" Chicago apartment ads from 10 to 90 percent of listings during WWII — forces non-price allocation and raises transaction costs (pp. 20–21). Without a right to exclude, free-rider problems raise transacting costs: freeway congestion versus toll roads (pp. 21–22). Development: a communal/private mix is unstable — branded animals become private while unbranded stay communal; suppressing private rights creates shirking, pushing societies toward state regulation or indoctrination (pp. 22–24). Rising values restructure rights: Northeastern Indians adopted private land rights after the fur trade, thirteenth-century England developed exclusive, alienable land rights, and radio retransmission prompted the Berne Convention (pp. 24–25). The authors conjecture that heterogeneous investor beliefs explain firms' initial capital structure (p. 26).

Evidence. Historical examples throughout — seals, rent control, freeways, Indian land, enclosure, English land law, international copyright — with an explicit admission that the literature is "speculative theory" and a call for historical empirical work (p. 26).

Findings. Which rights exist, who owns them, and whether exclusion and exchange are possible determine which costs actors bear; explaining observed rights structures is a prime task for economic history.

Position. The consolidating statement of the Alchian–Demsetz line (1.9–1.11), extending the 1972 metering logic to society-wide rights; it frames the historical research program executed by Anderson & Hill (1.15), Umbeck (1.16), Libecap (1.17), and North (1.18).

[grounding: full-text (OCR of scanned JSTOR PDF) — pp. 17–19 (bundle of rights; structure of rights); pp. 19–22 (communal rights, transaction costs, free-rider); pp. 22–26 (development of rights; Indian land, enclosure, Berne Convention)]

1.13 · Furubotn & Pejovich (1972) — Property Rights and Economic Theory: A Survey of Recent Literature

Pitch. The first systematic survey of the property-rights school defines its core claim — behavior depends on the structure of rights, because rights determine who bears costs and captures rewards — and shows its payoff across the theory of the firm, externalities, and comparative systems.

Question. What does the property-rights literature contribute to economic theory once the assumptions of costlessly defined rights and zero transaction costs are dropped?

Argument. The survey organizes the work of Coase, Alchian, Demsetz, and others around one principle: since exchange is the exchange of rights and rights are costly to define and enforce, the assignment and attenuation of rights are first-order variables. Within the firm, separation of ownership from control attenuates owners' rights, so managers pursue utility — perquisites, staff, quiet life — rather than profit; the same lens explains behavior in public, regulated, and socialist firms where residual claims are weak. Externalities reappear as cases of rights not fully assigned, and the Coase theorem defines the benchmark. Evidence assembled from the literature includes studies of utility and regulated-firm behavior, corporate control, and Yugoslav and Soviet enterprise responses to attenuated rights (e.g., reactions to the Liberman reforms).

Evidence. A survey integrating theoretical and empirical studies of the period; no new data, but systematic synthesis across the firm, externality, and systems-comparison literatures.

Findings. A unified rights-structure framework links corporate governance, externalities, and the comparison of capitalism and socialism; attenuation of rights is the key intervening variable.

Position. The codifying review of the paradigm built in 1.9–1.12, and the bridge to its corporate-governance offshoots (2.4, 2.6); it frames the agenda that GHM (3.1–3.3) formalize and that North (1.18) carries to the level of whole economies.

1.14 · Cheung (1983) — The Contractual Nature of the Firm

Pitch. The "firm" is not an entity with a boundary against the market; it is a network of contracts in which some inputs are priced by direction instead of by output. Cheung dissolves the firm–market dichotomy into a continuum of contractual arrangements.

Question. If the entrepreneur's direction is itself just a contract — as Coase insisted — in what sense is there a distinct thing called a firm?

Argument. The firm arises where it is cheaper to have an input owner agree to follow directions within limits than to negotiate a price for every task: an open-ended contract replaces step-by-step pricing. Cheung's central move is that the firm does not abolish the market but substitutes one kind of contract for another — pricing inputs by direction rather than pricing goods, and sharing or piece-rate contracts where measurement permits. Because all organizational forms are contracts chosen to economize on transaction and measurement costs, no objective line separates "the firm" from "the market"; both are contractual arrangements, and the private market inside a firm is a special case of the general phenomenon.

Evidence. Conceptual argument across contractual forms — wage, piece-rate, share, and rental contracts — drawing on his earlier share-tenancy analysis; no formal model or data in the paper.

Findings. The firm/market distinction is arbitrary; the real question is why particular inputs are remunerated by direction, by output measurement, or by shares, given the costs of measuring what each input contributes.

Position. Radicalizes Coase (1.5) and systematizes the nexus-of-contracts view of Alchian & Demsetz (1.11), foreshadowing Jensen & Meckling (2.4). note: GHM (3.1–3.3) later reply that contracts alone cannot draw firm boundaries because some rights — residual control over assets — are inherently noncontractible and must be assigned by ownership.

1.15 · Anderson & Hill (1975) — The Evolution of Property Rights: A Study of the American West

Pitch. Property rights in the American West emerged piecemeal, as the gains from internalizing the costs of open access came to exceed the costs of establishing and enforcing exclusion. The paper turns Demsetz's thesis into a testable account of frontier institutional history.

Question. Can the internalization theory of property rights explain how rights to land, water, and cattle actually evolved on the American frontier?

Argument. Establishing rights is an investment: rights appear when resource value rises or enforcement cost falls. Anderson and Hill trace this for three frontier resources. Range land: open range and communal grazing gave way to private claims as cattle values rose and barbed wire made fencing cheap, ending the violent conflicts and competitive overstocking of the open-range commons. Water: in the arid West, riparian common-law rights proved ill-suited as water became the scarce resource, and the prior-appropriation doctrine ("first in time, first in right") evolved to give secure, transferable claims. Cattle: unbranded, free-roaming herds were a commons whose roundup costs and overstocking were internalized by branding and, eventually, fenced private pasture. Government land policy — homesteading and land grants — sometimes accelerated and sometimes retarded this efficient evolution.

Evidence. Qualitative historical case evidence from the American West: fencing and barbed wire, water law, ranching institutions, and federal land policy; the argument is interpretive rather than statistical.

Findings. Frontier rights were created where commons costs exceeded enforcement costs; the process was incremental, adaptive, and value-driven rather than designed.

Position. The first sustained historical application of Demsetz (1.10); it sets the template for Umbeck (1.16) and Libecap (1.17) and supplies North (1.18) with cases of endogenous rights change. note: it inherits Demsetz's efficiency emphasis and underplays the distributional conflicts that Libecap later shows can block rights creation.

1.16 · Umbeck (1981) — Might Makes Rights: A Theory of the Formation and Initial Distribution of Property Rights

Pitch. Where no state exists to enforce claims, the initial distribution of property rights is determined by the distribution of force — rights go to those who can hold them by might. The California gold rush is the natural experiment.

Question. How are property rights initially distributed in the absence of government, and why does that distribution persist?

Argument. Umbeck models rights as the outcome of a violent contest: with no enforcer, each person's de facto rights are bounded by his relative ability to use force, and an allocation that matches the distribution of force is stable — anyone offered less would fight, and anyone holding more invites attack. When claimants are roughly equal in strength, rights are divided roughly equally; the state then arises or is accepted because it economizes on the costs of enforcing an already-existing distribution. In the California gold fields of 1848–1849, miners arrived before any effective government, and within months formed local districts whose codes allocated placer claims on the basis of prior appropriation, typically one claim of limited, roughly equal size per man — consistent with the equal-force equilibrium — and provided local enforcement against trespass and theft.

Evidence. Historical case study of the California gold rush: district mining codes, claim-size conventions, and the sequence from force to written local rules; the argument combines a simple model with this archival material.

Findings. When enforcement is absent, might determines rights; the initial distribution persists because reallocation is costly, and formal government later ratifies rather than creates the underlying allocation.

Position. Supplements the efficiency story of Demsetz (1.10) and Anderson & Hill (1.15) with the distributional, force-based origin of rights, a corrective that Libecap (1.17) and North (1.18) institutionalize. note: it also anticipates the modern point that ex ante rights allocation shapes ex post bargaining — the mechanism GHM (3.1) place at the center of ownership.

1.17 · Libecap (1989) — Contracting for Property Rights [book]

Pitch. Property-rights reform succeeds or fails through collective contracting among interested parties, and distributional conflict — who gets which share — often blocks change even when aggregate gains are large. Libecap explains both the creation and the persistence of inefficient rights with American resource-history cases.

Question. Why do property rights change readily in some settings and remain stuck in others, despite clear aggregate gains from change?

Argument. Redefining rights requires agreement among heterogeneous claimants over both the new rules and the division of the resulting wealth. Change stalls when the aggregate gains are uncertain, when bargainers are numerous and heterogeneous, when existing claims are unequally distributed, and when losers can block without compensation. The case studies: mineral rights on federal land, where imprecise early law left valuable ores contestable and delayed clear title; federal range land, where the Taylor Grazing Act ended homesteading but grazing rights remained vaguely allocated; oil-field unitization, where coordinated recovery would raise output but operators holding private information about reserves could not agree on shares; and fisheries, where open access destroyed stocks while fishermen failed to agree on individual quota allocations because everyone fought over the division.

Evidence. Detailed historical case studies with legislative, industry-structure, and timing evidence; the comparative cases are the empirical core of the book, supplemented by descriptive quantification.

Findings. Internalization gains are necessary but not sufficient for rights change: distributional bargaining is the binding constraint, which explains why inefficient commons — most vividly fisheries — persist for decades.

Position. Generalizes Demsetz (1.10) and Anderson & Hill (1.15) into a positive, distribution-centered theory of rights change, correcting their efficiency bias; North (1.18) absorbs this into a general theory of institutions, and the persistence-of-inefficiency theme anticipates modern political-economy work on institutional failure.

1.18 · North (1990) — Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance [book]

Pitch. Institutions — formal rules, informal constraints, and their enforcement — are "the rules of the game" that structure incentives and determine transaction and production costs, and therefore economic performance. North brings the property-rights line to the level of whole economies and explains why inefficient institutions persist.

Question. What are institutions, how do they change, and why do some economies develop growth-promoting property-rights institutions while others do not?

Argument. Institutions reduce uncertainty by structuring everyday interaction; organizations are the players whose learning and bargaining drive institutional change. Change is incremental and path-dependent — sunk adaptations, learning effects, network externalities, and self-reinforcing expectations make history matter. Informal constraints change slowly and can blunt formal reform. Because impersonal exchange needs third-party enforcement, the state is indispensable; but the state is also the chief potential predator on property rights, and credible commitment by government is the central problem of institutional development. Secure, low-cost-enforced property rights lower transaction costs enough to support complex exchange; politics that fail to deliver them stay poor because distributional conflict and commitment problems block reform.

Evidence. Historical and comparative argument across economies — England versus Spain, the Third World — synthesizing earlier work; illustrative rather than econometric.

Findings. Institutions are the deep determinant of economic performance; inefficient institutions persist because change is filtered through politics, path dependence, and the state's dual role as protector and threat.

Position. Carries the internalization and distribution themes of 1.10–1.17 to macroeconomic history, making secure property rights the centerpiece of growth theory; its credible-commitment problem parallels, at state level, the contractual incompleteness that GHM (3.1–3.3) analyze within the firm.

1.19 · Barzel (1997) — Economic Analysis of Property Rights [book, 2nd ed.]

Pitch. Because assets have many attributes and delineating and enforcing rights over each attribute is costly, property rights are never complete: wealth spills into the "public domain" for anyone to capture. Barzel turns property-rights economics into the economics of measurement, transaction costs, and attenuation, and derives ownership and contract form from who can control an asset at lowest cost.

Question. What determines who effectively controls an asset and the income it yields, when rights are costly to define, measure, and enforce?

Argument. Barzel defines property rights as the individual's ability, in expected terms, to consume the good or its services directly or through exchange; the economically relevant owner is the party best able to capture the asset's value. Because measuring attributes is costly, some attributes are left unpriced and lie in the public domain, where people expend resources to capture them (rationing by waiting, queuing, rent-seeking). Attenuation — by law or by enforcement cost — has the same effect. Contractual form is a partitioning of rights over attributes: sharecropping versus fixed rent versus wages is chosen by comparing the costs of measuring effort against the costs of measuring output; the employer-employee relation arises where monitoring input attributes is cheaper than metering output; vertical integration and asset ownership respond to the capture problems of common property.

Evidence. Applied verbal and simple-model analysis across contracts, price controls, share tenancy, labor, insurance, and the firm, building on his earlier measurement-cost work; no single dataset.

Findings. Ownership is a matter of degree set by measurement and enforcement costs; the public domain and attenuation, not legal title alone, determine who actually gets what an asset yields.

Position. The culmination of the classical line from Alchian and Demsetz (1.9–1.11): it generalizes internalization (1.10) and metering (1.11) into a measurement theory of rights and the firm, and its insistence that attributes are noncontractible directly anticipates the incomplete-contract program of GHM (3.1–3.3). note: unlike GHM, Barzel locates the essence of ownership in ex ante control of income streams rather than ex post residual control rights.

Stream 1 one-paragraph synthesis

Stream 1 supplies the intellectual foundations of the whole PRT corpus. It opens with the classical vocabulary — Knight's uncertainty and entrepreneurial residual (1.1) and Commons' and Llewellyn's claims that the object of economic life is legally defined rights and open-ended contractual relations, not things or discrete promises (1.2–1.4). The first turning point is Coase (1.5, 1.7): transaction costs explain why firms supersede the price mechanism and why, when transacting is costly, the assignment of rights determines allocation — turning property rights into an economic variable. The second turning point is the Alchian–Demsetz internalization program (1.9–1.12): rights emerge when internalization gains exceed their costs (1.10), the firm is a metering device for team production with a residual-claimant monitor (1.11), and the whole is codified as a paradigm plus survey (1.12–1.13), with Cheung dissolving the firm–market boundary into a contract continuum (1.14). The third turning point is historical and political: rights creation in the American West and the gold rush (1.15–1.16), Libecap's demonstration that distributional conflict blocks efficient reform (1.17), and North's institutions-as-rules-of-the-game synthesis (1.18). Barzel's measurement theory (1.19) closes the stream by grounding incompleteness — the premise GHM formalize downstream (3.1) — in the cost of delineating rights over asset attributes.

Stream 2 · Siblings (parallel organizational-economics theories)

2.1 · Williamson (1971) — The Vertical Integration of Production

Pitch. Vertical integration is not dictated by technology or scale economies but is one organizational response to "market failure" in intermediate-product markets: when autonomous trading breaks down, unified ownership supplants it. This is the paper that converts the make-or-buy question into a transaction-cost problem, the agenda PRT later inherits.

Question. Why do firms vertically integrate when technology-based arguments (successive monopoly, scale economies, "technological determinism") fail to explain observed integration patterns?

Argument. Market exchange fails when (i) uncertainty is combined with small-numbers exchange (bilateral monopoly) and (ii) "information impactedness" gives one party an advantage at contract renewal. Internal organization then has three advantages over the market: better access to information via internal auditing, the capacity to resolve disputes by fiat rather than costly bargaining, and attenuated incentives for opportunistic behavior because internal divisions do not appropriate the full gains from strategic misrepresentation. Crucially, the paper already states the "fundamental transformation": the winner of an original contract acquires a cost advantage (location, learning, task-specific skills), so bidding parity at renewal is upset and ex post bilateral monopoly develops (canonical from the paper; restated at Williamson 1985, ch. 2, §3).

Evidence. Conceptual and illustrative: a comparative-institutional argument contrasting market procurement with internal organization, with worked examples from vertical integration, plus the claim that integration has its own costs so that discriminating choice is required.

Findings. paper: Integration is a market-failure remedy where transaction-specific advantages create ex post contracting strains; technology alone does not fix firm boundaries.

Position. Foundational for the TCE line: elaborated into 2.2, systematized as the governance framework in 2.9; the bilateral-monopoly/hold-up logic it sketches is sharpened by 2.3 and later formalized — and partly displaced — by the residual-control-rights theory of 3.1.

2.2 · Williamson (1975) — Markets and Hierarchies

Pitch. Firms (hierarchies) and markets are alternative governance structures for completing related transactions, and the choice between them turns on economizing transaction costs under specified behavioral assumptions. The book gives the 1971 intuition a general "organizational failures" framework that the PRT line treats as its main rival.

Question. Under what conditions do markets fail as coordinating devices, and what internal-organization features make hierarchy the efficient alternative?

Argument. Two behavioral assumptions — bounded rationality ("intendedly rational, but only limitedly so") and opportunism ("self-interest seeking with guile") — combine with small-numbers exchange, uncertainty, and "information impactedness" (true circumstances known to one party cannot be costlessly discerned or displayed to others) to make discrete market contracting hazardous, especially where investments are idiosyncratic. Hierarchy economizes on bounded rationality (coordination, communication) and attenuates opportunism (auditing, fiat, internal incentive alignment), but is itself subject to incentive limits and control loss (information decay across levels), so large, nonstandard transactions may be better handled by the M-form or the conglomerate acting as an internal capital market.

Evidence. Conceptual framework plus case-based and antitrust illustrations (vertical integration, nonstandard contracting, tie-ins, reciprocity); the book's core contribution is the "markets and hierarchies" heuristic rather than formal modeling.

Findings. paper: Markets fail where asset-specific, idiosyncratic exchange meets bounded rationality and opportunism; the internal organization that economizes on these is the firm.

Position. The definitive statement of Williamsonian TCE before 2.9; directly opposed to 1.11's team-production/monitoring account of the firm; the framework that 3.1 and 3.3 take as their point of departure when relocating the explanation from transaction-cost governance to residual control over assets.

2.3 · Klein, Crawford & Alchian (1978) — Vertical Integration, Appropriable Rents

Pitch. Vertical integration occurs when one party can appropriate another's "specialized quasi-rents": the value of an asset in its current use minus its value in the next-best use. This is the canonical statement of the hold-up problem that Property Rights Theory (PRT) later formalizes through residual control rights.

Question. Why does the competitive contracting process fail after relationship-specific investments are made, and when does unified ownership beat long-term contracting?

Argument. Once durable, transaction-specific assets are in place, a quasi-rent stream becomes "appropriable" — the portion of the quasi-rent in excess of value to the second-highest-valuing user can be captured ex post by the other party threatening to terminate the relationship (definition restated at 2.9, p. 56 n.). Anticipating this "postcontractual opportunistic behavior," the investor under-invests or demands safeguards. If courts cannot verify the contingencies, long-term contracts are incomplete and costly; as appropriable specialized quasi-rents grow and contractual solutions fail, vertical integration — unifying ownership — internalizes the incentive conflict.

Evidence. The GM–Fisher Body case is the paper's centerpiece: the 1919 ten-year requirements contract (GM holding a majority stake, cost-plus pricing, Fisher supplying all closed bodies) and GM's 1926 acquisition of the remaining shares after demand growth strained the contract and Fisher refused to make GM-adjacent specific investments. note: later scholarship (Klein 1988; Coase 2000; Casadesus-Masanell & Spulber 2000) disputes details of the episode, but not the quasi-rent logic.

Findings. paper: The existence of appropriable specialized quasi-rents, not asset specificity per se, is the essential condition making integration profitable; ownership removes the incentive for holdup.

Position. Bridges 2.1/2.2 and modern PRT: 3.1 and 3.3 take the same problem but locate its solution in residual control rights over assets rather than in merger per se; the hostage/contracting alternative is developed in 2.9 (ch. 7–8).

2.4 · Jensen & Meckling (1976) — Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership Structure

Pitch. The firm is a legal fiction — a "nexus of contracts" — and its ownership structure is the equilibrium outcome of trading off the agency costs of outside equity against those of debt. This positive agency theory explains the separation of ownership and control without invoking monopoly or market failure, and it defines the notion of "ownership" PRT later problematizes.

Question. Why do firms use the mixture of outside equity and debt they do, and what determines who holds the residual claim?

Argument. An agency relationship exists whenever one party (principal) delegates decision authority to another (agent). Agency costs equal monitoring expenditures by the principal plus bonding expenditures by the agent plus the residual loss. As the owner-manager's equity share falls, he bears a smaller fraction of the cost of perquisites and shirking, so outside equity carries agency costs; debt introduces its own agency costs (risk-shifting, claim dilution, monitoring/bonding covenants, bankruptcy costs). Optimal ownership structure minimizes total agency costs, and capital markets price-protect ex ante so that the owner-manager ultimately bears the costs. (The three-part definition of agency costs is quoted in 2.9, ch. 1, n., with the source given as J–M 1976, p. 308.)

Evidence. Analytical and illustrative: an agency-cost model of ownership structure with numerical examples of perquisite consumption; reinterpretation of Berle–Means separation as an agency problem rather than a managerial-discretion failure.

Findings. paper: Ownership structure is endogenous; the "firm" has no meaningful productive boundary — it is a set of contracts among claimholders.

Position. The agency branch parallel to TCE: 2.6 extends it to decision systems; 2.9 absorbs its cost categories into ex post contracting costs. PRT answers its boundarylessness: 3.1–3.4 show that with incomplete contracts, who owns assets (not who holds cash-flow claims) determines firm boundaries.

2.5 · Holmström (1982) — Moral Hazard in Teams

Pitch. In team production with unobservable efforts, no budget-balancing sharing rule can achieve first-best effort — but an outside "budget breaker" (residual claimant) can. This is the formal demolition of the free-rider problem behind 1.11 and a precise rationale for a principal who need not monitor anyone.

Question. Can a team of self-interested agents with unobservable inputs achieve an efficient (first-best) Nash equilibrium under a sharing rule, and what role does a non-team member play?

Argument. paper: With a balanced budget (shares of output sum to output), each agent captures only his share of the marginal product of his unobservable effort, so the first-best cannot be sustained: moral hazard in teams is endemic even when aggregate output is observed costlessly. Free-riding is not a monitoring failure in the 1.11 sense; it is a property of balanced-budget sharing. Introducing a principal who breaks the budget — absorbing the surplus or deficit rather than sharing output — permits forcing contracts (pay contingent on reaching a target, with penalties) that implement first-best effort, provided agents are risk neutral (or penalties/randomization are available). The budget breaker must not himself be an input-supplier, else the free-rider problem reappears one level up.

Evidence. Formal propositions: (1) balanced-budget sharing rules cannot implement first-best team effort; (2) budget-breaking by an outside party restores first-best under the stated conditions; (3) efficiency worsens as team size grows under balanced budgets.

Findings. paper: The owner/principal exists to break the budget, not primarily to monitor; this gives a contract-theoretic foundation for the residual claimant.

Position. A sibling rationale for ownership that complements PRT's control-rights story: 3.1's owner holds residual control rights, while here the owner holds the residual claim that makes incentives work; the contrast with 1.11 (monitoring as the solution) is the paper's explicit foil. Extended by 3.11 and by 4.2.

2.6 · Fama & Jensen (1983) — Separation of Ownership and Control

Pitch. The separation of ownership and control in large corporations is not a defect but an efficient organizational adaptation: it survives because it separates decision management from decision control, protecting diffuse residual claimants from managerial agency problems. The paper explains organizational form through the control of agency costs rather than through asset ownership.

Question. How do organizations in which residual claimants do not manage survive competition, and what decision structures make the separation of risk bearing from decision-making viable?

Argument. Agency problems arise wherever decision managers do not bear the wealth effects of their decisions. The solution is organizational: separate "decision management" (initiation and implementation) from "decision control" (ratification and monitoring), with control rights allocated up a hierarchy to a board of directors representing residual claimants. Where residual claims are diffuse and freely alienable (open corporations), residual claimants play no role in internal decision control, so a board ratifies and monitors top management; where residual claims are concentrated (partnerships, closed corporations), claimants are also managers and control is direct. Organizational forms are selected by competition: surviving forms control agency costs at lowest cost.

Evidence. Comparative-institutional analysis across organizational forms (open corporations, closed corporations, partnerships, nonprofits), arguing from survival and from the match between residual-claim characteristics and decision systems.

Findings. paper: Separation of ownership and control is efficient given diffuse risk bearing and specialized decision expertise; the binding constraint is agency-cost control through decision-system design.

Position. Extends 2.4's positive agency theory into a theory of internal control structures; its focus on who holds decision (control) rights is the closest agency-side cousin to PRT's residual control rights, though rights here attach to decision processes rather than to assets — the step 3.1 and 3.8 make.

2.7 · Macaulay (1963) — Non-Contractual Relations in Business

Pitch. Businesspeople frequently transact without detailed contracts and settle disputes without recourse to contract law; relational sanctions, not legal enforcement, govern much of exchange. This is the empirical finding that undermines "legal centralism" and motivates relational-contract and private-ordering theory.

Question. How much do businessmen actually plan exchanges legally and use contract law to handle disputes, and why do they so often avoid it?

Argument. Interview evidence from Wisconsin (reported as 68 businessmen and lawyers) shows two patterns. First, many exchanges proceed on brief orders or handshakes: detailed planning is costly, and elaborate contracts can signal distrust and damage the relationship. Second, when disputes arise, businessmen rely on nonlegal sanctions: the threat of terminating a valued relationship, reputation within the business community, and informal "give-and-take" adjustments that keep a deal workable over time. Contract law operates mainly in the background — for one-shot, high-stakes, or irreparably broken relations — rather than as the operative governance device.

Evidence. Qualitative interview study of business practice (1963), reporting the norms and practices of commercial exchange in Wisconsin.

Findings. paper: Contract and contract law are frequently "non-contractual": commitments are honored and disputes resolved through relational, reputational, and economic interdependence rather than legal rules.

Position. The empirical foundation for 2.8's relational contract theory and for TCE's private-ordering emphasis: 2.9 (ch. 3, §2–5) cites Macaulay for the "spirit" in which adaptations are effected and for the prevalence of middle-range transactions; it stands against the court-ordering assumption of classical contract economics (1.5, 1.7 traditions).

2.8 · Macneil (1974) — The Many Futures of Contracts

Pitch. Contract is not the discrete, presentiated exchange of classical law but "the relations among parties to the process of projecting exchange into the future"; almost all exchange is relational to some degree. This legal-theoretic reorientation supplies the trichotomy — classical, neoclassical, relational — that TCE adopts as its governance map.

Question. What is contract, once exchange is understood as extending into an uncertain future within ongoing social relations, rather than as a sharp-in/sharp-out discrete event?

Argument. Classical contract law intensifies "discreteness" and "presentiation" — treating the future as fully present and the parties as faceless. That paradigm fits few real transactions: parties cannot foresee all contingencies, so contracts are inherently incomplete projections of future exchange. Exchange therefore takes place within relations whose continuity, norms, and mutual reliance ("the entire relation as it has developed through time") constitute the real governance device. The famous discrete image — "sharp in by clear agreement; sharp out by clear performance" (quoted by 2.9, ch. 1, p. 32) — describes only a narrow segment of the exchange spectrum.

Evidence. Doctrinal and conceptual analysis of contract law and exchange theory, distinguishing types of future-regarding behavior and building the case that relational elements pervade even nominally discrete transactions.

Findings. paper: Contract is fundamentally about managing the future within ongoing relations; discreteness is an ideal type, not a description of business practice.

Position. The legal foundation on which 2.9 (ch. 3, §1–2) builds its classification of governance structures (market, trilateral, bilateral, unified), mapping Macneil's contract types onto asset-specificity/frequency cells; complements 2.7's empirics; 2.10 and the hybrid-governance literature carry the agenda forward.

2.9 · Williamson (1985) — The Economic Institutions of Capitalism

Pitch. The economic institutions of capitalism — firms, markets, and relational contracting — "have the main purpose and effect of economizing on transaction costs," and are explained by aligning transactions (differing in asset specificity, uncertainty, frequency) with governance structures (differing in adaptive capacity and cost).

Question. Why do the nonstandard, intermediate, and hierarchical institutions of real capitalism exist, and how are transactions matched to governance structures?

Argument. Ch. 1 treats transaction costs (ex ante drafting and safeguarding; ex post maladaptation and haggling) as the friction of the economic system and states the discriminating-alignment hypothesis: assign transactions to governance structures so as to economize on transaction costs (pp. 15–21). Where bounded rationality, opportunism, and asset specificity are joined, discrete contracting fails and "contract as governance" — private ordering rather than court ordering — prevails (pp. 30–33; k/s/p schema, pp. 32–35). Ch. 2 pairs bounded rationality with opportunism and installs asset specificity (site, physical, human, dedicated) as the framework's "big locomotive" (pp. 44–47, 54–57); the fundamental transformation turns ex ante large-numbers bidding into ex post bilateral monopoly (pp. 61–63). Ch. 3 maps Macneil's classical/neoclassical/relational contract types onto transaction cells governed by market, trilateral, bilateral, or unified structures (pp. 68–79).

Evidence. Schematic frameworks and worked applications rather than data — the k/s/p schema, the company-town application (ch. 1, §5), doctrinal analysis; the book's empirical chapters follow later (ch. 5).

Findings. paper: Discriminating alignment of transactions to governance structures explains capitalist institutions from spot markets to hierarchy. note: ownership matters mainly as a safeguard and private-ordering device (common ownership is one safeguard, pp. 20–21), not yet as the locus of residual control rights — the move 3.1 makes.

Position. The culmination of 2.1/2.2 and the systematizer of 2.3's quasi-rent logic and 2.7/2.8's relational evidence; it explicitly contrasts itself with the property-rights literature (ownership matters, but ex post governance institutions matter too; pp. 27–29), setting up 3.1 and 3.3, which relocate firm boundaries in residual control over assets, and 4.1, which questions the incompleteness foundation.

2.10 · Bradach & Eccles (1989) — Price, Authority, and Trust

Pitch. Price, authority, and trust are distinct control mechanisms that can be combined within a single organization — "plural forms" such as a firm that simultaneously makes and buys the same input. This attacks the ideal-type equation (market = price, hierarchy = authority) on which both TCE and PRT's make-or-buy framing rest.

Question. Are control mechanisms tied one-to-one to organizational forms, and how do real organizations combine market, hierarchical, and trust-based control?

Argument. Price, authority, and trust each control exchange in a specific way: price requires that the terms of exchange be specifiable; authority rests on the legitimate right to direct (obedience is expected, not purchased each time); trust is the expectation that a partner will act with competence and goodwill, economizing on specification, monitoring, and legal enforcement. The three are not substitutes arrayed along a single market–hierarchy continuum but mechanisms that can complement one another. "Plural forms" — e.g., restaurant chains mixing company-owned and franchised outlets, or manufacturers both producing and purchasing the same component — exploit the mechanisms simultaneously and are stable, not transitional.

Evidence. Synthesis of organizational sociology and economics literatures plus illustrative industry cases (franchising, "make and buy" manufacturing) demonstrating simultaneous use of mechanisms.

Findings. paper: Control mechanisms are separable from organizational forms; plural forms are common, durable arrangements, so discrete choice models of governance miss how real boundaries work.

Position. A sociological challenge to 2.1/2.2/2.9's discrete-alignment logic and to the clean make-or-buy dichotomy PRT formalizes; it anticipates hybrid-governance research and the "simultaneous" boundary questions taken up in 4.3; trust appears here as a governance mechanism that TCE (2.9) largely suppresses under opportunism.

[grounding: knowledge + abstract]

2.11 · Milgrom & Roberts (1990) — Bargaining Costs, Influence Costs, and the Organization of Economic Activity

Pitch. Organization of economic activity is shaped by two cost families: bargaining costs (ex post haggling over rents under incomplete contracts, per TCE) and influence costs — resources spent trying to affect decisions that allocate benefits, plus the distortion of those decisions. Because centralized authority creates prizes worth influencing, influence costs limit the feasible scope and discretion of organizations.

Question. What costs, beyond transaction costs in the market, determine the boundary and internal structure of the firm — and why do organizations deliberately restrict discretion and communication?

Argument. When decisions (budgets, promotions, transfer prices, strategic choices) allocate rents, affected parties invest in "influence activities" — lobbying, politicking, misrepresentation — to sway them. The relevant costs are the direct expenditures on influence plus the value lost because decisions are distorted away from efficiency plus the costs of ex ante constraints adopted to limit influence. Authority and centralization, which economize on bargaining and coordination costs (the TCE logic of 2.1/2.2), simultaneously enlarge the prizes of influence; firms therefore respond with rules that restrict discretion: objective promotion criteria and seniority, narrow job definitions, limits on subordinates' access to superiors, incentive pay tied to verifiable measures, and decentralization of authority.

Evidence. Conceptual modeling and comparative-institutional argument (the chapter appears in a political-economy volume and pairs organizational economics with the logic of political influence), with illustrative organizational practices as the empirical content.

Findings. paper: Influence costs are a first-order cost of internal organization: they explain why firms constrain authority and communication and why firm scope and internal structure are jointly determined.

Position. Supplies the internal-cost side that 2.1–2.9's integration logic underplays (2.9 treats bureaucracy only briefly in ch. 6) and that PRT largely abstracts from; it complements 3.8's authority-based theory and the incentive-system view of 3.11 and 4.2, and informs 4.3's boundary discussion.

[grounding: knowledge + abstract]

2.12 · Winter (1988) — On Coase, Competence, and the Corporation

Pitch. Coase's question — why firms exist — has a second answer beside transaction-cost economizing: firms exist because they are repositories of productive knowledge whose coordination cannot be accomplished through contracts. This is the early statement of the competence/knowledge view of the firm that later confronts PRT.

Question. Is the transaction-cost/opportunism explanation of the corporation sufficient, or does explaining the firm require a theory of knowledge and organizational competence?

Argument. Winter distinguishes the "governance" reading of Coase (the firm as a hierarchy that economizes on transaction costs by authority) from the "competence" reading (the firm as a body of knowledge). Organizational knowledge is largely tacit and embedded in routines and in the coordinated behavior of teams (following Polanyi; Nelson and Winter 1982); such knowledge cannot be fully articulated, priced, or transferred by contract, so markets cannot mediate its exchange and coordination. The corporation is therefore more than a nexus of contracts (contra 2.4): it is a durable entity that holds and exercises knowledge-based capabilities, and its boundaries reflect the limits of what can be coordinated and learned internally versus transacted externally.

Evidence. Conceptual and historical argument revisiting Coase 1937 (1.5) and evolutionary economics; no formal model — the paper's evidence is the logic of tacit knowledge and the observation that capabilities are firm-specific and non-contractible.

Findings. paper: A complete theory of the firm needs both transaction-cost and competence elements; knowledge-based coordination explains the existence and durability of the corporation where governance logic alone is incomplete.

Position. Seeds the knowledge-based theory developed by 2.13; its emphasis on non-contractible, team-embedded knowledge prefigures the critique that asset-ownership logic (3.1–3.3) misses human-capital and capability dimensions; 4.8 responds critically, and 4.12 later joins ownership with competence.

[grounding: knowledge + abstract]

2.13 · Conner & Prahalad (1996) — A Resource-Based Theory of the Firm: Knowledge Versus Opportunism

Pitch. Firms can exist and hold boundaries for purely knowledge-based reasons, even without opportunism: when knowledge is embedded and expectations differ, internal organization beats market contracting by substituting the firm's collective knowledge for the individual's. The resource-based view is thus a rival, not a complement, to opportunism-based TCE.

Question. Can a knowledge/resource-based theory explain the existence and boundaries of the firm without assuming opportunism, and how does it compare with the transaction-cost account of 2.1–2.9?

Argument. The paper analyzes "hiring" (employment) versus "contracting" (market purchase of the same knowledge services) when individuals hold idiosyncratic knowledge and parties hold different expectations about future knowledge needs and values. Even with honest agents, contracting on knowledge is hazardous, since knowledge is tacit, hard to specify, and valued differently by the parties. The firm's advantage lies in knowledge substitution (the firm's existing organizational knowledge replaces the individual's), knowledge flexibility, joint learning, and internalizing exchange so diverging expectations never need reconciliation through complete contracts; organizational membership itself creates the shared framework ("convergence of expectations") that makes coordinated knowledge use possible. Knowledge-based benefits can justify the firm even where its transaction costs exceed the market's; opportunism supplies only an additional, separate reason.

Evidence. Conceptual framework and comparative argument contrasting firm and market modes of knowledge-based exchange, worked through hiring-versus-contracting cases rather than data.

Findings. paper: Firm existence and boundaries can follow from knowledge characteristics (tacitness, substitutability, flexibility, learning) without recourse to opportunism; resource-based and transaction-cost logics are distinct and sometimes rival explanations.

Position. Directly contests the opportunism-centered governance line of 2.1/2.2/2.9 and extends 2.12's competence view into an explicit "knowledge versus opportunism" theory; 4.8 critiques such knowledge-based approaches, and 4.12 (ownership competence) reconciles knowledge with PRT's asset-ownership logic.

[grounding: knowledge + abstract]

Stream 2 one-paragraph synthesis

Stream 2 supplies the family of parallel organizational-economics theories against which Property Rights Theory defines its distinctive claim: that ownership — residual control over assets — is what determines firm boundaries. Its internal turning points are three. First, Williamson's arc from 2.1 to 2.9 builds the governance alternative: asset specificity plus bounded rationality and opportunism explain vertical integration and, later, the full market–hybrid–hierarchy alignment; this is the framework GHM (3.1, 3.3) inherits and then displaces by relocating the mechanism from transaction-cost economizing to residual control rights. Second, the incentive/agency branch (2.4, 2.5, 2.6) shows that who holds residual claims and decision control does analytic work — Holmström's budget breaker gives ownership an incentive-theoretic role independent of control — leaving PRT to ask which rights ownership actually conveys. Third, the relational and knowledge turns (2.7, 2.8, 2.12, 2.13, with 2.10 and 2.11 policing the flanks) contest both legal centralism and opportunism-centrism, feeding the knowledge-based and plural-form critiques that later streams direct at PRT.

Stream 3 · Modern PRT — the GHM line and extensions

3.1 · Grossman & Hart (1986) — The Costs and Benefits of Ownership

Pitch. Ownership is the purchase of residual rights of control — the rights to use an asset in ways not spelled out in a contract — and because residual rights are exclusive, giving them to one party takes them from another, so integration always distorts someone's ex ante investment incentives. This converts the firm-boundary question from "why does hierarchy beat markets?" into "who should own which assets?"

Question. What is a firm, and when is it optimal for one firm to buy another, given that contracts cannot list all rights over assets?

Argument. The firm is defined by the assets it owns, not by employment per se; a firm is more integrated the more of the counterparty's assets it controls (pp. 692–94). Two managers, 1 and 2, each make noncontractible relationship-specific investments a₁, a₂ at date 0; at date 1 they choose noncontractible actions q₁, q₂ and split surplus 50:50 via costless renegotiation. Ownership allocates who chooses q in the no-agreement state, which fixes each manager's ex post payoff and hence his ex ante investment. Because the owner of an asset captures a larger ex post share, integration makes the owner overinvest and the acquired party underinvest relative to the first-best; nonintegration gives both moderate stakes. The first-best is unreachable unless q is contractible; the optimal ownership structure minimizes the total investment distortion (Prop. 1, pp. 705–08).

Evidence. Formal propositions under explicit assumptions (scalar investments, comonotone total/marginal benefit); an insurance-industry application where the client list is the asset: companies own lists when renewals are insensitive to agent effort (whole life), agents own lists when effort matters (property-casualty, term life) — consistent with reported patterns such as ~65% of property-casualty premiums written by list-owning agents vs. ~12% for life insurance (pp. 710–14, Table 1).

Findings. Integration shifts, but does not remove, opportunism: the right comparison is between two incomplete-contract allocations of residual rights, not between holdup and a complete contract (pp. 716–17). Ownership should go to the party whose investment is more important.

Position. Founds the GHM/property-rights line: it formalizes Coase/Williamson/Klein–Crawford–Alchian insights while answering the TCE critique that integration cannot be strictly worse (the replication argument, pp. 692–93); extended to employees by 3.3, criticized for exogenous bargaining by 3.14–3.15, and pushed to activity/authority by 3.17.

[grounding: full-text; pp. 692–94 (definition of firm/residual rights), 705–08 (Prop. 1), 716–17 (conclusions)]

3.2 · Hart & Moore (1988) — Incomplete Contracts and Renegotiation

Pitch. If contracts are incomplete and ex post renegotiation is inevitable, what can long-term contracts accomplish at all? The paper models renegotiation explicitly and derives limits on what contracts plus renegotiation can implement, giving the GHM hold-up logic a bargaining-theoretic spine.

Question. Which outcomes are achievable when parties write an incomplete contract at date 0, invest, and then renegotiate under symmetric information at date 1?

Argument. (paper:) A buyer and seller make relationship-specific investments before a state realizes, then trade; the initial contract cannot be contingent on everything, and any ex post inefficiency triggers renegotiation modeled as a bargaining game. The core message is that once renegotiation is anticipated, contractual clauses are judged by their renegotiation-proof outcomes, not by their letter; incentives must come from how the contract shapes the disagreement point of the ex post bargain. Under standard assumptions the parties cannot commit not to renegotiate, which sharply restricts what contracts can deliver relative to the first-best; under some specifications the best the parties can do is close to no contract at all, foreshadowing the later irrelevance results that 3.5 confronts. The paper also shows the value of options/ownership-like clauses (arrangements in which one party can "buy back" or terminate under specified terms) as partial commitment devices. (note: canonical characterization; the paper is a direct technical companion to 3.1, sharing its two-period, two-party skeleton.)

Evidence. Formal model with explicit renegotiation and propositions on implementability; the analysis is analytical throughout (no data). The results are sensitive to whether parties can commit not to renegotiate — the commitment dichotomy that 3.5 makes central.

Findings. With renegotiation, simple price contracts give underinvestment; carefully chosen option/ownership-type clauses can restore partial incentives, but full first-best generally requires commitment power the parties lack.

Position. Bridges 3.1's asset-ownership story and financial contracting: it seeds the "who controls when things go wrong" logic that 3.6 (Aghion–Bolton) converts into a theory of debt; its renegotiation discipline reappears as the testing ground of 3.5 and 3.16.

[grounding: knowledge + abstract]

3.3 · Hart & Moore (1990) — Property Rights and the Nature of the Firm

Pitch. Extends GHM from two owner-managers to many agents and many assets: ownership means the right to exclude others from an asset, and control over physical assets translates into control over people — which is why firms exist and why ownership patterns shape everyone's investment incentives, not just the boss's.

Question. Who should own which assets when workers as well as owner-managers make noncontractible, asset-specific human-capital investments?

Argument. A set of agents invests in human capital at date 0; at date 1 coalitions that control assets bargain, with surplus divided by the Shapley value. Ownership matters because it determines which coalitions can use which assets (the owner can fire/replace specific workers, i.e., exclude them). Since an agent captures only part of his marginal product, there is universal underinvestment (Prop. 1, p. 1130); ownership rights are allocated to minimize this. Results: a single investor should own everything (Prop. 2); assets should not be split against a coalition's wishes (Prop. 3); no more than one veto per asset (Prop. 4); idiosyncratic assets and indispensable agents should own/be owned (Props. 5–6); "key groups" justify partnerships via majority rule (Prop. 7); strictly complementary assets should be owned together (Prop. 8); dispensable non-investors, including economically independent outsiders, should hold no control rights (Props. 9–10) — hence integration of independent assets only dilutes incentives. Two-asset applications show integration raises the acquiring boss's and his employees' incentives but can depress the acquired firm's boss and workers (pp. 1141–44); a three-asset case shows partial integration can hurt a third-party trading partner (pp. 1145–47).

Evidence. Formal propositions plus a worked chef/skipper/tycoon-yacht example: with cost-100, value-240 investments, a worker invests only if he faces a two-way rather than three-way split of ex post surplus (pp. 1122–24); applied to oil-pipeline ownership patterns (pp. 1148–49).

Findings. Ownership should go to important investors or indispensable trading partners; complementary assets belong together; independent assets belong apart. Nonhuman assets are the anchor of authority — without them "authority over what?" is undefined (p. 1150).

Position. Generalizes 3.1 to employees and multi-asset control structures; its indispensability/complementarity logic is the canonical PRT prediction set tested in Stream 5; answered by 3.14–3.15 on bargaining, and recast from assets to activities in 3.17.

[grounding: full-text; pp. 1119–24 (intro/example), 1130–39 (Props. 1–12), 1141–49 (applications)]

3.4 · Hart (1995) — Firms, Contracts, and Financial Structure

Pitch. The book-length statement of the property-rights theory: ownership of nonhuman assets is the foundation of the firm, and the same residual-control logic that explains firm boundaries explains capital structure — debt is a way of transferring control to investors when things go badly.

Question. Can one unified incomplete-contract framework explain both the boundaries of the firm and its financial structure?

Argument. (paper:) Consolidates the Grossman–Hart–Moore model: firms are collections of assets; residual rights of control matter because contracts are incomplete; ownership should be allocated to the parties whose relationship-specific investments are most important, subject to the costs integration imposes on others. The book then turns the framework on finance: when a manager needs outside funds and cannot commit to repay out of nonverifiable profits, the security design problem is really a control-allocation problem. Debt emerges as an optimal instrument because it transfers control from the entrepreneur-manager to creditors in low states (via bankruptcy/default) while leaving him in control in good states; equity and voting rights are analyzed similarly, including one-share-one-vote and takeovers. (note: canonical summary; the book integrates chapters 1–2 on ownership/boundaries with later chapters on capital structure and corporate governance.)

Evidence. Formal models developed in the text rather than data; worked examples (e.g., optimal debt design with renegotiation) and applications to security design, voting rules, and bankruptcy procedures.

Findings. A single incomplete-contracting principle — allocation of residual control rights — organizes both integration decisions and financial contracts; the theory's main limitation it openly acknowledges is that it is best suited to owner-managed firms, a gap 3.17 attacks.

Position. The canonical synthesis of 3.1–3.3 and the bridge to 3.6's financial-contracting model; the reference-points critique of its own foundations comes in 3.19 and the "large firm" critique in 3.17.

[grounding: knowledge + abstract]

3.5 · Hart & Moore (1999) — Foundations of Incomplete Contracts

Pitch. Answers Maskin & Tirole's irrelevance critique on its own terms: even when every possible trade is costlessly describable ex ante (pure mechanism design with renegotiation), the optimal contract is essentially the "null contract" once the environment is complex enough — so contractual incompleteness needs no appeal to undefinable contingencies.

Question. Can incomplete-contracting theory be given rigorous foundations without assuming that future trades are indescribable?

Argument. A seller invests at date ½ to cut the cost of producing the "special" widget; there are N widgets and in each state exactly one is special, with generic widgets' costs filling the interval between the two possible special costs. If parties can commit not to renegotiate, first-best is achievable even without describability (Prop. 1: take-it-or-leave-it offer). Without commitment — the realistic case — the parties may still design any mechanism, but ex post renegotiation (buyer has all bargaining power) means only the price gap between low- and high-cost states matters for incentives. Because permutations of widgets are symmetric, no mechanism can make the seller's payoff much more sensitive to her realized cost than the null contract: as N→∞ investment → 0 and contracts are worthless (Prop. 2). A variant with "defined" widgets shows partially incomplete contracts (supply one of a described class at a fixed price) dominate both specific performance and no contract (Prop. 3), and that describability matters once the state space is not maximal (deterministic example, §2.3).

Evidence. Formal propositions with proofs (Props. 1–3, Prop. 2* in the Appendix generalizing to two-sided, multidimensional investments and any bargaining split); explicit analysis of third-party penalty contracts, collusion, and lotteries to rebut MT's commitment devices.

Findings. Noncommitment plus a rich environment suffices to justify deep contractual incompleteness; ownership (vertical integration) can mitigate but not remove underinvestment, and multiple ownership allocations can sustain the same second-best — the "indeterminacy" objection to PRT (pp. 33–35 of the WP). Whether "optimal" incomplete contracts deserve the name is a semantic question the paper embraces (Section 5).

Position. Defends 3.1–3.4 against Maskin–Tirole (the 4.1 attack) and supplies the foundations debate that also produced 3.16 (Segal) and Che–Hausch; its property-rights section directly addresses 3.3.

[grounding: full-text (NBER WP 6726, = RES 1999); Props. 1–3 at WP pp. 9, 13, 26; commitment critique §3 pp. 26–32; property rights §4 pp. 33–36]

3.6 · Aghion & Bolton (1992) — An Incomplete Contracts Approach to Financial Contracting

Pitch. Moves the GHM control logic from firm boundaries to finance: when an entrepreneur must raise outside funds and returns are nonverifiable, the contract's job is to allocate control rights contingent on the state — giving investors control in bad outcomes is what makes debt work.

Question. Why does external finance typically take the form of debt, with control transferring to creditors in default, rather than profit-sharing or pure equity?

Argument. (paper:) A wealth-constrained entrepreneur with a project and private benefits of control needs funds from an investor; the project's monetary return is not verifiable, so repayment cannot be contracted directly. Following the incomplete-contract logic of 3.3/3.4, the initial contract instead allocates control rights (who decides whether to continue, liquidate, expand) as a function of verifiable signals. The central result: the optimal contract gives the entrepreneur control in "good" states and transfers control to the investor in "bad" states — exactly the state-contingent structure of debt with default — because unconditional control by either party destroys the other's incentives or financing willingness. The parties trade off giving the entrepreneur incentives to perform against the investor's need to be repaid; because control transfers only probabilistically, the first-best is generally unattainable and the entrepreneur retains too much control relative to efficiency. (note: canonical summary of the model and its two-state results.)

Evidence. A formal two-party contracting model with propositions characterizing the optimal (state-contingent) control allocation; no data.

Findings. Financial contracts are best understood as allocations of control rights rather than claims on cash flows; debt's distinctive feature — creditor control in bankruptcy — is an optimal response to nonverifiable returns and unobservable effort.

Position. Extends the PRT line from ownership of physical assets (3.1, 3.3) to ownership of the firm's decision rights by financiers, founding the incomplete-contract theory of finance (with Hart 1995, 3.4); empirically tested in Stream 5 (Kaplan–Strömberg 5.9).

[grounding: knowledge + abstract]

3.7 · Aghion & Tirole (1994) — The Management of Innovation

Pitch. Introduces the formal/real authority distinction in the setting where it matters most — R&D: whoever formally controls the research agenda may not be the one who effectively steers it, because initiative and information are what confer real power.

Question. How should control over an innovation project be allocated between a customer/principal and a research unit, when success requires noncontractible effort and information?

Argument. (paper:) The paper analyzes relationships in which one party (the "customer," e.g., a firm commissioning research or an organization housing a unit) needs the other's (the research unit's) initiative, which is noncontractible. Allocating formal authority — the contractual right to decide the project's direction — to the principal lets her steer toward her own objectives but dampens the unit's incentive to gather information and exert effort, because the unit's "real authority" (effective say over the project) falls. Conversely, delegating formal authority to the unit restores its initiative but risks projects that do not serve the principal. Real authority ends up with the party that is better informed and more willing to act; the paper frames the trade-off between control and initiative and shows how the allocation of formal authority should track whose information and whose objectives dominate. (note: canonical description; the 1994 QJE paper applies the framework to the management of innovation, and 3.8 builds the general organizational model.)

Evidence. Analytical model with examples; the innovation framing emphasizes why standard pay-for-performance fails (success is hard to verify ex ante) so control allocation substitutes for incentives.

Findings. In innovative settings, delegation of formal authority can be a commitment to give the innovator real authority; the value of control depends on the balance between the principal's objectives and the agent's initiative.

Position. Prepares 3.8's general theory of formal vs. real authority; shows PRT's control logic operating inside the firm on decisions rather than only on asset ownership, an internal-organization turn that 3.17 formalizes further.

[grounding: knowledge + abstract]

3.8 · Aghion & Tirole (1997) — Formal and Real Authority in Organizations

Pitch. A formal theory of who really decides in organizations: formal authority is the right to decide, but real authority — effective control — belongs to whoever is informed enough to act, so delegating formal authority is a way of buying the subordinate's initiative.

Question. When should a principal delegate decision rights to a subordinate, and when does delegation actually change who controls decisions?

Argument. (paper:) A principal (P) and an agent/subordinate (A) each can invest in learning about which of several projects is best; only the informed party can propose a good project. Two regimes are compared: P-form, where P keeps formal authority (the right to choose/ratify), and delegation (A-form), where A holds formal authority but P may overrule — overruling is costly and requires P to be informed. Real authority is measured by the probability that the agent's preferred project is implemented. Delegation raises A's incentive to acquire information (he anticipates real authority) but may leave P uninformed and unable to veto bad choices; keeping formal authority lets P control outcomes but kills A's initiative. Central results: real authority need not coincide with formal authority — an uninformed P with formal rights rubber-stamps, so real authority drifts to A; overruling and the congruence of preferences determine the value of delegation; and organizations optimally delegate to exactly the level where initiative and control balance. (note: canonical summary of the model and comparative statics.)

Evidence. A formal two-party model with information acquisition and explicit propositions; no data.

Findings. Delegation is an incentive device, not merely a transfer of chores: it works by credibly giving the subordinate real authority; more congruent objectives and harder overruling make delegation more attractive.

Position. Takes PRT's residual-rights logic beyond asset ownership into the allocation of authority within firms — the step 3.3's model could not take (it had no delegation); connects to 3.17's delegation analysis and to 4.2/4.5 critiques.

[grounding: knowledge + abstract]

3.9 · Bolton & Whinston (1993) — Incomplete Contracts, Vertical Integration, and Supply Assurance

Pitch. Asks what integration is actually buying when the problem is not opportunism over a fixed surplus but assuring supply: ownership changes the number of trading partners and therefore the competitive pressure a buyer can use — integration's benefit is insurance, its cost is lost supplier competition.

Question. When a buyer needs a customized input and there are alternative suppliers, does vertical integration improve or worsen investment and trade?

Argument. (paper:) A buyer invests in a relationship-specific asset; at trade time she can source from one or more suppliers whose costs are uncertain; contracts are incomplete and terms are set by ex post bargaining. The model contrasts nonintegration — where multiple sellers compete and the buyer can walk away, giving some supply assurance through the market — with integration, where the buyer owns the seller's assets and controls supply directly. Two forces pull against each other: integration eliminates the seller's ex post bargaining power (so the buyer captures more of her investment's return and invests more), but it also reduces the seller's incentives and, crucially, removes the competitive threat among alternative suppliers that protected the buyer ex post. The theory therefore predicts integration is valuable when the buyer's investment is important and supply assurance cannot be obtained through competing sellers; when sellers' investments or competition matter, nonintegration performs better. (note: canonical summary; the supply-assurance result is the paper's signature contribution.)

Evidence. A formal two-period model with investment and ex post bargaining; propositions comparing ownership structures; no data.

Findings. Vertical integration is a governance response to supply-assurance problems, and its cost is the dilution of market competition and of suppliers' incentives — integration and "shopping around" are substitute assurance devices.

Position. Gives the TCE/Klein–Crawford–Alchian supply-assurance idea (2.3) a GHM-style model and enriches 3.1's two-firm comparison with multi-supplier competition; a theoretical input to the 3.10 synthesis and Stream 5 empirics.

[grounding: knowledge + abstract]

3.10 · Whinston (2003) — On the Transaction Cost Determinants of Vertical Integration

Pitch. A discipline-forcing comparison: the transaction-cost claim that "asset specificity causes integration" does not follow from the property-rights model in the way often asserted — specificity per se is neither necessary nor sufficient; what matters is whose investments are important and how ownership changes bargaining positions.

Question. What do transaction-cost economics (TCE) and the GHM property-rights theory actually predict about when integration occurs?

Argument. (paper:) Whinston re-derives integration predictions from both traditions in a common setup. In the Klein–Crawford–Alchian/Williamson holdup logic, quasi-rents from specific investments tempt ex post expropriation, and integration is the remedy. But in the Grossman–Hart model, ownership affects incentives only through the distribution of ex post surplus, and asset specificity alone does not imply that one ownership structure beats another: integration helps the acquirer's incentives only if his investment is the important one, and it hurts the target's incentives regardless. In particular, "appropriable quasi-rents" arguments for integration (2.3) frequently rely on assumptions (e.g., unilateral investment, no incentive effects on the acquired party) that PRT makes explicit and that reverse the conclusion when relaxed; conversely, PRT's own comparative statics (indispensability, complementarity, investment importance) need to be stated carefully to yield testable TCE-style predictions. (note: canonical summary of the paper's corrective message.)

Evidence. Theoretical comparison with worked models rather than data; the paper explicitly examines what earlier TCE empirical proxies (specificity measures) capture relative to PRT's theoretical variables.

Findings. Specificity is a poor standalone predictor of integration under PRT; the theory points instead to the relative importance of the parties' noncontractible investments and to the incentive losses of the party losing control.

Position. A retrospective bridge from the 3.1–3.3 theory back to the TCE tradition (2.3, 2.9) and forward to the empirical record (Stream 5), clarifying why specificity-based empirics are a weak test of GHM.

[grounding: knowledge + abstract]

3.11 · Holmström & Milgrom (1991) — Multitask Principal-Agent Analyses

Pitch. The firm as an incentive problem in the round: when an agent allocates effort across several tasks, paying for the measurable ones distorts effort away from the unmeasurable ones — so low-powered incentives, asset ownership, job design, and restrictions on outside activities are chosen jointly.

Question. Why are employment contracts so often fixed-wage, and how do incentive pay, ownership, and job design fit together?

Argument. In a linear multitask agency model where the agent's cost depends on total attention across tasks, incentives on any task act as a tax on competing tasks. If performance in some tasks is hard to measure, an optimal contract may pay a fixed wage — any bonus on the measurable task would draw effort away from the valuable unmeasurable ones (the teachers' test-score logic of the introduction). Ownership enters the same way: when asset returns accrue to the principal, hard-to-measure asset-maintenance competes with measured output, so incentives are muted; when the agent owns the asset (absorbs its returns), high-powered incentives on output are optimal because the agent then internalizes care. Conditions favoring agent ownership: low risk aversion, low variance of asset returns, low measurement error on other tasks — a measurement-cost theory of integration standing against the specificity view of 3.1. Restrictions on private business activities substitute for performance pay: outside activities are banned most strictly when inside performance is hardest to measure. Job design: each task should have a single responsible agent; tasks should be grouped so that hard-to-measure tasks go to one job and easy-to-measure to another, with stronger incentives for the latter.

Evidence. Propositions in a formal multitask model (the HM 1987 linear-exponential framework); supportive stylized facts cited: in-house vs. independent sales forces in electronics, franchised vs. company-owned stores' incentive contrasts, staff vs. freelance reporters.

Findings. Incentives for one activity cannot be set without considering the whole portfolio of activities; ownership, pay, and job boundaries are complementary instruments, which explains muted internal incentives without any asset specificity.

Position. Answers the 3.1 agenda's blind spot (integration without specificity) and is the direct companion to 3.12's "firm as incentive system"; connects to 2.4 agency theory and anticipates the 4.2 "subeconomy" view.

[grounding: full-text (JLEO 7 special issue, pp. 24–52); intro pp. 24–28; ownership & fixed-wage results §3; job-design Props. 5–7 at pp. 44–49]

3.12 · Holmström & Milgrom (1994) — The Firm as an Incentive System

Pitch. Integrates the multitask incentive theory with asset-ownership theory: ownership is one instrument among several (pay, asset assignment, activity constraints), and firms bundle them into coherent systems — which is why "the firm" is best described as an incentive system rather than as a solution to hold-up.

Question. What determines which activities are performed inside a firm, by employees, versus through markets with owners/contractors — if the answer must come from incentive considerations rather than asset specificity?

Argument. (paper:) Extends 3.11: an agent allocates effort between activities the principal values and activities the agent can turn to private or outside uses; which activities are available to the agent is itself a design choice, made through ownership of assets and through rules. Because instruments interact, an agent who owns (or controls) an asset he works with is exposed to stronger incentives and needs higher-powered pay to focus on the principal's task; but giving the agent ownership of an asset that could serve his own business also raises the opportunity cost of the principal's task, so firms often retain ownership of precisely those assets and pay fixed wages, constraining outside activities instead. Ownership and incentive intensity are complementary design variables, and the observed contrast — low-powered incentives and firm ownership of key assets inside firms; high-powered incentives and agent ownership (franchises, sales representatives owning client lists) outside — emerges without any specific investments. (note: canonical summary; the AER paper operationalizes "the firm as an incentive system," extending the 1991 multitask model to asset ownership and job design jointly. The corpus PDF tagged 3.12 is a mislabeled acquisition — it contains Holmström's 1999 RES career-concerns paper, not this 1994 AER paper — so this digest relies on canonical knowledge plus the abstract.)

Evidence. Formal model with propositions linking ownership, incentive pay, and activity restrictions; consistent with the franchising/sales-force patterns discussed in 3.11.

Findings. Firms are bundles of complementary instruments: pay power, asset ownership, and permissible activities move together, and boundaries follow from measurement and incentive coherence rather than from quasi-rent appropriation.

Position. Completes the Holmström–Milgrom counter-program to 3.1: ownership matters for incentives even with complete contracts and no specificity; feeds 4.2 (the firm as a subeconomy) and 4.3's critique of PRT's neglect of incentives.

3.13 · Baker, Gibbons & Murphy (2002) — Relational Contracts and the Theory of the Firm

Pitch. Rebuilds firm-boundary theory on self-enforcing relational contracts: since real agreements rest on the parties' ongoing relationship rather than on courts, asset ownership matters because it changes what each party can credibly promise — and integration can either support or destroy relational contracts.

Question. How do ownership and integration affect the feasibility and efficiency of agreements that cannot be enforced by third parties?

Argument. (paper:) Two parties transact repeatedly; the valuable agreements (quality, investment, deference) are relational — enforced by the shadow of the future, not the courts. Ownership of physical assets determines the disagreement points and hence the temptations to renege inside any relational contract. The central mechanism: whether integration helps depends on how it shifts the parties' payoffs from continuing versus cheating on the relationship. Under nonintegration, a party who cheats (e.g., withholds quality) still owns his assets and keeps their value; under integration, the boss owns the assets, so the subordinate's threat to leave is weaker and his "outside option" is only his human capital. Integration therefore concentrates power, which can strengthen the boss's relational promises (she controls the asset) but can also weaken the subordinate's incentive to supply noncontractible effort — reversing the usual TCE presumption that integration is the safe harbor. A leading application: the boss/worker split of authority and the choice of employment vs. arms-length contracting tracks whose noncontractible contribution is more important to sustain. (note: canonical summary of the model's ownership/bargaining results.)

Evidence. An infinitely repeated-game model with relational contracts, analyzed formally; the paper also develops the "informal authority" variant (a 1999 companion) where the boss's right to decide rests on relational enforcement.

Findings. Ownership in relational settings is about shifting the set of self-enforcing agreements; integration is valuable when it lets the boss credibly commit, costly when it kills the subordinate's initiative or outside opportunity.

Position. Gives the PRT question an explicitly dynamic, reputation-based foundation, linking 3.1/3.3 to Macaulay's relational contracting (2.7) and anticipating 3.17's delegation and 4.4's "four theories" framing.

3.14 · Chiu (1998) — Noncooperative Bargaining, Hostages, and Optimal Asset Ownership

Pitch. Replaces GHM's cooperative surplus-splitting with explicit noncooperative (Rubinstein) bargaining and shows the central GHM claim can flip: adding an asset to someone's ownership can reduce his investment incentive, because ownership changes outside options in ways the Shapley-value shortcut misses.

Question. Does the Grossman–Hart conclusion — ownership increases the owner's investment incentives — survive when ex post bargaining is modeled noncooperatively with binding outside options?

Argument. (paper:) In the GHM framework (3.1), ex post surplus is divided cooperatively (50:50 or Shapley), so owning an asset always raises the owner's disagreement payoff and hence his incentive to invest. Chiu models date-1 bargaining as alternating-offer (Rubinstein) bargaining with outside options, where a party's outside option matters only if it exceeds what he would get inside bargaining. This discontinuity changes the ownership-incentive mapping: if the owner's outside option is already binding or if ownership hands a "hostage" to the other party, marginal additions to ownership can depress the owner's ex ante investment while raising the non-owner's — the reverse of the standard result. Optimal ownership then depends on the fine structure of who can threaten what at the bargaining table, and the clean prescription "give assets to the party whose investment matters more" no longer holds in general. (note: canonical summary of the paper's counterexample logic and hostage analysis.)

Evidence. Formal noncooperative bargaining model with propositions demonstrating the reversal; numerical/parametric examples showing ownership allocations that the cooperative model would never pick.

Findings. Ownership effects are bargaining-model-dependent: under noncooperative bargaining with hostages/outside options, ownership can over- or under-motivate in ways GHM's cooperative shortcut conceals.

Position. Part of the 1990s wave (with 3.15) testing PRT's robustness to bargaining foundations; cited in 3.17's opening as a reason ownership is sometimes withheld; connects to the general critique in 4.1/4.3.

3.15 · De Meza & Lockwood (1998) — Does Asset Ownership Always Motivate Managers?

Pitch. Another foundation crack: once the parties' outside options respond endogenously to investment, ownership can over-motivate — the owner may invest too much in improving his outside option and too little in the relationship — so the GHM monotonicity result fails.

Question. Is it always true (as in 3.1/3.3) that assigning an asset to a manager raises his relationship-specific investment incentives?

Argument. (paper:) GHM assumes ex post surplus division that gives the owner a bigger share, motivating more investment. De Meza and Lockwood embed the model in a setting where each party has an outside option whose value depends on the investments made. Once outside options are endogenous, ownership has two effects on the owner: it raises his inside bargaining share (good for incentives) but it also raises the value of his outside opportunity, and his investments may be directed at improving that outside option rather than the relationship. Under plausible parameter configurations the owner's investment exceeds the efficient level in one direction while the non-owner underinvests — or the non-owner invests more than the owner — contradicting the property-rights prescription to concentrate ownership on the party with the important investment. Hence "does asset ownership always motivate managers?" The answer is no: ownership can over-motivate in the sense of distorting effort toward outside-value-enhancing uses. (note: canonical summary of the model's reversal.)

Evidence. Formal two-party model with explicit outside-option functions and propositions identifying when the standard result holds and when it reverses; no data.

Findings. The ownership → incentives monotonicity of GHM requires the parties' outside options to be unresponsive to investment; with responsive outside options, the optimal ownership allocation is subtler and the theory's predictive sharpness drops.

Position. With 3.14, a key internal robustness critique of 3.1/3.3's bargaining shortcut; both are cited by 3.17's introduction as reasons ownership may be deliberately withheld; their challenge feeds 4.1 and the broader "new directions" debates (4.5, 4.12).

3.16 · Segal (1999) — Complexity and Renegotiation

Pitch. An independent foundation for incomplete contracts from the complexity side: as the environment gets richer (more potential trades), the null contract becomes optimal even when everything is describable — because renegotiation plus incentive compatibility leaves contracts nothing to work with.

Question. Can contractual incompleteness be derived from environmental complexity alone, without assuming that contingencies are indescribable?

Argument. (paper:) Segal analyzes a buyer-seller relationship with many potential states/trades ("complexity" indexed by the number of possible widgets or terms of trade), ex ante investment, and mandatory renegotiation under symmetric information. His result mirrors and generalizes the mechanism-design discipline of 3.5: when the number of possible trades is large, no contract — however sophisticated — can give the investing party materially better incentives than the null contract, because any contractually specified outcome is renegotiated to the efficient trade and the mechanism can only screen states coarsely. The driving force is the parties' inability to commit not to renegotiate combined with the richness of the environment: incentive constraints across the many states bind the contract to delivering roughly state-independent payoffs, so investment incentives collapse to what bargaining-from-scratch delivers. Where the environment is thin (few states), specific-performance-type contracts can still work — which is why describability and renegotiation each matter only under particular conditions. (note: canonical summary of the "complexity" foundation.)

Evidence. Formal theorem in a buyer-seller model with N potential trades showing convergence of optimal-contract investment to the null-contract level as N grows; an important methodological sibling of 3.5 with a distinct (complexity-based) construction.

Findings. Complexity plus renegotiation endogenously generates contractual incompleteness; the result undercuts the requirement that incomplete-contracting theory posit unexplainable contingencies.

Position. Co-develops the foundations that 3.5 explicitly builds on (HM 1999 credits Segal's construction); together they answer Maskin–Tirole (4.1), and the paper is cited by 3.17 for the view that incompleteness can be endogenous.

3.17 · Hart & Holmström (2010) — A Theory of Firm Scope

Pitch. Moves PRT from "who owns which assets" to "who decides what" and from owner-managers to large firms: integration is about concentrating profit-driven decisions while sacrificing the private benefits of unit managers and workers — and the model finally accommodates delegation via reference points and shading.

Question. When should units that impose externalities on each other be in one firm, and when should they stay separate — and what makes delegation a real intermediate form?

Argument. Two units A and B each choose Yes/No on coordination (common standards, compatible curricula); profits v are divertible by the boss, private benefits w (autonomy/job satisfaction) are nontransferable. Nonintegration: each unit's manager-boss maximizes her own v + w, ignoring the other unit — so coordination fails when its gains are unevenly split (too little coordination). Integration under a professional boss: she maximizes total profit v_A + v_B, internalizing cross-unit profit externalities but ignoring private benefits — so she coordinates too much when coordination is profitable but destroys autonomy (Δw ≤ 0). Since the boss can divert profits, profit-sharing cannot fix the trade-off; the first-best is reached under nonintegration when non-coordination is efficient and under integration when coordination is (Prop. 1). To justify why ex ante organizational form matters and to allow delegation, Section 3 adds the Hart–Moore (2008) reference-point machinery: contracts/organizational forms set entitlements; a party who gets less than his best contractual outcome is aggrieved and shades, imposing deadweight losses θ·(shortfall). Shading makes the boss partly internalize managers' preferences (Prop. 2) and makes delegation credible: because reversing delegated authority counts as hold-up that raises shading (θ̄ > θ), the boss reverses only in extreme states — delegation is an intermediate governance form yielding (weakly) less coordination than integration (Props. 3–4), strictly optimal only under uncertainty.

Evidence. Formal propositions; a Cisco case study: buying StrataCom (1996, $4.7bn, stock +10%) to control ATM-IOS integration rather than to avoid hold-up, and the "Mario rule" against firing acquired employees read as delegation; Schoar's finding that diversified firms' plants are ~7% more productive but pay ~8% more is cited as evidence that private benefits are priced.

Findings. Boundaries matter because integration reweights profits against private benefits; delegation, unintelligible in standard PRT, becomes meaningful once reversing it triggers shading.

Position. Refocuses the 3.1–3.3 asset-ownership agenda onto activities and decision rights (answering the large-firm critique of 3.4); builds directly on Hart & Moore's reference points (the behavioral turn in 3.19) and cites 3.8, 3.13–3.15 as related authority/incentive work; answered by 4.6-style critiques.

3.18 · Aghion, Dewatripont, Legros & Zingales (2016) — The Impact of Incomplete Contracts on Economics (Introduction)

Pitch. The editors' retrospective on the GHM revolution: incomplete-contracting theory changed how economics models ownership, control, and the firm, and this introduction maps what the program achieved, where it was attacked, and which frontiers remain open.

Question. What has the incomplete-contract (property-rights) research program contributed to economics, and where does it stand thirty years on?

Argument. (paper:) The volume gathers the field's key papers and retrospective essays around the GHM framework (3.1–3.3, 3.5–3.6) plus its critics (Maskin–Tirole, 4.1). The introduction argues that the program's enduring contribution is methodological: it showed that when contracts are incomplete, the allocation of residual control rights is a first-order economic variable — organizing not only the theory of the firm but also finance (3.6, 3.4), corporate governance, privatization, and the theory of authority. It also candidly reviews the unresolved tensions: the foundations debate over describability/commitment, the theory's weak fit for large public corporations, the need for behavioral ingredients (reference points, 3.19), and the thin empirical record — pointing to the very critiques developed in Stream 4 and the empirical work of Stream 5. (note: canonical description of the volume's framing; details of individual contributed chapters are not summarized here.)

Evidence. An edited volume assembling theoretical contributions, with the introduction providing synthesis rather than new data.

Findings. PRT's legacy is the control-rights lens applied across economics; its future lies in better foundations, behavioral extensions, and serious empirics.

Position. A self-assessment at the end of the GHM development line: it consolidates 3.1–3.17 and frames the debate with Stream 4's critiques and Stream 5's evidence as the program's next phase.

3.19 · Hart (2017) — Incomplete Contracts and Control (Nobel Lecture)

Pitch. The Nobel lecture's honest arc: the property-rights theory that won the prize works best when contracts are absent ex post but completeable ex ante; adding the reality that parties cannot commit not to renegotiate — and that contracts act as reference points for entitlements — yields a theory of control with shading and deadweight losses, and explains authority and firm boundaries anew.

Question. How should we model control and the firm once we take seriously that contracts are incomplete both ex ante and ex post, and that aggrievement is costly?

Argument. (paper:) The lecture restates the GHM logic (3.1–3.3): residual control over assets allocates ex post bargaining power and hence ex ante investment incentives. It then confronts the theory's weaknesses (it fits owner-managed firms; ex post Coasian bargaining makes initial ownership irrelevant in many settings) and introduces the reference-point approach developed with John Moore (2008): a contract specifies the parties' entitlements; within the contract's "win-set" parties may feel differently about outcomes, and the party who receives less than his most preferred contracted outcome is aggrieved and shades on performance in noncontractible ways, imposing real deadweight losses. Because renegotiation is then costly and who holds authority determines whose entitlements get priority, the ex ante allocation of control rights matters even without specific investments. This framework explains fixed wages and employment (the employee accepts a limited entitlement set), why bosses sometimes delegate and sometimes intervene, and why ownership (rather than mere promises) anchors commitments; ex ante choice of contract/ownership is distinct from ex post outcomes precisely because aggrievement makes renegotiation destructive. (note: canonical summary of the lecture's program.)

Evidence. A survey lecture: no new data; formal reasoning and examples drawn from the reference-point model and its applications (also the basis of 3.17).

Findings. Reference points and shading give incomplete contracts "bite" ex post, reviving the theory of control for large firms and relational settings that the original GHM model could not reach.

Position. The capstone of the GHM line: it restates 3.1–3.3, absorbs the foundations debate (3.5, 3.16, 4.1) by changing the primitive (from nonverifiability to aggrievement), and hands the program to the behavioral/incentive extensions in 3.17 and Stream 4.

Stream 3 one-paragraph synthesis

Stream 3 is the modern property-rights core: Grossman & Hart (1986) and Hart & Moore (1990) define the firm by asset ownership, derive integration as the optimal allocation of residual control rights under noncontractible investments, and generate the canonical predictions — ownership to important investors and indispensable partners, complementary assets owned together, independent assets kept apart. The stream's first turning point is the foundations turn: Hart–Moore (1999) and Segal (1999) show that renegotiation plus complexity makes the null contract optimal, defending incompleteness against Maskin–Tirole but conceding that the theory's bargaining shortcuts (Shapley division) are load-bearing; Chiu (1998) and De Meza–Lockwood (1998) exploit exactly that, showing ownership can under- or over-motivate when bargaining and outside options are modeled properly. The second turning point is the internal-organization turn: Holmström–Milgrom (1991, 1994) and Baker–Gibbons–Murphy (2002) tie ownership to the whole incentive system and to relational contracts, while Aghion–Tirole (1997) and Hart–Holmström (2010) push control rights from assets to decisions and authority, ending in the reference-points model of Hart (2017). The third turning point is self-assessment: Hart (1995) and Aghion–Dewatripont–Legros–Zingales (2016) consolidate the program and concede its limits — large firms, empirics, foundations — handing the baton to Streams 4 and 5. The stream thus supplies the theoretical spine of the whole review: precise, testable ownership predictions, and a clear statement of where and why they fail.

4.1 · Maskin & Tirole (1999) — Unforeseen Contingencies and Property Rights

Pitch. The irrelevance theorem: if parties can anticipate all payoff-relevant states, the mere inability to enumerate contingencies or actions ex ante does not prevent them from writing contracts that implement first-best outcomes — so "unforeseen contingencies" alone cannot ground contractual incompleteness, and therefore cannot ground the property-rights case for ownership.

Question. Can the hold-up problem and the Grossman–Hart–Moore premise — that residual control rights matter because contingencies cannot be contracted on — be derived from the sheer presence of unforeseen contingencies?

Argument. Maskin and Tirole model a setting where the payoffs of every state are describable even when states themselves cannot be listed in advance. They invoke mechanism design: parties can write a message game (in the spirit of Moore–Repullo) such that, in equilibrium, the implemented outcome coincides with the complete-contracting benchmark; with risk neutrality and ex ante transfers, relation-specific investments reach first-best levels. Contractual incompleteness therefore needs an extra friction — the cost of specifying states, complexity, or bounded rationality — and cannot be taken as primitive.

Evidence. Formal propositions (canonical knowledge, developed in the authors' 1999 companion articles, e.g., Review of Economic Studies). The companion literature supplies the response: complexity arguments (Segal 1999, 3.16 in this corpus) and behavioral foundations (Hart–Moore 1999, 3.5) show when the theorem fails.

Findings. GHM-style PRT cannot justify itself by pointing to unforeseeable contingencies; ownership effects require a genuine, modeled source of contractual friction.

Position. Attacks the incomplete-contracting foundation beneath 3.1–3.3 (Grossman–Hart 1986; Hart–Moore 1990); answered within this corpus by 3.5 (Hart & Moore, "Foundations of Incomplete Contracts") and 3.16 (Segal), which supply the complexity rationale the theorem denies.

4.2 · Holmström (1999) — The Firm as a Subeconomy

Pitch. Firms are not just clusters of owned assets or transaction-cost-minimizing hierarchies; they are "subeconomies" that deliberately shelter internal activity from market forces, and this sheltering — not ownership or hold-up — is what boundaries and internal organization are about.

Question. If markets are such powerful incentive devices, why would firms systematically suppress them — paying salaries, muting commissions, blocking outside offers — and what does this say about the source of firm boundaries?

Argument. Building on the multitask logic of 3.11–3.12 (Holmström–Milgrom), Holmström treats ownership, incentives, job design, and authority as complements that firms must coordinate as a package. High-powered market incentives distort behavior inside firms (multitask misallocation, rat races, influence activities), so the firm's central function is to dampen market forces selectively — to create an internal "subeconomy" with its own rules replacing price signals. Because selective intervention fails (one cannot expose units to market discipline selectively without wrecking careers, cooperation, and long-term investments), the boundary between firm and market is where this damping stops.

Evidence. Argumentative and example-based (canonical knowledge): why employees who own no assets still work effectively; why inside sales forces take low commissions and salaries; why internal transfer pricing is politically fraught; the firm as coordinating multiple instruments simultaneously.

Findings. Ownership is one instrument among many; the theory of the firm should center on which market forces a firm blocks, and on what it substitutes for them — an internal governance "constitution."

Position. Answers 3.1–3.3 (ownership-centered PRT) and the market-failure framing of 2.2/2.9; deepens 3.12 (Holmström–Milgrom); 4.3 (Holmström & Roberts) and 4.4 (Gibbons) codify it as the "incentive-system theory" alongside PRT.

4.3 · Holmström & Roberts (1998) — The Boundaries of the Firm Revisited

Pitch. The theory of the firm has become too narrowly focused on hold-up and asset specificity: ownership boundaries serve many purposes, and investment incentives are provided by many instruments, so both transaction-cost and property-rights models mis-predict the ownership patterns one actually observes.

Question. Can the observed variety of firm boundaries — Japanese subcontracting, exclusive sourcing, airline alliances, spin-offs — be explained by hold-up and asset-specificity considerations alone?

Argument. After contrasting the two dominant strands — Williamson's TCE (frequency, uncertainty, asset specificity; market as default "black box") and GHM's PRT (firm as jointly owned assets; non-contractible investments; underinvestment) — the paper argues that ownership is but one cell in a matrix of coordination/motivation problems × instruments. Relational safeguards, reputation, monitoring, and market exposure substitute for ownership in protecting specific investments; and ownership is chosen in response to agency problems, knowledge transfers, and common-asset protection as much as to investment incentives (pp. 74–79, 91–92).

Evidence. Case material throughout: Japanese auto suppliers design parts and own the dies their U.S. counterparts' automakers own (pp. 80–82; only 3 of ~150 Toyota suppliers left the kyohokai association in eleven years); Nucor's sole-supplier scrap deal with DJJ and Co Steel's on-premises "charges" (pp. 83–84); BSkyB, Topsy Tail, Microsoft, Genentech's ~10 marketing + 20 licensing + 15+ research partners (pp. 84–85); sales-force agency studies and gasoline-retail franchising results of Shepard (1993) and Slade (1996) (pp. 86–89); Thermo Electron spin-offs for market monitoring (pp. 89–90); knowledge sharing at ABB (>1,300 business units) and BP (peer assists) (pp. 90–91).

Findings. High frequency and mutual dependence support — rather than hinder — cross-boundary cooperation; a usable theory must treat "contractual assets," power beyond asset ownership, and substitutes for ownership as first-class instruments.

Position. Answers both 2.2/2.9 (Williamson) and 3.1–3.3 (GHM); the economics-side statement of the critique that 4.2 (Holmström) formalizes and that 4.4 maps; explicitly points to Rajan & Zingales (4.5) and relational-contract work (3.13) as the way forward.

4.4 · Gibbons (2005) — Four Formal(izable) Theories of the Firm?

Pitch. The boundary-of-the-firm literature contains not one theory but four — rent-seeking, property rights, incentive systems, and adaptation — each with a distinct distortion, instrument, and empirical signature; keeping them apart matters because they are routinely conflated (especially TCE and PRT).

Question. What are the leading economic theories of firm boundaries, how do they really differ, and which of them can be (or already are) given formal statement?

Argument. Gibbons separates the four: (1) rent-seeking/influence-cost theory (Milgrom–Roberts): the distortion is influence activity; the tools are flat organizations and restricted intervention; (2) property-rights theory (GHM): the distortion is ex ante underinvestment from hold-up; the tool is asset ownership; (3) incentive-system theory (Holmström–Milgrom): the distortion is multitask misallocation; the tools are the coordinated package of low-powered incentives, ownership, and job design; (4) adaptation theory (Williamson's TCE, plus relational contracting à la Baker–Gibbons–Murphy 2002): the distortion is poor coordination of unanticipated change; the tools are authority, decision rights, and relational contracts. Each is "formal(izable)" — a formal model exists or can be built. The theories differ on the phenomena they explain (who owns assets, why firms use flat pay, why integration helps adaptation), and empirical tests built for one logic (e.g., asset-specificity regressions for TCE) do not speak to the others.

Evidence. Contrastive reconstruction of the four literatures and their canonical models; worked comparisons of what each predicts for employment, make-versus-buy, and ownership (canonical knowledge).

Findings. Progress requires researchers to declare which of the four distortions they are modeling and to stop borrowing instruments across theories without their attendant assumptions.

Position. Maps 3.1–3.3 (PRT) against 3.11–3.12 (incentive system), 2.2/2.9 (adaptation), and 2.11 (influence costs); systematizes the 4.2/4.3 push for a broader theory and provides the taxonomy on which 4.10 and 4.12 build.

4.5 · Rajan & Zingales (1998) — Power in a Theory of the Firm

Pitch. Within the property-rights framework's own assumptions, ownership of physical assets is not the only — or the most effective — source of power: regulating access to a critical resource gives agents power that is more contingent on their making the right specific investment, and ownership itself can dampen the incentive to specialize.

Question. If power (not price) governs intra-firm transactions, where does that power come from, and is asset ownership the best way to create it and to define the firm?

Argument. The firm offers agents who make relationship-specific investments power. GHM locates power in ownership of physical assets; Rajan and Zingales widen its source: access — the ability to use or work with a critical resource (a machine, an idea, a person) — lets a manager specialize her human capital and thereby acquire bargaining power. In a Shapley-value bargaining model, the owner rations access: with substitute investments one manager gets access (Proposition 1); with additive investments several do, since a "rat race" raises aggregate investment (Proposition 2); with complementary investments one manager again suffices, multiple complementary investors holding too much power. Ownership has a dark side: when specialization burns outside options an owner's incentive to invest falls (Proposition 4; Hart–Moore had assumed outside values weakly rise with investment), and one owner's stake crowds out the rat race that access generates (Proposition 5). This yields third-party ownership, insecurity as a spur to investment, and internal organization as the regulation of internal access.

Evidence. Formal propositions with proofs and worked applications: Fisher Body's refusal to locate plants near GM as the textbook "adverse effect of ownership"; GM's ~750,000 workers making ~8m cars vs. Toyota's ~65,000 making >4.5m in the late 1980s; state ownership (mints); arbitration as third-party control (NBER WP pp. 19–36; § II–VI).

Findings. A firm is best defined as a set of critical resources plus the people granted access to them; such organizations can exist even without enforceable property rights (e.g., the Mafia), and internal organization adds value by rationing power.

Position. Extends 3.1–3.3 (GHM) to a broader theory of power while attacking GHM's identification of power with asset ownership; complements 3.7–3.8 (Aghion–Tirole) on alternative sources of authority; answered by 4.6, which takes access-based organization to hierarchy, growth, and internal careers.

4.6 · Rajan & Zingales (2001) — The Firm as a Dedicated Hierarchy

Pitch. Taking 4.5's access logic to the origin and growth of firms: an entrepreneur with a critical resource builds the organization by meting out access so employees specialize rather than expropriate; the resulting hierarchy — steep or flat — protects organizational rents from insiders and determines firm growth and size.

Question. How can an entrepreneur induce employees who must be given close access (to learn the idea, the clients, the technique) to protect rather than steal the source of organizational rents — and what internal structure, growth path, and size follow?

Argument. Access lets managers learn; learning lets them expropriate and compete. A specialized manager is loyal — his firm-specific investment is worthless in a rival team — so the entrepreneur sequences access and promotion: hire one at a time, promote by seniority, and let each cohort of specialized managers act as a "Praetorian guard" defeating any defecting newcomer (Proposition 1). Because a specialized manager's rents rise with the subordinates whose access he mediates, hierarchy creates positional power; a steep hierarchy (physical-capital industries, low expropriability) pays rents through promotion, while a flat hierarchy (human-capital industries, high expropriability — law and consulting) sells ownership internally to a select few, producing up-or-out careers and maximal differentiation (Propositions 2, 6, 7). Weak property rights keep hierarchies small; better enforcement enlarges them.

Evidence. Formal propositions and comparative statics, illustrated with the watchmaker example, Intel's founding from Fairchild, the finding that 71% of Inc. 500 founders replicated ideas from prior employers, Saxenian's Route 128 vs. Silicon Valley contrast (enforced vs. unenforced non-compete clauses), and cross-country evidence (Kumar–Rajan–Zingales 1999: larger firms in asset-intensive industries and under efficient judiciaries) (WP pp. 1–7, 21–22, 27).

Findings. Hierarchies grow because specialized employees become a critical resource themselves — a web of specific investments competitors cannot instantly replicate ("organizational capital"); organization, defined by power over access, need not coincide with legal ownership, which is why firms can exist where property rights are unenforceable (Section VI–VII).

Position. Extends 4.5 from a theory of power to a theory of internal structure, growth, and size; directly challenges the GHM (3.1/3.3) premise that firms require enforceable ownership of alienable assets; supplies internal-organization content that 4.3 and 4.7 call for.

4.7 · Zingales (2000) — In Search of New Foundations

Pitch. Corporate finance — capital structure, governance, valuation, even policy — is built on an implicit theory of the firm; the theories that served the asset-intensive, vertically integrated corporation cannot handle the emerging human-capital-intensive firm, so the theory of the firm must be rebuilt around the sources and uses of organizational power.

Question. What is the entity being financed, governed, and valued once firms are human-capital-intensive, non-integrated, and boundary-fuzzy — and what new questions must a theory of the firm answer to serve corporate finance again?

Argument. Zingales reads each incumbent theory of the firm through its corporate-finance consequences: the nexus of explicit contracts (Alchian–Demsetz, Jensen–Meckling) yields shareholder supremacy, DCF valuation, and event studies; adding implicit contracts introduces "organizational capital" and makes distress potentially permanent; Myers' growth options make underinvestment central but leave unexplained why options are glued to assets in place; GHM's property-rights view delivers debt theory but "failed to deliver a convincing model of outside equity" and cannot separate ownership from control. Four questions define the new agenda: (1) how an organization acquires power beyond ordinary market contracting without unique physical assets; (2) how that power is maintained, enhanced, and lost; (3) how internal authority works differently from markets; (4) how surplus is divided among members. The Rajan–Zingales access model is the illustration.

Evidence. Case vignettes: Saatchi & Saatchi (fund managers holding ~30% of votes triggered the exit of Maurice Saatchi and key executives, who took major accounts to M&C Saatchi); Autodaq.com issuing equity to suppliers as "glue"; IBM's and Bertelsmann's failures to seize internet growth options as "governance overhang"; the TDC valuation case (WP pp. 22, 28–30, 34–41).

Findings. When human capital dominates, financing and governance choices can redraw firm boundaries; the primary governance task shifts from protecting shareholders to preserving the integrity of the organization and aligning the capture of growth options with their rewards.

Position. Brings the 4.5/4.6 program into corporate finance against the nexus-of-contracts (2.4) and GHM (3.1–3.3) foundations; turns 4.3's "power derives from more than asset ownership" into a research agenda that 4.12 later reorients toward owners' judgment.

4.8 · Foss (1996) — Knowledge-Based Approaches to the Theory of the Firm: Some Critical Comments

Pitch. The knowledge-based theory of the firm (Kogut–Zander; Conner–Prahalad) explains firm existence by the superiority of internal knowledge transfer and coordination, but it does so without comparative institutional analysis and without behavioral assumptions, so it cannot yet stand as an alternative to contractual theories.

Question. Can "knowledge" and "coordination" — without opportunism, incentives, or a comparative benchmark — explain the existence, boundaries, and internal organization of firms?

Argument. Foss argues three things. First, knowledge-based accounts under-specify human behavior: they need bounded rationality to make knowledge transfer costly and must say what happens when parties disagree or defect, otherwise the theory is frictionless. Second, they commit the "Nirvana" fallacy: showing that markets transmit knowledge imperfectly does not show firms do it better — an internal market for knowledge faces the same incentive problems, and the replication/selective-intervention argument applies to knowledge flows across market boundaries too. Third, they neglect incentive and authority costs inside the firm: employees can hoard knowledge, and hierarchy is not free. The firm's knowledge advantages and its incentive problems must be theorized together.

Evidence. Analytical criticism of the knowledge-based program, principally directed at Kogut & Zander (1992) and Conner & Prahalad (1996) (canonical knowledge); Foss pairs the critique with a call to combine contractual and competence logics rather than to replace one with the other.

Findings. Knowledge-based theory is a necessary complement, not a substitute, for transaction-cost/property-rights reasoning; its claims need the same operational discipline (behavioral assumptions, comparative benchmarks, testable predictions) demanded of contractual theories.

Position. Within this corpus, intervenes between 2.12–2.13 (Winter; Conner–Prahalad) and the contractual line (2.2, 3.1–3.3); Williamson (4.10) cites it approvingly as a bridge critique, and it prefigures 4.12, where Foss et al. build an explicitly competence-based view of ownership on top of classical property-rights economics.

4.9 · Ghoshal & Moran (1996) — Bad for Practice: A Critique of the Transaction Cost Theory

Pitch. Williamson's transaction cost economics, applied normatively, is not merely incomplete but dangerous: built on opportunism and rational control, it sets off a self-fulfilling prophecy in which control breeds the very opportunism it was meant to suppress, and it blinds managers to the "organizational advantage" that distinguishes firms from markets.

Question. Is a theory that explains organizations as market-failure devices — "hierarchies" whose purpose is to attenuate opportunism through sanctions — a safe basis for managerial prescription?

Argument. Ghoshal and Moran "unpack" opportunism: Williamson conflates opportunism as an attitude with opportunistic behavior as an outcome, treating attitudes as fixed while making behavior a function of transaction characteristics and safeguards. Drawing on psychology (intrinsic motivation, reactance, dissonance), they model the feedback: fiat, monitoring, and control raise the cost of some opportunistic acts but erode the "feeling for the entity," increasing the propensity to behave opportunistically and pushing employees from "consummate cooperation" to "perfunctory compliance" (work-to-rule). The net effect is theoretically ambiguous and empirically likely positive — control causes the opportunism it presumes, a "double hermeneutic" in which the theory brings out the worst in the behavior it assumes. Firms trapped in rational control gravitate to measurable, low-uncertainty domains where markets beat them ("in the end, markets"). Firms' real advantage lies in purposive adaptation: shared purpose plays the role in organizations that price plays in markets, enabling action on missing markets, dynamic efficiency, and innovation.

Evidence. Argument plus assembled social-psychological evidence (Strickland's "dilemma of the supervisor"; Enzle–Anderson surveillance experiments; crowding-out results) and prior critiques by Hill, Hart, and North (AMR pp. 17–30, 38–42).

Findings. TCE has some value as positive theory but its normative application destroys organizational advantage; strategy scholars should build a theory of the "organizational economy" rather than bolt "trust" onto a market-failure framework.

Position. The strongest management-side assault on 2.2/2.9 (Williamson); runs parallel to the economics-side critique in 4.3; answered directly by Williamson in 4.10, and its "purposive adaptation / moral factor" themes echo in 4.2's subeconomy argument.

4.10 · Williamson (1999) — Strategy Research: Governance and Competence Perspectives

Pitch. The governance (transaction cost) and competence (capabilities) perspectives are rivals but ultimately complements: governance has been operationalized through six key moves, and competence should be held to the same standard; most competence-based criticisms of TCE are mistaken or overdrawn, but the constructive ones — holism, firm-level strategy, and learning — define a shared research agenda.

Question. How should the governance and competence perspectives each be operationalized for strategy research, and which of their mutual criticisms are real?

Argument. Williamson rehearses the six moves that made TCE operational: behavioral assumptions on human actors (bounded rationality, foresight, opportunism); the transaction as unit of analysis (frequency, uncertainty, asset specificity); the firm as governance structure rather than production function; the economizing purpose (discriminating alignment; the k=0/k>0, s=0/s>0 schema); the empirical record; the remediableness criterion. He runs the competence perspective (Penrose, Richardson, Nelson–Winter routines, Prahalad–Hamel, Teece–Pisano–Shuen) through the same six moves, finding tautological definitions, ex post rationalization, and no account of the limits to firm size. Against competence critics he defends opportunism (zeroing it out eliminates the incentive, control, and contract-law differences that define modes of governance), rejects the "static TCE" charge (the Fundamental Transformation is already intertemporal), and concedes management deserves more attention. Constructive moves: from piecemeal transactions to interaction effects; from generic governance to firm-specific strategy — "How should firm A, with its pre-existing strengths and weaknesses, organize X?" (Table 1's six levels); and learning, where Levinthal–March's myopia findings complement the remediableness lens.

Evidence. Empirical claims: 400+ published TCE studies by the late 1990s with "remarkable congruity" of theory and evidence, and Joskow's judgment that the work is in better shape than most industrial-organization empirics (SMJ pp. 1091–1092). The competence treatment leans on cited success stories (GTE vs. NEC) and the Cyert–March department-store pricing routines.

Findings. Governance and competence are "rival and complementary — more the latter than the former"; both are bounded-rationality constructions, and competence's path to usefulness runs through operationalization with asset specificity still central.

Position. The TCE principal's answer to 4.9 (Ghoshal–Moran) and to the knowledge/competence camp of 2.12–2.13 and 4.8; frames the generic-particular division of labor that 4.12 inverts by making owners, not transactions, the carriers of competence.

4.11 · Williamson (2000) — The New Institutional Economics: Taking Stock, Looking Ahead

Pitch. The New Institutional Economics organizes itself around four levels of social analysis — embeddedness, the institutional environment, governance, and resource allocation — and its stocktaking conclusion is that each level economizes in its own way, with transaction cost economics as the operationalized core of the governance level.

Question. What has the New Institutional Economics (NIE) achieved — Coase, North, and the transaction cost program — and what does its research agenda look like?

Argument. Williamson lays out the four-level schema: Level 1 is social embeddedness (religion, customs, informal institutions; social theory; change on a 100-to-1,000-year scale); Level 2 is the institutional environment — formal rules of the game, polity, judiciary, property rights (the economics of property rights; "first-order economizing"; 10–100 years); Level 3 is governance — the "play of the game," contracting and organization, where transaction cost economics operates ("second-order economizing"; 1–10 years); Level 4 is neoclassical resource allocation and employment (continuous). The levels are connected: the institutional environment provides the rules within which governance structures are chosen, and the paper takes stock of how NIE has moved from vague "transaction costs" to refutable, empirically corroborated alignment hypotheses, while looking ahead to open issues (remediableness, the limits of calculativeness, the interplay of the levels, and institutions as cumulative).

Evidence. Survey/stocktaking of the NIE literature across its four levels, including the empirical success of TCE and the contributions of Coase and North (canonical knowledge).

Findings. Institutions matter, are susceptible to economic analysis, and operate at different speeds across levels; NIE's comparative, remediableness-based approach is the disciplined alternative to hypothetical-ideal benchmarking.

Position. The programmatic frame that absorbs 4.10 and answers the anti-governance critiques of 4.9 by relocating them at specific levels; positions property rights economics (Level 2) and TCE/PRT-style governance (Level 3) inside one research program — the target against which 4.5–4.7 and 4.12 argue.

4.12 · Foss, Klein, Lien, Zellweger & Zenger (2021) — Ownership Competence

Pitch. The incentive view of ownership — agency theory, incomplete contracting, and standard property-rights economics — treats owners as interchangeable and ownership as a motivator; instead, owners differ in the skill with which they own, and matching, governance, and timing competence explain value creation better than ownership stakes do.

Question. Why does who owns matter for value creation over and above how much is owned, and what is the competence with which ownership is exercised?

Argument. Incentive theories implicitly assume cognitively homogeneous actors who, once given ownership, deploy assets optimally; but owners hold heterogeneous, idiosyncratic theories of value creation that cannot be traded on "closed" strategic factor markets, so judgment can be tested only by assuming ownership. Anchoring in classical property-rights economics (Alchian's rights of use, appropriation, and transfer), the paper defines ownership competence as three skills: matching (what to own — foreseeing valuable resource combinations), governance (how to own — incentives, monitoring, rent-sharing, managerial selection), and timing (when to own — entry, staging, exit, and recognizing when another owner would do better). Value creation therefore varies with owner competence, moderated by ownership concentration, life-cycle stage, environmental uncertainty, and factor-market efficiency.

Evidence. Theory paper drawing on cited evidence: Sorensen (2007) on VC "smart money" (sorting plus influence; return persistence reflecting heterogeneity in investor skill); Hsu (2004) on high-reputation VCs buying equity at a 10–14% discount; Feldman & Montgomery (2015) on directors with large stakes but little top-level experience lowering firm value; Thomsen & Pedersen (2000) on owner identity mattering beyond ownership concentration (SMJ pp. 304–318).

Findings. Solving agency problems may matter less than minimizing the efficiency loss from separating ownership from competence: the market for corporate control primarily sorts assets to their most competent owners, and incompetent owners — not just bad incentives — are an ultimate source of inefficiency.

Position. Redirects the debate from "who should own to fix hold-up/incentive problems" (3.1–3.3, 3.12) and from access/power (4.5–4.7) to owners' judgment; completes the critique program begun in 4.8 by fusing competence with classical property-rights economics (Alchian, Barzel) and the judgment-based entrepreneurship line.

[grounding: full-text — SMJ 42(2): 302–328; abstract pp. 302–303, § 2 pp. 305–308, § 3 pp. 308–311, § 4 pp. 311–318, discussion/predictions pp. 318–322]

Stream 4 one-paragraph synthesis

Stream 4 is the debate stream of the PRT literature: it attacks the incomplete-contracting foundation, widens the source of power beyond asset ownership, and eventually redefines the question from incentives to competence. Three turning points structure the stream. The first is foundational: Maskin–Tirole's irrelevance theorem (4.1) shows that unforeseen contingencies alone cannot justify contract incompleteness, undercutting the 3.1–3.3 premise that ownership exists because contracts fail; Holmström's "subeconomy" (4.2) and Holmström–Roberts' boundary survey (4.3) then argue that hold-up and asset specificity are one problem among many, with Gibbons (4.4) sorting the field into four rival theories. The second is the power turn: Rajan–Zingales (4.5, 4.6) and Zingales (4.7) replace asset ownership with regulated access and the dedicated hierarchy, explaining organization where property rights are unenforceable and re-grounding corporate finance; Foss (4.8) and Ghoshal–Moran (4.9) press the competence and normative critiques that Williamson answers by operationalization (4.10, 4.11). The third is the competence turn: Foss, Klein, Lien, Zellweger and Zenger (4.12) convert ownership itself into a heterogeneous, learnable skill — matching, governance, timing — so that the stream ends by making owners' judgment, not assets or access, the unit of strategic analysis.

5.1 · Monteverde & Teece (1982) — Supplier Switching Costs and Vertical Integration in the Automobile Industry

Pitch. The first cross-component statistical test that relationship-specific engineering effort pushes production inside the firm: GM and Ford make rather than buy components whose development demands assembler-specific applications engineering. It anchors the empirical hold-up line that later separates transaction-cost (TCE) from property-rights (PRT) predictions.

Question. Does the amount of transaction-specific engineering effort required to develop an automotive component predict whether the assembler produces it in-house?

Argument. Preproduction component development generates assembler-specific design and production knowledge. A supplier that carries out this development gains a first-mover advantage, creating appropriable quasi rents; the threat of ex post holdup makes integrated production more likely the greater the required applications engineering effort. The test is a probit of the make/buy choice.

Evidence. 133 automotive component groupings, coded for GM and Ford U.S. production in 1976; "integrated" = 80 percent or more of the component produced internally. The specificity proxy is a design engineer's rating of engineering effort per component (validated against a second engineer's ratings, correlation ≈ 0.8), plus a dummy for components specific to a single assembler and subsystem dummies. In the probit, the engineering-effort rating is positive and significant (t ≈ 3.6 at the 80 percent threshold) and robust to in-house cutoffs of 70 and 90 percent; the assembler-specific dummy is positive and significant; GM is more integrated than Ford. note: coefficients from the article text (Table 2), cited as canonical knowledge per this paper's assignment.

Findings. Components requiring heavier and more assembler-specific development effort are significantly more likely to be made in-house, supporting the specificity/quasi-rent logic at the transaction level.

Position. An empirical forerunner of the GHM hold-up mechanism (3.1, 3.3), though framed in Klein–Crawford–Alchian/Williamson terms (2.3, 2.9); it opens the make-or-buy empirical line extended by 5.4/5.5, 5.6, and 5.11, and is catalogued in 5.12.

5.2 · Joskow (1987) — Contract Duration and Relationship-Specific Investments

Pitch. Seminal evidence from U.S. coal markets that contract structure responds to the threat of hold-up: the duration of coal supply contracts rises steeply with relationship-specific investments. It demonstrates that parties substitute ex ante contractual commitment for repeated bargaining exactly where quasi rents are large.

Question. Do buyers and sellers commit to longer contractual terms when relationship-specific investments are more important?

Argument. Site and physical specificity (a generating plant at the mine mouth, dedicated reserves, single-source dependence) create ex post bilateral monopoly. Longer contracts signed at the outset reduce the parties' exposure to repeated bargaining and opportunism; where specificity is low and the market thick, short-term or spot contracting suffices.

Evidence. 277 coal contracts between electric utilities and coal suppliers. For each contract the paper measures the duration agreed at the contract-execution stage and proxies relationship specificity — mine-mouth location, dedicated reserves, dependence on a single mine, and the availability of alternative suppliers. Regressions (interval/ordered models handling censored durations) show contract duration increases strongly with these specificity measures. note: headline magnitudes are reported directionally here; specific coefficients are not quoted.

Findings. Coal contracts last far longer — frequently decades where the mine feeds a dedicated, adjacent plant — when relationship-specific investments are large, and are short or spot-like when alternative supply is abundant.

Position. The decisive early contractual-governance companion to the integration evidence in 2.3/2.9 and to 5.3; Whinston (2001/3.10) and Lafontaine–Slade (5.12) later use it to argue that specificity results alone cannot distinguish TCE from PRT.

5.3 · Joskow (1988) — Asset Specificity and the Structure of Vertical Relationships

Pitch. Extends the coal evidence (5.2) from contract terms to the full governance menu, showing that increasing site specificity moves transacting from spot markets to long-term contracts to outright vertical integration. It maps specificity onto ownership, the margin PRT is built to explain.

Question. When does a coal-burning electric plant buy fuel on the spot market, under long-term contract, or own its mine outright?

Argument. Coal supply arrangements for electric generation run the gamut of discrete governance structures. As transport costs fall or the number of nearby mines rises, specificity declines and market procurement becomes workable; mine-mouth plants, with the largest sunk, co-located investments, are the natural candidates for vertical integration or very long contracts.

Evidence. Same coal-and-electricity setting as 5.2, examined at the plant/unit level: descriptive and regression evidence linking the form of the coal-supply arrangement to geographic proximity between mine and plant, dedicated reserves, and the thickness of the alternative-supplier market. note: direction of effects reported; exact samples and coefficients not quoted.

Findings. Vertical integration into coal mines and very long-term contracts concentrate where site specificity is greatest, while thick coal markets support spot purchases — the governance continuum tracks asset specificity.

Position. Bridges 5.2's contracting result and the ownership margin at the center of GHM (3.1/3.3); cited by 5.11 and by the surveys 5.12 and Whinston (3.10) as canonical TCE-era evidence whose discriminating power against PRT is limited.

5.4 · Baker & Hubbard (2003) — Make Versus Buy in Trucking: Asset Ownership, Job Design, and Information

Pitch. Tests PRT-plus-multitask logic on the shipper's margin using the diffusion of on-board computers (OBCs) as a shock to what can be monitored and coordinated. The twist: the same technology moves boundaries in opposite directions depending on which capability it adds.

Question. How does information technology that expands contractibility or improves coordination shift whether shippers run private fleets ("make") or hire for-hire carriers ("buy")?

Argument. Model of asset ownership in trucking in which the value of shipper ownership depends on job design and measurement: OBCs come in two classes — trip recorders that monitor driving (incentive-improving, making driver actions contractible) and electronic vehicle monitoring systems that transmit location and messages (resource-allocation- and coordination-improving). Incomplete contracts (Grossman–Hart) and job-design/measurement ideas (Holmström–Milgrom) frame the predictions.

Evidence. Truck-level Census data (Truck Inventory and Use Surveys) across 1987–1997, comparing how adoption of each OBC class related to shippers' use of private versus for-hire carriage. Headline result (verified from the abstract): OBCs' incentive-improving features pushed hauls toward private carriage; their coordination/resource-allocation-improving features pushed hauls toward for-hire carriage. note: specific coefficients not quoted.

Findings. Whether better information integrates or outsources a transaction depends on whether it makes noncontractible effort verifiable or improves inter-firm coordination — firm boundaries respond systematically to the contracting environment.

Position. Companion to 5.5 (which studies the driver-ownership margin); extends 3.1/3.3 and 3.11-style multitasking to a natural experiment; treated by 5.12 as simultaneously a monitoring, multitasking, and property-rights test.

5.5 · Baker & Hubbard (2004) — Contractibility and Asset Ownership: On-Board Computers and Governance in U.S. Trucking

Pitch. The cleanest quasi-experiment in this stream: whether drivers own their trucks (owner-operators) is governed by a trade-off between driving incentives and bargaining costs over backhauls, and the diffusion of on-board computers — which made good driving contractible — moved hauls from owner-operators to company drivers and firms grew more integrated.

Question. What determines whether the driver or the carrier owns the truck, and how does ownership respond when a monitoring technology makes driver behavior contractible?

Argument. Ownership of the truck carries residual control rights (industry convention: "no forced dispatch"), so driver ownership solves the agency problem of noncontractible good driving but creates "bargaining costs": dispatchers lose control over backhauls, inviting underinvestment in backhaul search, rent-seeking, and haggling (pp. 1446–50). OBCs reduce the first cost without affecting the second, so adoption should shift ownership to carriers, most on long hauls where the driving problem is worst.

Evidence. 1987 and 1992 Truck Inventory and Use Surveys (N = 19,308 and 35,204 truck-tractors). Owner-operator shares were higher on long hauls (0.211 for >200 miles in 1987) and for unidirectional-demand "no backhaul" trailers (0.181 vs. 0.140; Table I, p. 1453), and fell overall from 14.6 to 10.1 percent between 1987 and 1992. First-difference logits on state-product-trailer-distance cohorts imply that raising OBC adoption from 0 to 0.2 cut the owner-operator share 8–13 percent overall and 15–17 percent in the long-haul segment, accounting for between one-quarter and one-half of the 30 percent aggregate decline (pp. 1467–73; Tables V–VII). Fuel-economy regressions give an incentive effect of at least 0.16 mpg for long-haul company drivers with trip recorders (coefficients 0.289 vs. 0.127 for owner-operators; Table VIII, p. 1475).

Findings. Improvements in contractibility reduced independent contracting and increased asset integration inside carriers, especially where allocating control to drivers was costly; this is the prediction that ownership responds to the contracting environment, read off PRT (pp. 1476–77).

Position. Directly tests GHM residual-control logic (3.1/3.3) with exogenous-feeling technology diffusion; the driver-level counterpart to 5.4's shipper-level result; flagged in 5.12's monitoring tables and contrasted by 5.11.

[grounding: full-text — QJE 119(4):1443–79; framework and propositions pp. 1446–52; results pp. 1467–76]

5.6 · Woodruff (2002) — Non-Contractible Investments and Vertical Integration in the Mexican Footwear Industry

Pitch. A direct test of the PRT investment-allocation prediction in a developing-country market: ownership of retail distribution should go to the party — manufacturer or retailer — whose non-contractible investments matter more, rather than following specificity alone.

Question. Does the relative importance of non-contractible investments by footwear manufacturers versus their downstream retailers determine vertical integration and exclusivity in distribution?

Argument. Under GHM logic, residual control should be allocated to whoever bears the more important non-contractible investments. Footwear retailers make local, hard-to-contract investments in marketing and adaptation (proxied by the frequency of fashion change, which forces constant retailer effort), while manufacturers invest in product differentiation and brand value; the two margins push ownership in opposite directions.

Evidence. Survey and contracting data on Mexican footwear manufacturers and their retail relationships. Frequent fashion change — indexing downstream non-contractible effort and volatility — is associated with less manufacturer integration and less reliance on exclusive arrangements; measures of upstream product differentiation push the other way. note: directions reported; sample sizes and coefficients not quoted.

Findings. Retailers keep ownership where their non-contractible investments dominate, and lose it to manufacturers where brand/differentiation investments dominate — consistent with PRT and, as Lafontaine–Slade stress, a PRT-friendly reading of the negative risk–integration relationship.

Position. Extends 3.1/3.3 predictions to an arms-length, survey-based setting; 5.12 (Tables 1, 2, 8, 13) treats it as evidence where TCE and PRT predictions part company; complements the alliance/contract evidence of 5.7 and 5.8.

5.7 · Lerner & Merges (1998) — The Control of Technology Alliances: An Empirical Analysis of the Biotechnology Industry

Pitch. The canonical empirical study of control-rights allocation in R&D alliances, testing Aghion–Tirole's claim that rights go to the party whose non-contractible contribution matters most subject to bargaining power: financially healthier small biotech firms retain more control from their pharmaceutical partners.

Question. What determines which partner holds control rights in biotech–pharma technology alliances — the potential for underinvestment/opportunism, or relative bargaining power?

Argument. Following Aghion–Tirole (1994), control should go to the smaller firm when its marginal research contribution matters most (early-stage projects) and when its outside-financing position lets it bargain for rights. Three clinical case studies (ImmuLogic, ALZA, Repligen) motivate the coding of 24 control rights, each scored 1 if assigned to the larger, financing firm (plus 5 "key" rights covering trials, process development, manufacturing, and marketing).

Evidence. 200 alliances (1980–1995) coded by Recombinant Capital from SEC filings. Measures of the small firm's financial position (revenues, R&D, cash, total assets, shareholders' equity) correlate significantly negatively with the number of rights ceded to the larger firm (e.g., ≈ −0.24 for assets and equity on the 24-right measure; Table 5); in regressions a one-standard-deviation rise in shareholders' equity cuts the predicted number of rights going to the larger firm by ~10.4 percent, from 8.6 to 7.7 (Table 7). Prior-quarter biotech equity issuance also reduces rights ceded. The stage prediction fares worse: early-stage agreements allocate more, not fewer, rights to the financing firm, though stronger patent positions behave as theory predicts (pp. 23–27). The authors conclude that external-financing-driven bargaining power is the dominant driver (pp. 26–28).

Findings. Control-rights allocation in technology alliances tracks the smaller party's financial health and capital-market conditions far more strongly than the stage-based marginal-contribution logic; the interpretation is that financing constraints, not just ex post opportunism, allocate residual control.

Position. The full text available is the April 1997 NBER WP 6014 version of the Journal of Industrial Economics article (JIE 46(2):125–156, 1998); it operationalizes 3.7's Aghion–Tirole model, is extended by 5.8 to portal alliances and echoed in 5.9's VC contracts, and its bargaining-power mechanism reappears inside firms in 5.13.

5.8 · Elfenbein & Lerner (2003) — Ownership and Control Rights in Internet Portal Alliances, 1995–1999

Pitch. Transfers the Lerner–Merges (5.7) design to the first dot-com boom: content providers that needed portals for distribution, and portals that needed content for traffic, negotiated ownership (equity) and control provisions in several hundred agreements. The results extend the finding that financing constraints, more than project characteristics, allocate control rights.

Question. In alliances between internet portals and content providers, what explains who takes equity and who holds control rights?

Argument. Content providers were young, cash-hungry firms selling into a portal-controlled bottleneck, so the Aghion–Tirole bargaining-power mechanism should operate sharply: providers with stronger finances and alternatives should retain ownership and control, while financially constrained providers should cede both. Portal demand for content and provider dependence determine the deal's balance of rights.

Evidence. Coding of several hundred portal–content agreements signed 1995–1999, drawn from SEC filings, covering equity stakes, exclusivity, and governance/control terms, related to the financial condition of the content provider and market conditions. note: directions summarized; exact sample counts and coefficients not quoted.

5.9 · Kaplan & Strömberg (2003) — Financial Contracting Theory Meets the Real World: Evidence from Venture Capital Contracts

Pitch. Reads 200 actual VC contracts against five families of financial-contracting theory and finds that real contracts separate cash-flow, voting, board, liquidation, and control rights, make them contingent on performance, and shift control to the investor in bad states — the world of Aghion–Bolton/Dewatripont–Tirole, with strong hold-up-mitigating provisions.

Question. Do real-world venture capital contracts resemble the predictions of principal-agent, control, "stealing," inalienability, and screening theories?

Argument. VCs are theory's investor: value-maximizing with few private benefits of control. The contracts allocate cash-flow, voting, board, liquidation, and other control rights separately and make them state-contingent; founder vesting and non-competes raise the cost of the entrepreneur leaving, mitigating the Hart–Moore inalienability hold-up problem.

Evidence. 200 VC investments in 118 portfolio companies by 14 VC firms (financings 1987–1999, most 1996–1999). Convertible preferred appears in 189/200 rounds; participating preferred in 72. VCs hold voting control in 56 percent of financings (minimum-contingency case; Table 3), board control in 26 percent (boards average 6.1 seats; Table 4); liquidation claims exceed or equal the investment in 98 percent of rounds, and redemption/put rights appear in 84 percent. Automatic-conversion provisions appear in 94 percent, triggering at a median IPO price 3.0 times the round price (4.0 for early-stage; Table 8). Contingencies are pervasive: 36.5 percent of rounds include state-contingent provisions on financial/non-financial performance, actions, or securities sales (Table 7), with control shifting by voting in 17.5 percent of rounds; founder vesting in 42 percent (55 percent of pre-revenue rounds) and non-competes in ~70 percent of companies. Cross-sectional logits: VC control (voting/board) rises with pre-revenue status and financing round and falls for repeat entrepreneurs (Table 11). The authors conclude the contracts best match Aghion–Bolton (1992) and Dewatripont–Tirole (1994) control-transfer predictions and screening logic, with cash-flow incentives, control, and contingencies used as complements (pp. 1–45 of WP text; §§3–6).

Findings. Contracts are rich, state-contingent, multi-dimensional allocations of rights that shift from VC to entrepreneur as performance improves — closest to incomplete-contracting control theories and inalienability concerns, and poorly described by pure principal-agent, stealing, or single-mechanism security-design models.

Position. Extends 3.6/3.7-style control allocation to financial contracting between an investor and an entrepreneur (a financing, not a vertical, pair); shows the empirical centrality of separated, contingent control rights that 5.7 documents in alliances and 5.13 documents inside firms.

5.10 · Arora & Merges (2004) — Specialized Supply Firms, Property Rights and Firm Boundaries

Pitch. Argues that strong intellectual property rights can substitute for vertical integration as protection for specialized upstream investments: when a supplier's non-contractible contributions are patent-protected, the supplier can stay independent even opposite powerful downstream firms, qualifying the PRT allocation rule.

Question. When can specialized upstream supply firms remain outside the boundaries of downstream owners, and what role do property rights play?

Argument. In GHM, ownership is the safeguard for non-contractible investments because residual control is the only enforceable protection. Arora–Merges contend that intellectual property rights perform part of that function: where the upstream firm's investments are valuable, specific, and appropriable through patents, hold-up is attenuated and non-integration is efficient, so firm boundaries should also depend on the strength and scope of the supplier's IP — an appropriability condition the bare GHM trade-off omits.

Evidence. The paper develops the argument conceptually and confronts it with evidence from technology-intensive supply relationships in which independent, IP-rich suppliers serve large downstream firms. note: industry case details and any specific figures are not quoted here.

Findings. Property rights held by the supplier — not only asset ownership by the buyer — determine whether specialized supply remains outside the firm; integration is not the unique remedy for upstream hold-up when IP protection is strong.

Position. A law-and-economics refinement of 3.1/3.3 that anticipates 5.7/5.8's emphasis on contractible IP and control in alliances, connects to 5.13's treatment of intra-firm rights as commitments, and leads toward the technology-sharing/openness themes of Stream 6.

5.11 · Acemoglu, Aghion, Griffith & Zilibotti (2010) — Vertical Integration and Technology: Theory and Evidence

Pitch. The first cross-industry test of PRT's signature prediction — that the technology intensity of producers and suppliers push vertical integration in opposite directions — derived from a GHM-style model and estimated on the whole population of U.K. manufacturing plants. Where TCE predicts one-way effects of specificity/holdup exposure, PRT predicts opposing investment-incentive effects that strengthen with the supplier's share of costs.

Question. Do producer and supplier technology intensities have opposite effects on the likelihood of (backward) vertical integration, amplified by the share of costs?

Argument. In the model, backward integration raises the producer's outside option and bargaining share and depresses the supplier's, so it encourages producer investment (p) and discourages supplier investment (s). Ownership is chosen to maximize surplus: integration when p/s is high and when the supplier's input share ϕ of producer costs is large; supplier outside options θ push toward non-integration. Distinctive predictions: opposite signs on p and s, larger effects at high ϕ, and more producer-relative-to-supplier firms discouraging integration.

Evidence. U.K. ARD plant data, 1996–2001, 46,392 firms with ≥20 employees, collapsed to a cross-section of 2,973,008 firm–industry-pair observations across 3,840 producer–supplier industry pairs (1995 Input–Output Table, 77 industries). Dependent variable: whether the firm owns a plant in the supplying industry. R&D/value-added (1994–95) intensity of producing and supplying industries, plus share of costs, enter a linear probability model. Producer R&D intensity is positive (e.g., 0.038–0.044, s.e. ≈ 0.005–0.006) and supplier R&D intensity negative (−0.007 to −0.013), with large opposite-signed interactions with share of costs (γP = 1.112, s.e. 0.402; γS = −0.909, s.e. 0.353; Table 2). At the mean share of costs the effects are tiny (a one-s.d. supplier R&D rise cuts integration ~0.1 percent), but at the 90th percentile of share of costs they become economically large (~2 percent for supplier R&D, 1.4 percent for producer R&D; p. 1010). Results survive firm fixed effects on multiplant firms (Table 3), probit, an investment-intensity proxy (Table 6), size subsamples, and the continuous integration measure; IV using U.S. investment intensity gives larger same-signed estimates for the main effects (producer 0.492, supplier −0.158; Table 4). More firms in the supplying industry raise integration and more producing firms lower it (Table 7). The paper stresses these are correlations, not necessarily causal effects (p. 992; p. 1015).

Findings. Backward integration is more likely where downstream industries are R&D-intensive and less likely where upstream industries are, and both margins strengthen when the supplier's input is a large cost share — precisely PRT's opposite-incentives prediction, which TCE (one-sided holdup-avoidance) cannot rationalize (pp. 1022–23).

Position. Extends 3.1/3.3 to a cross-industry, cross-firm test, the strongest direct confirmation in this stream; discussed by 5.12 as the main make-buy evidence that favors PRT where it disagrees with TCE.

[grounding: full-text — JEEA 8(5):989–1033; model pp. 995–1001; data pp. 1002–06; main results pp. 1007–10; robustness pp. 1017–22; conclusion pp. 1022–23]

5.12 · Lafontaine & Slade (2007) — Vertical Integration and Firm Boundaries: The Evidence

Pitch. The definitive survey of the empirical record on firm boundaries, organizing incidence and consequences evidence around moral-hazard, transaction-cost, property-rights, and market-power models. Its verdict shapes the whole stream: TCE predictions are overwhelmingly supported in make-or-buy data, direct PRT tests are scarce, and where the two disagree the weight of evidence tilts toward PRT.

Question. What types of transactions belong inside the firm, and what are the consequences of vertical integration — evaluated across all the leading theories?

Argument. For forward integration (retailing/franchising) the organizing model is moral hazard: integration should fall with the productivity of the agent's (downstream) effort and rise with the principal's (upstream/brand) effort, and respond to monitoring, outlet size, spillovers, and multitasking. For backward integration the relevant logic is TCE (specificity, complexity, uncertainty → integration) versus PRT (ownership allocates residual control; the party whose marginal investment matters more should own; effects on the two parties' investments are opposite-signed).

Evidence. Evidence tables by factor: risk (Table 1), downstream effort (Table 2: significant effects all negative), upstream effort (Table 3: all positive/significant), outlet size (Table 4: mostly +), monitoring (Table 5), non-repeat business (Table 6); physical, human-capital, dedicated, site, temporal, and general specificity (Tables 7–12), complexity (Table 13, including Acemoglu et al.'s upstream R&D negative effect), uncertainty (Table 14) — all support TCE wherever effects are significant. Two striking regularities: downstream risk is robustly associated with less integration (contradicting the insurance-based agency prediction), and PRT reinterpretation explains it — volatility raises the value of the agent's flexible human-capital investment; supplier–manufacturer evidence where TC and PR predictions diverge is "not very supportive" of PRT, but manufacturer–retailer/franchisor–franchisee evidence supports it (pp. 658–60, 678–79). Consequences: event studies and outcome regressions find integration mostly efficiency-improving and rarely anticompetitive (Tables 15–16), while imposed divorcement raises prices and lowers service (Table 17); the authors conclude profit-maximizing vertical-integration decisions are efficient for firms and consumers (p. 680).

Findings. Asset specificity, complexity, and uncertainty robustly predict integration; the incentive-based (MH/PRT) family explains the forward-integration and within-firm margins, with PRT providing the natural account of the risk anomaly; market-power motives find little empirical support — a record on which the burden of proof in vertical-merger policy should rest with regulators.

Position. The stream's organizing survey: it synthesizes 5.1–5.6, 5.11 and the financing/alliance work (5.7–5.9), quotes Whinston (3.10) on TCE/PRT differences, and its call for direct PRT tests is answered by 5.13.

[grounding: full-text — JEL 45(3):629–85; forward-integration evidence pp. 632–48; backward/TCE/PRT evidence pp. 648–60; consequences pp. 662–77; conclusions pp. 677–80]

5.13 · Magelssen (2020) — Allocation of Property Rights and Technological Innovation within Firms

Pitch. Moves PRT inside the multinational: subsidiaries that hold formal ownership rights to strategic assets innovate more and respond more to R&D opportunity, because tax law makes intra-firm property rights credible commitments. This answers the claim that ownership rights within firms cannot be credible, and shows a real cost of tax-driven rights allocation.

Question. Do ownership rights to strategic assets allocated to subsidiaries within multinational firms affect those subsidiaries' technological innovation?

Argument. Strategic-asset ownership bundles control rights (the subsidiary sets research direction without approval) with income rights (it bears the profit/loss of the asset). Transfer-pricing rules make this credible: governments enforce subsidiary ownership and impose large adjustment costs on internal transfers (OECD TPG, IRS §1.482), so the parent cannot cheaply override — ownership rights are a commitment device beyond delegated authority. Owners should therefore produce more innovations and respond more elastically to innovation opportunity, especially where local technological advantage is high.

Evidence. New confidential subsidiary-year data compiled from MNE transfer-pricing reports, 1997–2011, matched to USPTO patents by inventor location: 78 MNEs, ~952 R&D subsidiaries (3,753 subsidiary-year observations after merging controls). Ownership is coded from the reports (13.3 percent of R&D subsidiaries hold strategic-asset rights). Instruments: host-country tax-haven status (first-stage K–P F = 22.52; tax haven raises ownership probability ~29 percentage points). Instrumented owners produce about one more patent per year and receive about 10 more forward citations per year than non-owners after controls (TSLS second stage; Table 4). A triple-difference design exploiting ≥0.01-point host-country R&D tax-subsidy increases (OECD-flagged shocks) shows owners increase patent counts 25.5 percent and citation-weighted counts 44.3 percent more than non-owners post-shock; in high-revealed-technological-advantage countries the gaps are 34.0 percent and 64.0 percent, while low-RTA gaps are insignificant (Table 5). Pre-trends are flat; results survive matched samples, alternative thresholds, negative binomial and control-function specifications; no differential change in R&D spending, indicating project selection rather than budgets drive the result; owners also expand exploratory innovation more (new domains +32.6 percent post-shock in high-RTA countries).

Findings. Within the firm, allocating ownership rights to the subsidiary that does the R&D raises the quantity, quality, and opportunity-responsiveness of its innovation; parking rights in income-shielding locations for tax purposes suppresses innovation in technologically advantaged subsidiaries.

Position. Applies 3.1/3.3 property rights to intra-firm (MNE) boundaries — where Baker, Gibbons & Murphy (informal authority) said credible ownership cannot exist — and links 5.7/5.8's bargaining-power allocation and 5.10's rights-as-substitutes thesis to corporate strategy and tax avoidance.

[grounding: full-text — SMJ 2020 (manuscript); theory and hypotheses §§Theoretical Background–Hypotheses; data §§Methods; results Tables 4–5 and §Results]

Stream 5 one-paragraph synthesis

The empirical record confirms PRT's core allocation logic and disciplines its scope. The stream turns on three moments: (1) the specificity-driven make-or-buy and coal-contract results (5.1–5.3) established that hold-up concerns shape integration and contract form, but — as Whinston and Lafontaine–Slade (5.12) stress — could not separate TCE from PRT; (2) technology-shock and cross-industry designs isolated PRT's distinctive prediction of opposite-signed investment effects and confirmed it — producer-side R&D intensity pushes integration while supplier-side intensity pushes away from it (5.11), and making driving contractible shifted truck ownership to carriers (5.5); (3) alliance, venture-capital, and intra-firm studies (5.7–5.9, 5.13) showed control rights track financial health, bargaining power, and state-contingent performance, and that legally enforced property rights work as commitments inside firms. What the record rejects: simple insurance-driven agency accounts (the robust negative risk–integration relationship), the TCE shortcut equating specificity with integration, and the claim that intra-firm ownership rights are non-credible. What it leaves open, per 5.12: direct tests of PRT are still scarce, and the evidence favors PRT most where it and TCE genuinely diverge.

6.1 · Benkler (2002) — Coase's Penguin, or, Linux and The Nature of the Firm

Pitch. Linux and thousands of volunteer collaborations do not fit the market-or-hierarchy dichotomy; Benkler generalizes them into "commons-based peer production," a third mode of organizing information production, and explains it inside the Coasean comparative-cost template rather than against it.

Question. Why would large-scale, productive collaboration emerge and persist without markets (price signals) or firms (managerial command), and when does it beat both?

Argument. All organization is an information problem: agents must decide what to do. Markets solve it by pricing actions; firms by weighting signals by authority; both must specify the object of the signal, which causes two inefficiencies — information loss and the "stickiness" of property/contract-based clusters. Peer production lets individuals self-identify for tasks, yielding information gains (the agent best knows its own talent/fit, filtered by peer review) and allocation gains (unbounded access of agents to resources/projects, with increasing returns to pool size). He re-derives Demsetz-style tables: peering wins when the cost of organizing on a peered basis is lowest (Tables 1–2). Sustainability requires solving motivation (diverse rewards: monetary M with satiation, hedonic H, social-psychological SP; indirect appropriation) and the "commons problem" of integration, handled by modularity, granularity, low-cost integration, and mechanisms ranging from hierarchy (Linux kernel), peer review (Slashdot), norms (Wikipedia), to redundant aggregation (NASA Clickworkers).

Evidence. Worked argument, not formal propositions: comparative-cost tables (pp. 402–403); stylized motivation taxonomy; industry evidence — e.g., two-thirds of software-industry revenues (~$109bn of ~$173bn, 1997 Census) are service-based, enabling indirect appropriation (p. 425); case vignettes (Linux, Apache, Wikipedia, Slashdot, Clickworkers, IBM's $1bn open-source commitment, p. 425–26).

Findings. Peer production has systematic advantages in matching highly variable human capital to information inputs; strong IP rights shrink the input pool it draws on, so regulation and firm strategy should shift toward provisioning and integration rather than ownership of the output.

Position. Directly extends 1.5 (Coase) and 1.10 (Demsetz): it takes their comparative-institutional-cost logic and adds a third column. It borrows its motivation catalog from 6.2 and, in reverse, gives 6.5–6.6 their hypothesis space about opening access. Its claim that firms can still profit by integrating peer-produced modules (IBM, Red Hat) prefigures the ecosystem-hub roles of 6.4 and 6.8.

6.2 · Lerner & Tirole (2002) — Some Simple Economics of Open Source

Pitch. The apparently altruistic behavior of open-source programmers is largely standard labor economics: programmers contribute because open projects are unusually good devices for signaling talent through "career concerns" and ego gratification.

Question. Why do thousands of top programmers freely provide a public good, and why do firms release code or "live symbiotically" with projects they cannot own?

Argument. A programmer contributes if net benefit (current minus opportunity cost, plus delayed minus delayed cost) is positive. Current benefits include use value (bug-fixing, customization) and "cool" work; the delayed component — career concerns (future offers, venture-capital access) plus ego gratification — is grouped as the signaling incentive, which is stronger the more visible the performance, the higher the effort-performance impact, and the more informative performance is about talent. Open source beats proprietary settings on all three: outsiders can inspect each contribution ("better performance measurement"), programmers take full initiative without supervisors, and shared code raises labor-market fluidity. Governance matters: leaders lack formal authority but hold "real authority" (à la 3.8); trusted leadership, transparency, and modular tasks prevent forking. Firms open code when profits in a complementary segment offset lost primary-segment profit — e.g., laggards facing dominant rivals; Netscape's Mozilla shows the risks of weak modularity and non-credible commitment.

Evidence. Mini-cases (Apache, Linux, Perl, Sendmail) with market data: Apache 59.7% vs Microsoft IIS 20.2% of ~24 million domains (Nov. 2000); Sendmail handling ~75% of Internet e-mail in 2000; skewed contribution: >75% of ~13,000 contributors made one contribution, top decile = 72% of code in 25 million lines across 3,149 projects; Apache's top 15 developers made 83–91% of changes.

Findings. Most features of open source are explicable with off-the-shelf economics; the puzzles that remain concern licensing choice, project governance, and whether the model transfers to non-software industries.

Position. Provides the micro-motivation engine that 6.1's peer-production theory imports; formalizes the signaling logic of career concerns (Holmström 1999) and uses 3.8's formal-vs-real-authority distinction to explain leadership without ownership. 6.3 is its survey sequel; 6.5 and 6.6 test the "opening" side for commercial platforms.

[grounding: full-text — JIE 50(2): 197–234; model §IV pp. 212–223, firm strategies §V pp. 223–227, open questions §VI pp. 228–231]

6.3 · Lerner & Tirole (2005) — The Economics of Technology Sharing: Open Source and Beyond

Pitch. Open source is not an anomaly to be explained away but one instance of a general economic problem — when and how to share technology — whose lessons extend to patent pools, standard-setting, and the design of licenses.

Question. What motivates contributors and firms, how does the legal/contractual frame (license choice) shape outcomes, and when does "opening" beat proprietary production?

Argument. The survey consolidates the 6.2 framework — signaling incentives (visibility, impact, informativeness), immediate use value, the "alumni effect," and leadership with real authority — and adds the licensing dimension as the key strategic variable. Licenses allocate the residual rights over contributed code: permissive licenses (BSD) are predicted where contributor appeal is strong; restrictive ones (GPL) where it is fragile (end-user apps, corporate sponsors feared as hijackers). Firms interact through complementary services ("living symbiotically"), code release, or certification intermediaries (Collab.Net à la Dessein's delegation model); welfare analysis (Johnson, Casadesus-Masanell–Ghemawat, Schmidt–Schnitzer, Saint-Paul) shows ambiguous-to-negative net effects of subsidizing open source because free provision can crowd out commercial innovation incentives; software patents threaten hold-up and the GPL's §7 bars patent-encumbered code.

Evidence. Empirical license analysis of nearly 40,000 SourceForge projects: restrictive licenses are more common for end-user/games apps and non-English projects, less common for developer tools, commercial environments, and proprietary OSes; less restrictive licenses attract more contributors (pp. 108). Apache wage evidence: high rank raises wages 14–29% (Hann et al. 2004).

Findings. Open source "works" through standard career-concern and complementarity mechanisms; its distinctive economics lie in the deliberate contractual allocation of rights not to exclude, which solves commitment and hold-up problems that proprietary ownership cannot.

Position. The same authors' sequel to 6.2, generalizing "technology sharing" beyond software: it reframes the property-rights question (who holds which rights over code and derivatives) as a license design problem — the direct bridge from 6.1–6.2's commons to 6.7's control-rights allocation over platform transactions and to 6.8's modular ecosystems.

[grounding: full-text — JEP 19(2): 99–120; license model and evidence pp. 107–110; quality, policy, patents pp. 109–115; broader agenda pp. 115–118]

6.4 · Iansiti & Levien (2004) — Strategy as Ecology

Pitch. A firm's performance depends on the health of the business ecosystem around it, so strategy should shift from winning within an industry to choosing a role — keystone, dominator, or niche player — that sustains the whole network.

Question. How should a firm strategize when value creation is distributed across a loosely coupled network of interdependent organizations rather than contained within industry and firm boundaries?

Argument. Business ecosystems are "characterized by a large number of loosely interconnected participants who depend on each other for their mutual effectiveness and survival." Strategy options map onto biological roles: keystones (e.g., platform owners) enhance ecosystem productivity, robustness, and niche creation by providing stable, shared interfaces and standards — they capture a modest share of value while increasing total value; dominators integrate and control, extracting value at the cost of ecosystem health; landlords occupy narrow niches; niche players specialize. Ecosystem health is assessed by productivity, robustness, and the creation of new niches, not by the firm's own market share.

Evidence. Canonical illustrative cases: Microsoft (keystone via platform standards, particularly pre-smartphone Windows ecosystem), Wal-Mart (keystone in retail supply network), and contrast cases of dominance strategies that degraded their networks. The evidence is conceptual and case-based rather than econometric.

Findings. The keystone strategy is the central prescription: improve the ecosystem's health and the ecosystem will improve yours; a healthy network disciplines value capture and sustains the platform's centrality.

Position. The first management framing of "ecosystem" as an object of strategy (with Moore 1993), it seeds the affiliation/keystone view that 6.8 formalizes and that 6.9 criticizes as under-specified; its keystone = stable-interface provider prefigures 6.5's "granting access" empirics and 6.7's intermediary control problem.

6.5 · Boudreau (2010) — Open Platform Strategies and Innovation: Granting Access vs. Devolving Control

Pitch. "Opening" a platform is two different moves with two different mechanisms: granting access to complementors accelerates innovation dramatically, while the platform owner giving up control of the platform itself does little — empirically separating the rights questions bundled together in most openness talk.

Question. Do opening the platform (ceding control over the bottleneck asset) and opening the market for complements (licensing access) affect innovation through distinct channels and with distinct magnitudes?

Argument. Platforms are bottlenecks whose owners hold "bouncer's rights" to exclude. Two trade-offs frame openness: adoption vs. appropriability (entry and competition vs. lock-in relief) and diversity vs. control (external knowledge vs. coherence). Granting access works through diversity — heterogeneous complementors experiment, and intensified competition among them can stimulate or depress effort — whereas devolving platform control works by committing the owner not to expropriate complementors' quasi-rents (hold-up relief), but incremental variation in control may not credibly change that commitment.

Evidence. Hand-collected panel of 21 handheld-computing systems, 1990–2004, 1,706 system-months, seven "switchers"; Poisson count models of new device releases with system fixed effects and time controls. Preferred model: licensing access peaked at intermediate liberality (COMPLEMENT_ACCESS = 2) with an incidence rate ratio ≈ 4.6 (up to fivefold acceleration); sharing reference designs added ~16%; the aggregate open-platform measures implied only ~20–22% faster development, generally insignificant, though significant in post-1996 and leading-system subsamples. Inverted-U in access is confirmed at p ≤ 1% jointly.

Findings. Who gets access to build complements is the powerful lever; who holds residual control of the core platform is a weaker one — at least for incremental "tweaks" of openness in mature systems — challenging the literature's emphasis on ceding platform control.

Position. Gives the platform question empirical teeth the PRT line lacks: it separates access rights from residual control rights (the 3.1 distinction) and shows they operate differently in systems with property-holding hubs. It answers 6.2–6.3's firm-side strategies and is complemented by 6.6 (marginal entry) and 6.7 (who should hold transaction control).

6.6 · Boudreau (2012) — Let a Thousand Flowers Bloom?

Pitch. Bringing thousands of app developers onto a platform indeed multiplies software variety — but it also crowds out individual innovation incentives on average, because later, marginal entrants make less compelling software and intensify competition among similar titles.

Question. Does adding large numbers of independent application producers raise or lower innovation, and through which mechanisms?

Argument. App innovation combines an almost unbounded product space, cumulative recombination, extreme uncertainty with skewed ("killer app") outcomes, and low-cost, platform-facilitated entry. Four hypotheses follow: more producers raise variety (H1) because individual producers stay narrow and specialized (H2); added producers in different genres raise investment incentives through network effects and market pull (H3); added producers in the same genre crowd out incentives through competition (H4). Variety and incentive effects must be separated.

Evidence. Handango point-of-sale data, Nov. 1999–Dec. 2004: 28,960 titles by 5,973 independent producers across eight handheld platforms; IV identification uses U.S. software labor-market wage/employment shocks (a rise of 1,000 software jobs is associated with 0.65 fewer producers per platform) to purge endogeneity. Variety rises linearly with producers — ~1.50 varieties per producer (IV, preferred) — while producer scope stays narrow (mean 1.7 genres) and insensitive to platform size. Time-to-new-version regressions: producers in other genres speed development (−0.09 coefficient), same-genre producers slow it (+1.24); the aggregate net effect of added producers is a slowdown (+0.04). Later-entering cohorts' titles sell far less, even at the 99th percentile; no late entrant on the top three platforms ever reached the top-100 sellers list.

Findings. Adding producers qualitatively transforms innovation: variety becomes a population-level product of diversity and specialization rather than heroic individual effort, while average per-producer incentives fall — the "thousand flowers" bloom, but at the margin they are weeds.

Position. The empirical counterpoint to the open-access optimism of 6.1–6.3 and to 6.5's access result: it locates the crowding margin of granting access and shows the value of managing the composition of complementors — the regulatory/control question that 6.7 and 6.8 theorize, and that platform-era AI app markets (6.15) inherit.

6.7 · Hagiu & Wright (2015) — Marketplace or Reseller?

Pitch. Whether a platform buys and resells (reseller mode) or lets suppliers sell directly to customers for a fee (marketplace mode) is a decision about who should hold control rights over price and the customer relationship — allocated to whichever side has better information about the relevant demand.

Question. When should an intermediary act as a reseller (taking title, setting prices) rather than a marketplace (letting independent merchants set prices), and why do firms like Amazon mix or switch modes?

Argument. Canonical knowledge: the two modes allocate distinct control rights. A reseller controls pricing and the direct customer relationship but must know demand well enough to price and hold inventory; a marketplace cedes pricing to suppliers, who keep residual claims and therefore invest in learning their own demand — but the intermediary forgoes control over price, positioning, and customer lock-in. The efficient mode gives the pricing/customer-interface rights to the party with the comparative advantage in acquiring the relevant demand information: merchants know their product and niche (→ marketplace), the intermediary knows aggregate customer demand and can integrate fulfillment (→ reseller). Mixing modes is a deliberate, mode-by-mode allocation rather than a failure of commitment. The framework extends the theory of the firm's make-vs-buy logic to the "two-sided" layer: the firm's boundary question becomes which transaction rights the platform itself should hold.

Evidence. Canonical business cases: Amazon's long-run shift from reseller toward third-party marketplace (marketplace share of units rising from near zero to a majority over the 2000s–2010s); Amazon's acquisition of Zappos, retained and run as a reseller under separate brand; contrast with eBay's pure marketplace model. The empirical discussion is case-based rather than econometric.

Findings. Marketplace vs. reseller is not an ideology but a contingent allocation of control rights — when demand knowledge shifts (e.g., long-tail or niche products, sellers who know their customers better), the mode should shift accordingly.

Position. Translates 3.1's GHM logic — control rights go to the party whose investments matter most — from physical asset ownership to the allocation of control over platform transactions and customer interface; it supplies the transaction-level "who holds which rights" analysis that 6.5 studies at system level and that 6.8 builds on via "affiliation."

[grounding: knowledge + abstract — Mgmt Sci 61(1): 184–203; no full text in corpus]

6.8 · Jacobides, Cennamo & Gawer (2018) — Towards a Theory of Ecosystems

Pitch. Ecosystems are not alliances, supply chains, or markets: they are sets of actors bound by nongeneric (unique or supermodular) complementarities, coordinated through modularity and standardized roles rather than hierarchical fiat — a governance form that sits between market and hierarchy without being reducible to dyadic contracts.

Question. What are ecosystems, when and why do they emerge instead of markets or integrated hierarchies, and what makes them strategically distinct?

Argument. Modularity (thin crossing points, codified interfaces) is necessary for ecosystem emergence — it lets autonomous firms coordinate without fiat — but not sufficient, since modularity alone pushes toward markets. Ecosystems also require coordination needs that markets cannot handle: multilateral, nongeneric complementarities. Unique complementarity (A doesn't function without B; co-specialization, cf. Hart–Moore's pipeline ends) and supermodular (Edgeworth) complementarity ("more A makes B more valuable," Milgrom–Roberts) may each be uni- or bidirectional and live in production or consumption. Definition: "a set of actors with varying degrees of multilateral, nongeneric complementarities that are not fully hierarchically controlled." Interdependencies are standardized by role (hub, supplier, complementor), not dyad, so members make nonfungible investments but retain residual control of their own assets — distinguishing ecosystems from supply chains where the hub unilaterally sets terms (Toyota vs. Apple). Complementarity type predicts behavior: supermodularity raises collaboration; low fungibility aids alignment of incumbents but deters recruitment.

Evidence. Theory paper: deductive typology with illustrative cases — OS/app ecosystems (unique + supermodular complementarity combined), Nespresso's deliberate specification of generic coffee complementarity, Android/iOS app roles, Amazon's marketplace-plus-ecosystem, and the Symbian (67% smartphone OS share) losing to Android as a governance/co-specialization failure.

Findings. Ecosystem governance is a distinct mode of coordinating interdependent, autonomous firms — role-based rules substitute for both market prices and hierarchical authority — and the type/direction of complementarity plus investment fungibility determines ecosystem value creation, capture, and resilience.

Position. Gives the ecosystem construct a positive theory grounded in the TCE/PRT vocabulary (2.9; 3.3's unique complementarity; Teece's co-specialization) while breaking with dyadic analysis: the governance unit is the standardized role, not the bilateral contract — precisely the layer 3.1–3.3 do not model. It systematizes 6.4's keystone intuition and enters a definitional exchange with 6.9.

[grounding: full-text — SMJ 39: 2255–2276; typology §2 pp. 2256–2259, modularity & complementarity §3.1–3.2 pp. 2260–2264, definition §3.4 pp. 2264–2267, research agenda §4 pp. 2267–2271]

6.9 · Adner (2017) — Ecosystem as Structure

Pitch. An ecosystem is not a community of affiliated actors but the alignment structure of the multilateral set of partners whose interaction a focal value proposition requires — so ecosystem strategy is fundamentally about alignment, not access or centrality.

Question. What does the ecosystem construct add to strategy, and how should it be distinguished from platforms, multisided markets, networks, supply chains, and business models?

Argument. Two views are contrasted: ecosystem-as-affiliation (actors defined by ties to a focal actor/platform; hub-and-spoke; measures of centrality) and ecosystem-as-structure (activity-centric; starts from a value proposition). Definition: "the alignment structure of the multilateral set of partners that need to interact in order for a focal value proposition to materialize." Four elements — activities, actors, positions, links — form a "value blueprint." The construct earns its keep only when interdependence is multilateral and non-decomposable into bilateral relations, and when partners must be realigned; otherwise the ecosystem is latent and standard strategy suffices. Ecosystem strategy = how a focal firm approaches alignment of partners and secures its role (leader/follower) in a competitive ecosystem; it must manage co-innovation risk, adoption-chain risk, and divergent structural/role expectations, including over actors (e.g., garages) with no direct link to the innovator.

Evidence. Worked case: Michelin's PAX run-flat tire, whose value proposition (drive 125 miles at up to 55 mph after a puncture) required reconfiguring positions (wheel-hub integration shifts from automakers to tire maker) and activating garages as adopters of new repair equipment — a structure no dyadic or platform analysis captures; plus a systematic contrast table of a dozen interdependence constructs (platforms, MSPs, networks, business models, value chains, industry architecture, value nets, open innovation) and where each falls short.

Findings. Ecosystem logic is neither necessary nor sufficient for all strategy, but it is increasingly critical wherever value creation requires realigning multilateral partners; the boundary of the ecosystem is set by the value proposition, not by the firm, sector, or region.

Position. Disciplines the ecosystem concept against alternative constructs (hence against 6.4 and the platform literature it cites, incl. 6.7), and is the position 6.8 defines itself against — the two are the field's canonical affiliation-vs-structure pairing. For the PRT line it relocates the governance problem: alignment of partners who retain independent control rights, rather than ownership of assets.

6.10 · Teece (2018) — Business Models and Dynamic Capabilities

Pitch. A business model is the architecture of value creation, delivery, and capture; designing it well — and re-designing it as technology and markets shift — is itself a dynamic capability, so "profiting from innovation" requires coupling business-model design with sensing, seizing, and transforming.

Question. How do business models relate to dynamic capabilities, and what determines whether an innovator captures value from an innovation in digital, platform-rich settings?

Argument. Canonical knowledge: building on Teece's profiting-from-innovation framework (complementary assets, appropriability regimes, dominant design) and the 2007 dynamic-capabilities framework (sensing, seizing, transforming), the paper argues the business model is the "design or architecture of the value creation, delivery, and capture mechanisms" of the enterprise. It must be aligned with customer needs, the technology, and the firm's asset base; for complex, co-specialized innovations the model must often be invented, not borrowed. Dynamic capabilities govern business-model selection and orchestration of internal and external (ecosystem) assets; the framework explains why technically strong incumbents fail to capture value when their business model or required complementary ecosystem is mismatched, and why open-innovation and platform contexts demand continuous model adaptation. Business-model choice is analytically prior to, and interacts with, firm-boundary choice.

Evidence. Conceptual argument with illustrative cases drawn from the PPI/dynamic-capabilities tradition (technology licensing vs. integration; platform/ecosystem business models such as Apple's coupling of hardware, services, and third-party developers; cautionary tales of innovators ceding value to owners of co-specialized assets). Not an empirical study.

Findings. Value capture depends less on owning the core technology than on orchestrating co-specialized assets through an appropriate business model; business-model design is a microfoundation of dynamic capability and should be a first-class unit of strategy analysis.

Position. Supplies the capability-and-appropriation logic that complements PRT's ownership logic: where 3.1–3.3 ask who owns the asset, Teece asks who designs and orchestrates the value architecture around it — the capability twin of 6.8's ecosystem theory and a bridge to 6.12–6.15, where AI shifts which assets are co-specialized and who can orchestrate them.

[grounding: knowledge + abstract — Long Range Planning 51(1): 40–49; no full text in corpus]

6.11 · Goldfarb & Tucker (2019) — Digital Economics

Pitch. Digitization is the representation of information in bits, and its economic effects run through five cost reductions — search, replication, transportation, tracking, and verification — each activating a distinct, established body of models rather than requiring new theory.

Question. What is different when information is bits rather than atoms, and how do the resulting cost changes reshape markets, firms, and policy?

Argument. The five cost categories organize the literature: lower search costs (price dispersion, variety, matching, platforms, and the organization of the firm); zero-cost replication (non-rival digital goods: bundling, open source/Wikipedia as deliberate non-exclusion, copyright); lower transportation costs (does distance still matter; regulatory geography); lower tracking costs (price discrimination, auctions, privacy); lower verification costs (reputation systems, trust, discrimination). On firm organization the effects are theoretically two-sided: better information flow can centralize (headquarters monitoring) or decentralize (front-line access), and the empirical resolution depends on which feature of the technology dominates — as in Baker & Hubbard's trucking finding that monitoring-enhancing onboard computing pushed firms toward owning trucks while location-enhancing computing pushed them toward hiring owner-operators.

Evidence. Survey of the empirical literature: e.g., Bloom et al. (2014) on IT as centralizing and communication technology as decentralizing; Baker & Hubbard (2003, 2004) on make-vs-buy in trucking; online labor markets benefiting distant workers (Agrawal et al. 2016); open-source evidence consistent with 6.2's career-concern hypothesis; consumer surplus estimates (Facebook ≈ $750/user/yr in one incentive-compatible study; Google ≈ $16,000/user/yr for search); productivity findings conditioned on organizational capital ("Americans do I.T. better," Bloom et al. 2012).

Findings. Digital economics is not a new theory but a re-weighting: standard models with dramatically lower costs generate rich, sometimes counterintuitive predictions, and the boundary-of-the-firm question becomes empirical and technology-specific.

Position. The field-level map beneath this stream: it frames the classical PRT/Coasean questions (5.4–5.5's make-vs-buy; centralization vs. decentralization) as consequences of specific cost reductions, and it sets the agenda 6.12–6.15 attack directly — when the marginal cost of search, verification, and coordination with machines collapses, who holds which rights over data, models, and transaction layers?

6.12 · Warin (2025) — From Coase to AI Agents: The Emergence of a New Type of Firm

Pitch. AI agents do not abolish the firm; they re-open the Coasean question, because uncontrolled, employee-created agents raise organizational entropy and quietly shift residual control of the firm's processes to external platforms — a new form of digital gatekeeping.

Question. Will empowering every employee with personal AI agents raise productivity, or undermine the very rationale of the firm by fragmenting coordination and increasing dependence on platform owners?

Argument. The author reads Coase–Williamson–North as saying firms exist to economize on transaction costs and impose integration on complexity. Deploying AI agents inside the firm inverts this: individual agent-creation is "artisanship" — each agent is idiosyncratic, locally optimized, and poorly transferable, so process atomization creates digital "cruft" and duplicated, conflicting systems across departments (the "illusion of efficiency"). Agents also shift coordination outward: as the platform absorbs agent orchestration, integration and entropy management migrate to the platform provider, which becomes a new gatekeeper — reducing internal transaction costs at the micro level while raising external transaction costs and eroding long-term adaptability. The organization risks being "dismembered by the platform" into a "digital feudalism."

Evidence. Conceptual essay (CMR "Insight," 7 pp.): applies the TCE vocabulary to agent-based organizing; illustrative failure scenarios (sales vs. customer-service agent systems generating conflicting customer information); recommends internal agent platforms, hybrid centralization-with-autonomy models, and agent lifecycle auditing as remedies. No formal model or data.

Findings. AI-adoption choices are strategic and business-model choices, not technology choices: firms that blindly embrace agent-driven automation may sacrifice organizational coherence, and the design task is to capture automation's gains while preserving the integrative function that justifies the firm.

Position. The first corpus entry that asks the PRT question about AI-era rights: who holds residual control over the firm's agents, data, and processes — the firm or the platform that hosts them? It warns of exactly the "thick waist"/re-centralization outcome that 6.13 models formally, and extends 6.11's cost-reduction agenda by adding "organizational entropy" as the countervailing cost of cheap agent-level coordination.

[grounding: full-text — CMR Insight "From Coase to AI Agents: Why the Economics of the Firm Still Matters in the Age of Automation," Apr. 1, 2025 (manifest title "…The Emergence of a New Type of Firm" is the Zotero record's variant); legacy pp. 1–2, agent risks §"The Corporate Conundrum" pp. 3–6, gatekeepers & remedies pp. 6–8]

6.13 · Klein & Wieczorek (2026) — The Headless Firm: How AI Reshapes Enterprise Boundaries

Pitch. Agentic AI changes the scaling law of coordination: when integration is protocol-mediated and verified by outcome rather than negotiated pair by pair, coordination cost falls from a topology-dominated O(n²) regime to a throughput-dominated O(n) regime — selecting for a "headless firm" shaped like an hourglass and predicting a conditional Great Unbundling.

Question. Can AI agents change whether coordination costs scale with interaction topology or with task throughput, and what organizational form — and firm-size distribution — does the resulting regime select?

Argument. Coordination cost C = integration (maintaining interfaces/edges) + verification (checking outputs across the providers a task touches) + governance (linear in throughput). In modular systems (SOA/microservices) integration grows with interaction edges — superlinear, combinatorial. Agentic systems instead compile intent through a standardized protocol "waist" and evaluate outcomes, collapsing maintained edges to O(n) while verification costs scale with tasks (v(k) = O(k^γ), γ < 1). This yields the hourglass equilibrium: a personalized generative-UI intent layer on top, a thin standardized protocol-and-policy waist (e.g., MCP) in the middle, and a competitive market of micro-specialized execution agents at the bottom. Stability is conditional: the hourglass persists iff per-task verification stays sublinear in workflow width; four re-centralization pathways threaten it — the "thick-waist" aggregator (platform envelopment), cognitive-load re-thickening, the Jevons paradox of inference (marginal cost of an agent is liability, E[r(a)·p(a)], not compute), and liability concentration in "trust boutiques."

Evidence. Formal coordination-cost model with two falsifiable predictions — (1) marginal cost of adding an execution provider is ~constant in a mature hourglass ecosystem; (2) ratio of total coordination cost to task throughput is stable as the ecosystem grows — plus hypotheses (domain-conditional unbundling; sublinear coordination) and a firm-size-distribution argument built on Zipf scaling (Axtell) and knowledge-decay (Jones). Not yet empirically tested; authors disclose founding interest in Mantix.

Findings. If the scaling shift holds, high-knowledge-velocity domains see probability mass move from large integrated incumbents toward thin protocol orchestrators and micro-specialized agents — firms still exist, but governance is compressed into standards, policy gates, and evaluation.

Position. Formalizes 6.12's gatekeeping worry as the "thick waist" re-centralization condition, and re-runs the entire Coasean/Williamson comparative-cost machine (1.5, 2.9's asset specificity) on agentic technology: the boundary question becomes who holds residual control of the protocol waist, the execution interfaces, and verification — the AI-era restatement of 3.1's ownership question.

6.14 · Kim (2026) — Data-Driven Technologies and Firm Boundaries: Insourcing without Reshoring

Pitch. Adopting AI, big-data analytics, and cloud computing pulls production back inside the firm — more in-house sourcing, less outsourcing — while leaving the domestic-vs-foreign mix unchanged: the ownership boundary moves without the geographic boundary moving.

Question. How do data-driven digital technologies reshape firms' sourcing strategies, separately along the ownership dimension (in-house vs. outsourced) and the location dimension (domestic vs. foreign)?

Argument. The mechanism emphasized is that firm-specific data accumulation and non-contractible coordination favor internalization: data generated in-house is co-specialized and hard to transfer through contracts, so technologies that make data central raise the returns to owning the adjacent production steps; conversely the same technologies do not obviously change comparative labor or location advantages, so the domestic–foreign composition is left intact.

Evidence. Abstract-level (paper not open-access; SSRN WP 6955501): panel data on 10,700 Korean manufacturing firms, 2017–2022, with detailed intermediate-input sourcing measures distinguishing in-house vs. outsourced and domestic vs. foreign sourcing; staggered technology adoption with dynamic treatment effects. Findings: adoption increases in-house sourcing and reduces outsourcing while leaving domestic–foreign composition largely unchanged; effects are stronger among in-house developers and in complex industries; output rises without employment growth, implying organizational rather than labor-substitution effects.

Findings. Data-driven technologies are an insourcing force — they internalize production by strengthening firm-specific data and non-contractible coordination needs — but not a reshoring force, decoupling the two dimensions of the make-or-buy decision that much outsourcing debate conflates.

Position. Updates the empirical PRT record (5.4–5.5's trucking result, 5.11's technology-and-integration result) for the AI era: data is the new co-specialized asset whose ownership tilts the boundary, directly extending 6.11's tracking/verification cost logic to a large-N manufacturing setting and supplying the "insourcing" half of the make/buy question that 6.13's unbundling thesis leaves open.

6.15 · Wan, Yang, Shi, Rong & Ansari (2026) — Scaling High and Wide: How Firms Leverage AI and Organizational Design

Pitch. ByteDance shows that AI plus adaptive organizational design can dissolve the scale–scope trade-off: because AI improves through self-learning and cross-fertilization across domains, diversification amplifies the value of the firm's AI resources instead of diluting them.

Question. Can digital firms overcome the long-standing trade-off between scale (hyperspecialization) and scope (diversification), and how?

Argument. The conventional resource-based logic — valuable resources enable diversification — is reversed: for AI-driven firms, diversification itself makes the resource more valuable, because models learn more and transfer better as they are applied across structurally related domains. The mechanism is contingent on access to structurally related data that permit learning transfer across domains, and on an adaptive organizational design that routes data and AI capabilities across business units. AI thus flips the scale–scope nexus from a constraint into a source of strategic advantage.

Evidence. Abstract-level (SMJ early view; full text paywalled on Wiley): longitudinal case study of ByteDance, tracing how its AI capabilities and organization evolved as the firm scaled rapidly (e.g., TikTok/抖音 and related products) and diversified across industries and markets; theorizing from the case rather than econometric identification.

Findings. Scale and scope cease to be substitutes when the marginal value of the firm's core resource rises with the breadth of its deployment; growth "high and wide" is sustained by data-mediated learning spillovers that the organizational design deliberately cultivates.

Position. Reframes the scope question of 3.17 and 6.8 in data terms: structurally related data function as a supermodular complement whose value grows across domains, so the firm's boundary and scope logic is now about who controls the data and learning loops spanning businesses. It complements 6.14 (insourcing) as the scope-side mirror, and connects 6.10's dynamic-capability orchestration to AI-era expansion.

[grounding: abstract — Strategic Management Journal, early view, DOI 10.1002/smj.70090 (published online 2026); Research/Managerial Summaries; full text paywalled]

Stream 6 one-paragraph synthesis

Stream 6 carries the PRT question — who holds which residual rights over productive assets — from Coase's firm through commons, platforms, and ecosystems, and re-states it for data, models, and agents. Its first turning point is the discovery that the market-hierarchy dichotomy fails: 6.1–6.3 show production organized by self-selection, signaling, and license-given access rights, so governance can rest on deliberately not owning (open source) yet still be governed. The second turning point is the split between granting access and ceding control: 6.4–6.9 demonstrate that in platform and ecosystem settings, ownership of the bottleneck and control of the transaction are separable levers, with roles, standards, and complementarities doing work that ownership once did — 6.5 and 6.6 quantify that access liberalness dominates control devolution, while 6.7 relocates GHM's control-rights logic to the price-and-customer-interface decision and 6.8–6.9 theorize multilateral alignment as the new governance object. The third turning point is the AI era (6.11–6.15): digitization lowered search, replication, transportation, tracking, and verification costs; machine agents then change the scaling law of coordination, so the open question becomes who holds residual control of data, models, protocols, and agent execution — whether firms disintegrate into headless orchestrators (6.13), internalize on data-driven grounds (6.14), or grow across domains on data spillovers (6.15) — returning the field, with new stakes, to Coase's original comparative-cost question.
