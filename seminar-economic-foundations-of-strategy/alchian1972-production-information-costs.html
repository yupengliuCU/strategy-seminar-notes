一句话总结

一句话总结

企业是解决团队生产之计量问题（metering problem）的一种契约装置：一位作为剩余索取者（residual claimant）的中心代理人监督各项协作投入，遏制联合产出掩盖各成员边际产品（marginal product）时出现的偷懒（shirking）。企业不是市场海洋中的一座权威孤岛——这正是本文要反对的图景。

Alchian and Demsetz（1972）开启了第三周主线（Week-3 arc）的古典阶段：本文定义了监督/计量问题与剩余索取者的解决方案，此后主线的每一站都以之为前提。Demsetz（1967）曾论证产权是为内部化外部性而出现；本文把这一逻辑带进企业内部，把团队生产中的偷懒视为一种由一束剩余权利加以内部化的外部性。现代产权理论（Grossman and Hart 1986；Hart and Moore 1990）、所有权能力研究议程（Foss、Klein 及其合作者），以及主线上的实证应用（如 Magelssen 2020），都继承了它的问题——谁拥有什么、为什么——即使它们用对资产的正式剩余控制权（residual control rights）取代了它的监督机制。

1. 背景与动机（Background and motivation）

Alchian and Demsetz 开篇即交代经济组织理论的任务：它必须做两件事——"解释在何种条件下，专业化与合作生产的好处更适合在企业这样的组织内部获得，还是在市场之中跨主体获得；并解释组织的结构"（p. 777）。这两个问题是有意绑在一起的。只告诉你企业何时胜过市场的理论，对企业的内部架构——谁雇佣谁、谁拥有什么、谁监督谁——保持沉默；而作者要推导的恰恰是这些事实。

文章的攻击目标，是当时主流的、Coase 式的企业图景——把企业看作一块权威（authority）的领地。针对这幅图景，论文抛出了它最著名的一段话（p. 777）：

It is common to see the firm characterized by the power to settle issues by fiat, by authority, or by disciplinary action superior to that available in the conventional market. This is delusion. The firm does not own all its inputs. It has no power of fiat, no authority, no disciplinary action any different in the slightest degree from ordinary market contracting between any two people. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 777

>

中文复述：人们常把企业刻画成靠命令（fiat）、权威或纪律处分来裁决问题的力量，且这些手段胜过普通市场。这是错觉。企业并不拥有它所有的投入；它在命令、权威、纪律处分上，与任意两个人之间的普通市场缔约没有任何细微差别。

"错觉（delusion）"一说靠类比来论证。雇主能"惩罚"雇员的唯一方式是停止未来的生意往来或提起诉讼——与对付杂货商（grocer）的手段完全相同。"叫雇员打这封信而不是归档那份文件，就像我让杂货商卖我这个牌子的金枪鱼而不是那个牌子的面包一样"（p. 777）。任何一方都没有继续下去的约束；"雇主与雇员之间的长期契约，并不是我们称为企业的那个组织的本质"（p. 777）。雇主—雇员关系是一连串双方都必须接受的再谈判（renegotiation），而非某项管理特权的行使。

如果命令（fiat）不是企业的区分性特征，那什么才是？杂货商之问提供了答案（pp. 777–778）：

Wherein then is the relationship between a grocer and his employee different from that between a grocer and his customers? It is in a team use of inputs and a centralized position of some party in the contractual arrangements of all other inputs. It is the centralized contractual agent in a team productive process not some superior authoritarian directive or disciplinary power. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), pp. 777–778

>

中文复述：杂货商与雇员的关系、杂货商与顾客的关系，差别到底在哪？差别在于投入的团队式使用（team use of inputs），以及某个当事方在与其他所有投入的契约安排中占据的中心位置。关键是一个团队生产过程中的集中化契约代理人（centralized contractual agent），而不是某种更高级的权威指令或纪律权力。

第二个更隐蔽的对手是古典价格理论本身。分配论的边际生产率理论（marginal-productivity theory of distribution）悄悄假定：已经存在某个组织在按生产率计量报酬。Alchian and Demsetz 把因果方向倒转过来："所依赖的那套具体报酬制度，会激发特定的生产率反应"（pp. 778–779）。计量得差的组织产出低生产率，计量得好的组织产出高生产率。因此在他们手里，组织经济学就是计量（metering）经济学——测量与付酬的经济学——并且在正的信息成本（positive information cost）下展开。

2.1 计量问题（The metering problem，论文 §I）

"经济组织面临两项关键要求——计量投入生产率和计量报酬"（p. 778）。如果报酬相对于努力是随机发放的，就不会有人付出生产性努力；如果报酬与生产率负相关，组织就是在招引破坏行为（sabotage）。只要产出可分离且可直接观察，市场就能廉价地解决这个问题：一个农民按现行价格把小麦产出提高 10%，收入就提高 10%，于是市场"直接计量产出、揭示边际产品并分配报酬"（p. 778）。整套古典的生产与分配理论装置都预设这种零成本计量存在。团队生产打破了这一预设，因为在团队生产中，个人努力与可观察产出之间的联系被切断了。

2.2 团队生产与偷懒（Team production and shirking，论文 §II）

基准例子是两个人往卡车上搬货：如果只观察每天装进车的总重量，谁也无法确定每个人的边际产品。Alchian and Demsetz 用生产函数把这一点形式化：Z 的团队生产至少使用两种投入 Xi 与 Xj，且交叉偏导 ∂²Z/∂Xi∂Xj ≠ 0 为正，因此 Z 无法写成两个可分离函数之和。团队组织问题由三个条件界定：(1) 使用多种类型的资源；(2) 产品不是各协作资源可分离产出之和；(3) 并非团队生产所用的一切资源都属于同一个人（pp. 779–780）。

With team production it is difficult, solely by observing total output, to either define or determine each individual's contribution to this output of the cooperating inputs. The output is yielded by a team, by definition, and it is not a sum of separable outputs of each of its members. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 779

>

中文复述：在团队生产中，单靠观察总产出，很难界定或确定每个个体对协作投入之产出的贡献。按定义，产出由团队产出，它不是每个成员可分离产出之和。

不可分离性使计量成本相对可分离生产"高出一个数量级"（p. 779）。其后果就是偷懒。休闲与收入都进入效用函数，因此每个人都会调整努力，使休闲与产出之间的边际替代率等于他自己的实际（realized）替代率——而由于松懈（relaxation）难以被完全侦测，其成本的一部分由队友承担。"侦测这类行为的困难，使他行为的私人成本低于其全部成本"（p. 780）。侦测支出只会被推进到边际收益等于边际成本那一点，所以均衡中总有一些偷懒存活；侦测成本"像是对工作报酬课税"，"可行的偷懒（viable shirking）是结果"（p. 781）。作者举的大学例子把这个观点落到实处：教师把办公室电话和文具用于私人目的，行政人员则加以容忍，因为揪出每个违规者的成本高于省下的钱——报酬只是在货币形式上更低，而以"特权、额外待遇或附加福利（privileges, perquisites, or fringe benefits）"的形式更高（p. 780）。

But since costs must be incurred to monitor each other, each input owner will have more incentive to shirk when he works as part of a team, than if his performance could be monitored easily or if he did not work as a team. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 780

>

中文复述：既然相互监督必须付出成本，那么与绩效容易被监督或根本不以团队方式工作相比，每个投入所有者在作为团队成员工作时会有更强的偷懒激励。

为什么不让每个人拥有一切，或让市场来监督团队？一人所有制（one-owner）这条路被很快搁置：奴隶制被禁止；人们厌恶为买下全部资产而借贷的风险；买卖价差（purchase–resale spread）可能超过租用成本（p. 779）。市场这条路因两条理由失败（p. 781）：其一，可能竞价替换偷懒成员的外部人，自己也得侦测偷懒——而在只能观察团队总产出时，侦测天然昂贵；其二，接受更小报酬份额的挑战者，仍只承担自己偷懒所造成产出损失的一部分，因此他的偷懒激励至少与在位者一样大。

2.3 监督的解决方案：谁来监督监督者？（The monitoring solution: who monitors the monitor?，论文 §III）

建设性的一步是监督的专业化。一人专司核查团队成员投入的绩效——计量产出、分配报酬、把投入行为作为边际生产率的线索来观察、下达任务（教练与队长的对比：教练把战术发进场内，队长贴身观察并向其报告，p. 782）。但专业化把激励问题向上推了一层：谁来监督监督者？潜在监督者之间的市场竞争是一种约束，但基于刚才给出的理由，它并不完善。决定性的第二重约束是"让他对团队的净收益拥有索取权，即扣除对其他投入的支付之后的净额"（p. 782）。剩余索取者自我约束，因为作为监督者偷懒现在直接由他自己埋单；而他通过减少偷懒来赚取剩余——既经由他向投入所有者支付的价格，也经由他对投入使用方式的观察与指挥（p. 782）。

要约束别人，他还必须能在不重新谈判整个团队的情况下修订单个契约，这就是为什么剩余索取者地位与成员资格权利（membership rights）融为一体（p. 783）：

It is this entire bundle of rights: 1) to be a residual claimant; 2) to observe input behavior; 3) to be the central party common to all contracts with inputs; 4) to alter the membership of the team; and 5) to sell these rights, that defines the ownership (or the employer) of the classical (capitalist, free-enterprise) firm. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 783

>

中文复述：正是这一整束权利——(1) 作为剩余索取者；(2) 观察投入行为；(3) 作为与各项投入缔约的共同中心当事方；(4) 改变团队构成；(5) 出售这些权利——定义了古典（资本主义、自由企业）企业的所有权（或雇主）。

这一结构的出现需要两个必要条件：(1) 团队生产能提高生产率，而其中各投入的边际产出难以直接计量；(2) 通过观察或规定投入行为来估计边际生产率是经济的（p. 783）。当投入活动是产出的良好线索时——码头工人、装配线——细致的指挥有效；当投入活动不是产出的良好线索时——律师、广告专员、医生——靠观察来监督无效，取而代之的是更松的缰绳加利润分享（p. 786）。按这种读法，每个成员与所有者之间的关系就是一份"交换条件（quid pro quo）"契约；雇员与雇主一样可以随时终止关系，长期契约"不是企业的本质属性"，"威权式（authoritarian）""独裁式（dictational）"或"命令式（fiat）"的属性与其概念或效率无关（p. 783）。图 1 画出完整的因果链。

2.4 组织形态的多样性即计量方式的多样性（Organizational variety as metering variety，论文 §IV–V）

随后这套框架被推到各种替代性组织形态上，每一种都被解读为对同一个偷懒问题的不同回答。利润分享型企业（profit-sharing firms）只在小团队中可行：均分利润使每个成员承担更大份额的自身偷懒损失，而偷懒激励"与均分利润方案下团队的最优规模正相关"（p. 786）；在不均分的情况下，避免偷懒的激励可用赫芬达尔指数（Herfindahl index）度量（注 10）。社会主义企业（南斯拉夫）说明政治约束：当法律要求全体雇员分享剩余时，监督者偷懒加剧造成的损失超过雇员偷懒减少带来的收益，于是工人委员会（workers' committee）应运而生去监督监督者——建议解雇经理的契约（pp. 786–787）。公司（corporation）面对的是剩余索取权分散在众多股东手中的问题：有限责任保护小投资者，可自由转让的股票让异议者退出（"无需其他任何股东批准即可出售公司股票的权利，" p. 788），对经理偷懒的约束来自潜在经理的外部竞争，以及把表决权集中为决定性集团的接管（takeover）或代理权争夺战（proxy battle）（pp. 788–789）。在一个著名脚注里，作者把股东重述为"不过是资助企业的群体中风险厌恶较低或更乐观的成员"——其剩余索取权主要不服务于监督功能（注 14，pp. 788–789）。互助与非营利企业（mutual and nonprofit firms）无法把改善的管理资本化进股价，于是潜在监督者之间的竞争更弱，"因此，在非营利、互助所有的企业中应当观察到更多的偷懒"（p. 790）。合伙（partnerships）适合艺术与专业团队——规模小、自我监督，且更可能出现在亲属或长期相识、彼此熟悉工作习惯的人之间（p. 790）——而工会（employee unions）则表现为雇员雇来监督难以计量的雇主绩效（如或有性附加福利）的专业监督者（p. 790）。第五节补充说，"团队精神（team spirit）"与忠诚在经济上是理性的：每个成员都偏好一个无人偷懒的团队——包括他自己——而忠诚是让实际替代率趋近真实替代率的廉价途径（p. 790）。

2.5 企业拥有哪些投入？（Which inputs does the firm own?，论文 §VI–VII）

由于剩余索取者式的监督者必须有能力以自身财富支付承诺的损失，他要投入资本；"企业的剩余索取者所有者，将是在企业中投入可再售资本设备（resalable capital equipment）的投资者"——即相对初始成本再售价值高、且预计在企业内长期使用的投入（p. 791）。在通过观察使用来侦测使用者成本与折旧最廉价的地方，预测是拥有而非租用：易损坏的锤子应被拥有而非租用，因为所有者在场时的不小心使用事后很难从锤子上计量出来；卡车则倾向于由司机拥有，因为"由司机来监督卡车使用中的爱惜程度相对便宜"（p. 792）。最后，第七节把企业一般化为"一种高度专业化的替代市场（highly specialized surrogate market）"（p. 793）：通过监督众多投入，雇主积累了关于其生产才能的卓越知识，把雇员放进好的投入组合从而把这些信息"出售"给雇员，并且能比一般市场搜寻更廉价地重组异质资源——这正是 Alchian and Demsetz 预测日本式终身雇佣（lifetime employment）应催生大型联合大企业（conglomerate firm）的依据（pp. 793–794）。

三个例子值得细看，因为它们承载的论证分量最重。外放加工制（putting-out system）直接瞄准交易成本理论：把织工集中到廉价动力源附近，降低了谈判契约的成本，然而出现的却是工厂——企业的扩张。作者坚持认为，原因在于"设备联合使用中的团队生产变得更加重要"：工人们通过共享机器相互作用，使得边际生产率的测量更困难——哪怕缔约变得更便宜——而集中化活动又让管理投入行为更容易（p. 784）。这个例子是被特意选来充当市场交易成本理论与他们的计量理论预测不同组织反应的场合。

锤子例子（p. 792）演示投入所有权逻辑。一把不易损坏、边际产品容易侦测的锤子，使用者成本为零，可以租用；一把易损坏、不小心使用会造成额外折旧的锤子，通过观察使用来监督比事后检查锤子更廉价。因此，不在场的所有者会对预期的滥用收取更高的租金，使自用（owner-use）比租用更便宜——这就是"所有权比租用更经济"这类误导性说法的有效内容（p. 792）。职业运动员脚注（注 16）把同一原则推到极限：当球队老板握有运动员的未来服务时，运动员从表现下滑中承担的财富损失更小，因此老板必须监督他的身体状况——作者推测，这就是运动员的未来服务被拥有而非被雇佣的原因（p. 792）。

比较制度观察——南斯拉夫的工人自治企业、非营利组织的偷懒、作为雇员监督者的工会、亲属间的合伙——都充当粗糙的自然实验：政治或法律约束迫使某种剩余索取安排成立，理论则预测随之出现的补偿性控制装置（否决经理的工人委员会、非营利组织中更多的偷懒、专门监督附加福利的工会专家）。作者对这些案例的证据地位很坦率。关于有表决权与无表决权股票的证据，他们写道"我们的粗略证据只配得起'激发兴趣'的分量"（注 14，p. 789）；对体育联盟的解释，他们承认没有实证检验能把过度投资解释与单纯的垄断限制区分开（注 15，p. 791）。论文自己可检验的含义，是以定性预测形式列出的——企业拥有哪类投入、利润分享在哪里可行、哪里应当观察到更多偷懒——而不是量化假说。

4. 文献定位（Position in the literature）

本文最主要的对话者是 Ronald Coase（1937），它与《企业的性质》的关系既恭敬又修正。Alchian and Demsetz 接受交易成本取向，同时拆掉其中的权威故事（p. 783）：

Our view of the firm is not necessarily inconsistent with Coase's; we attempt to go further and identify refutable implications. Coase's penetrating insight is to make more of the fact that markets do not operate costlessly, and he relies on the cost of using markets to form contracts as his basic explanation for the existence of firms. We do not disagree with the proposition that, ceteris paribus, the higher is the cost of transacting across markets the greater will be the comparative advantage of organizing resources within the firm; it is a difficult proposition to disagree with or to refute. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 783

>

中文复述：我们对企业的看法与 Coase 未必不一致；我们试图走得更远并找出可反驳的含义。Coase 的洞见在于重视市场并非无成本运作这一事实，并以利用市场缔约的成本作为企业存在的基本解释。我们并不反对如下命题——其他条件相同时，跨市场交易成本越高，在企业内部组织资源的比较优势越大；这个命题很难反对，也很难反驳。

抱怨在于，Coase 的框架"就其迄今被发展出的程度而言"止步太早（p. 784）：

Coase's analysis insofar as it had heretofore been developed would suggest open-ended contracts but does not appear to imply anything more—neither the residual claimant status nor the distinction between employee and subcontractor status (nor any of the implications indicated below). And it is not true that employees are generally employed on the basis of long-term contractual arrangements any more than on a series of short-term or indefinite length contracts. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 784

中文复述：Coase 迄今被发展出的分析只会导向开放性契约（open-ended contracts），似乎不再蕴含别的什么——既不蕴含剩余索取者地位，也不蕴含雇员与分包商地位之分（也不蕴含下文列出的任何含义）。雇员一般也并非基于长期契约安排受雇，而是基于一连串短期或不定期契约。

Frank Knight（1921）是第二个被挑战的先辈。Knight 的风险分配论——企业家作为不确定性的承担者——被视为一种事后补充，它解释不了剩余索取者的监督角色（p. 785）：

We deduce the system of paying the manager with a residual claim (the equity) from the desire to have efficient means to reduce shirking so as to make team production economical and not from the smaller aversion to the risks of enterprise in a dynamic economy. We conjecture that "distribution-of-risk" is not a valid rationale for the existence and organization of the classical firm. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 785

>

中文复述：我们之所以推出以剩余索取权（股权）支付经理的制度，是出于要有高效手段减少偷懒、使团队生产经济的愿望，而不是因为企业家在动态经济中对风险厌恶较小。我们推测，"风险分配"并不是古典企业存在与组织的有效理由。

与 Harold Demsetz 自己早前那篇《迈向产权理论》（"Toward a Theory of Property Rights"，1967）的关系是解释性的而非文字上的：Alchian and Demsetz（1972）没有引用它，但两文构成同一个研究纲领。Demsetz（1967）论证，当内部化的收益超过其成本时，产权就会出现以内部化外部性——他的拉布拉多毛皮贸易案例显示，加剧的海狸捕猎生出了私人土地权。Alchian and Demsetz（1972）恰恰把这一内部化逻辑应用到企业内部：团队生产中的偷懒是团队成员相互施加的外部性，而把剩余索取权指派给中心监督者，正是内部化它的制度装置。这就是第三周主线随后加以形式化与扩展的古典产权理论路线：Williamson（1975, 1985）经由机会主义与资产专用性（asset specificity）重新表述同样的缔约问题，却恢复了 Alchian and Demsetz 曾经否认的层级治理角色；Grossman and Hart（1986）与 Hart and Moore（1990）把所有权形式化为对资产的剩余控制权（control rights），把核心摩擦从监督移向事前投资与套牢（hold-up，参见 Hart 1995）；Kim and Mahoney（2005）把产权理论读作一种经济组织的系统观，并将其接入战略管理；所有权能力（ownership competence）议程（Foss、Klein 及其合作者）在资产所有权中复兴了对判断（judgment）的 Knight 式强调；Magelssen（2020）则提供了主线上产权理论所有权预测的实证应用。表 2 把这些经典论文彼此对照。

5. 局限（Limitations）

5(a) 作者自陈的局限（The authors' own stated limitations）

  • 他们把团队生产当作企业的"一个本质（必要？）条件"来处理，并用问号标出自己的不确定；小麦品质的例子表明，高监督成本的非团队来源也可能催生类似安排，他们承认"尚未深入地分析其他来源"——他们偏好的说法是"当前且随时可修正的推测"（pp. 784–785）。
  • 他们没有解释为什么联合使用的资源不由一个人全部拥有；这个问题被三条简短理由搁置——奴隶制被禁止、风险厌恶可能阻碍借贷买下全部资产、买卖价差可能超过租用成本（p. 779）。
  • 团队规模被当作外生给定，尽管剩余索取权的集中程度会影响它；这被承认是一种近似（注 10，p. 786）。
  • 对风险分配论的否定本身被标为推测（"risk distribution 不是有效的理由"，p. 785），结尾"企业作为私人市场"的解释也被说成"高度推测性但可能有意义"（p. 794）。
  • 定义被刻意收窄："企业"一词的日常用法"含义臃肿，我们不可能指望解释每一个"挂上这个名字的实体；联合大企业与控股公司可能是按 Knight 思路运作的投资装置，而不是他们意义上的企业（p. 785）。
  • 若干经验观察附有明确保留：有表决权与无表决权股份的证据"只配得起'激发兴趣'的分量"（注 14，p. 789）；没有实证检验能把体育联盟的过度投资解释与垄断解释分开（注 15，p. 791）。

5(b) 独立判断（Independent judgment）

  • [论文主张（paper claim）] 企业之所以存在，是因为它比市场交换更廉价地计量团队生产。这一主张被当作论文的中心结论断言，并以 p. 783 陈述的两个必要条件为前提。
  • [证据（evidence）] 支撑材料清一色是举例性的——搬货、教员额外待遇、锤子、日本、南斯拉夫——没有系统性数据；作者自己把其中一部分评为"粗略"（注 14，p. 789）。论文自己可检验的含义（哪些投入被拥有、利润分享在哪里存活、偷懒集中在哪里）是定性的，此处未予操作化。
  • [推断（inference）] 监督理论本身并不能确定企业的边界（boundary）——为什么正是这一组投入组成团队、团队在何种规模上停止扩张。作者把团队规模当作外生（注 10），并把企业的优势定位于对已在内投入的更廉价重组（pp. 793–794）——这解释内部范围，强于解释某一既定资产为何被拥有而非被缔约。现代产权理论通过资产层面的剩余控制权（Grossman and Hart 1986）提供边界解释，这是 Alchian and Demsetz 没有尝试的补充。
  • [开放问题（open question）] 在大型公众公司中，剩余索取权分散在众多股东手里，而按论文自己的注 14，这些股东是投资者而非监督者。于是 p. 782 处闭合"谁来监督监督者"链条的剩余索取者纪律实际上缺席，Alchian and Demsetz 只能退回到接管竞争与外部经理竞争。这些装置是否强到足以替代集中的剩余索取权，是断言而非论证。
  • [论文主张（paper claim）] 要紧的摩擦是偷懒，而不是机会主义套牢；它特地从团队产出的不可分离性中产生。现代产权理论把摩擦移到事前投资与对准租金（quasi-rent）的事后讨价还价（Grossman and Hart 1986；Hart and Moore 1990）；本文既没有调和这两种机制，也没有说明各自何时占主导。
  • [开放问题（open question）] "计量成本"从不独立于它被用来解释的组织结果加以度量，这带来循环论证的风险：观察到企业，那么计量想必成本高昂。论文对可检验含义（拥有哪类投入；非营利组织中偷懒更多）只做了示意，却没有给出外部研究者可以拿去处理数据的侦测成本度量——这正是主线上的实证应用试图填补的空缺。

6. 结论（Conclusion）

本文确立的是一个定义与一种机制。古典资本主义企业是一种契约结构——联合投入生产；多个投入所有者；一个与所有投入契约共同的当事方；独立重新谈判任何一份投入契约的权利；剩余索取权；出售整个中心地位的权利——解释它的机制，则是通过专业化、剩余索取者式的监督来减少团队生产中的偷懒。结语以最强的形式重述反 Coase 的命题（p. 794）：

The essence of the classical firm is identified here as a contractual structure with: 1) joint input production; 2) several input owners; 3) one party who is common to all the contracts of the joint inputs; 4) who has rights to renegotiate any input's contract independently of contracts with other input owners; 5) who holds the residual claim; and 6) who has the right to sell his central contractual residual status. The central agent is called the firm's owner and the employer. No authoritarian control is involved; the arrangement is simply a contractual structure subject to continuous renegotiation with the central agent. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 794

>

中文复述：古典企业的本质在此被界定为一种契约结构：(1) 联合投入生产；(2) 多个投入所有者；(3) 一个与联合投入的所有契约都共同的当事方；(4) 他有权独立于与其他投入所有者的契约，重新谈判任何一份投入契约；(5) 他持有剩余索取权；(6) 他有权出售自己的中心契约剩余地位。这个中心代理人被称为企业所有者与雇主。其中不涉及任何威权控制；安排不过是一种持续与中心代理人再谈判的契约结构。

从这一单一机制出发，作者推出了数量惊人的结构：利润分享在何时可行；公司、非营利组织、工会与合伙各自如何回应偷懒问题；企业拥有而非租用哪些投入；为什么企业可以读作一种汇集异质投入知识的私有市场。对第三周主线的读者来说，战略上的收获只有一句话：必须被信任去约束他人的一方，正是被授予索取权——以及成员资格权利——从而使约束变得值得的那一方。 让剩余索取权与再谈判权利去匹配团队的计量问题，所有权结构随之而来；试图靠权威来运转团队，权威就会被证明是一种幻觉。

Reading note. Alchian and Demsetz (1972) open the classical stage of the Week-3 arc: this paper defines the monitoring/metering problem and the residual-claimant solution that every later station of the arc presupposes. Demsetz (1967) had argued that property rights emerge to internalize externalities; this paper carries that logic inside the firm, treating shirking in team production as an externality that a bundle of residual rights internalizes. Modern PRT (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart and Moore 1990), the ownership-competence agenda (Foss, Klein, and co-authors), and the arc's empirical applications (e.g., Magelssen 2020) all inherit its question — who owns what, and why — even where they replace its monitoring mechanism with formal residual control rights over assets.

1. Background and motivation

Alchian and Demsetz open by stating the task of a theory of economic organization. It must do two things: "explain the conditions that determine whether the gains from specialization and cooperative production can better be obtained within an organization like the firm, or across markets, and to explain the structure of the organization" (p. 777). The two problems are deliberately joined. A theory that only tells you when firms beat markets is silent about the internal architecture of firms — who hires whom, who owns what, who monitors whom — and it is precisely those facts the authors want to derive.

The target of the essay is the then-standard, Coasean picture of the firm as a zone of authority. Against that picture the paper levels its most famous paragraph (p. 777):

It is common to see the firm characterized by the power to settle issues by fiat, by authority, or by disciplinary action superior to that available in the conventional market. This is delusion. The firm does not own all its inputs. It has no power of fiat, no authority, no disciplinary action any different in the slightest degree from ordinary market contracting between any two people. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 777

The "delusion" claim is argued by analogy. An employer can "punish" an employee only by withholding future business or by suing — exactly the sanctions available against a grocer. "Telling an employee to type this letter rather than to file that document is like my telling a grocer to sell me this brand of tuna rather than that brand of bread" (p. 777). Neither side is bound to continue; "long-term contracts between employer and employee are not the essence of the organization we call a firm" (p. 777). The employer–employee relation is a sequence of renegotiations that must be acceptable to both parties, not the exercise of a managerial prerogative.

If fiat is not the differentia of the firm, what is? The grocer question supplies the answer (pp. 777–778):

Wherein then is the relationship between a grocer and his employee different from that between a grocer and his customers? It is in a team use of inputs and a centralized position of some party in the contractual arrangements of all other inputs. It is the centralized contractual agent in a team productive process not some superior authoritarian directive or disciplinary power. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), pp. 777–778

A second, subtler adversary is classical price theory itself. The marginal-productivity theory of distribution quietly assumes that some organization already meters rewards in accord with productivity. Alchian and Demsetz reverse the causation: "the specific system of rewarding which is relied upon stimulates a particular productivity response" (pp. 778–779). An organization that meters poorly delivers low productivity; one that meters well delivers high productivity. The economics of organization is therefore, in their hands, the economics of metering — of measuring and rewarding — carried out at positive information cost.

2.1 The metering problem (paper §I)

"Two key demands are placed on an economic organization—metering input productivity and metering rewards" (p. 778). If rewards are random with respect to effort, no productive effort is supplied; if rewards are negatively correlated with productivity, the organization invites sabotage. Markets solve this problem cheaply whenever output is separable and directly observable: a farmer who raises wheat output by 10 percent at the prevailing price sees receipts rise 10 percent, so the market "meters the output directly, reveals the marginal product and apportions the rewards" (p. 778). The entire classical apparatus of production and distribution presumes such zero-cost metering exists. Team production breaks that presumption, because there the link between individual effort and observable output is severed.

2.2 Team production and shirking (paper §II)

The benchmark example is two men lifting cargo into trucks: watching only the total weight loaded per day, no one can determine each man's marginal product. Alchian and Demsetz formalize the point with the production function: team production of Z uses at least two inputs Xi and Xj with a positive cross partial, ∂²Z/∂Xi∂Xj ≠ 0, so Z cannot be written as the sum of two separable functions. Three conditions define the team-organization problem: (1) several types of resources are used, (2) the product is not a sum of separable outputs of each cooperating resource, and (3) not all resources used in team production belong to one person (pp. 779–780).

With team production it is difficult, solely by observing total output, to either define or determine each individual's contribution to this output of the cooperating inputs. The output is yielded by a team, by definition, and it is not a sum of separable outputs of each of its members. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 779

Non-separability raises the cost of metering "by an order of magnitude" (p. 779) relative to separable production. The consequence is shirking. Leisure and income both enter utility, so each person adjusts effort to equate the marginal rate of substitution between leisure and output with his realized rate — and because relaxation is imperfectly detectable, part of its cost is borne by teammates. "The difficulty of detecting such actions permits the private costs of his actions to be less than their full costs" (p. 780). Detection expenditure is pushed only to where its marginal gain equals its marginal cost, so some shirking survives in equilibrium; detection cost "acts like a tax on work rewards" and "viable shirking is the result" (p. 781). The authors' university example makes the point concrete: faculty use office telephones and stationery for personal ends, and administrators tolerate it because identifying each offender would cost more than the saving — pay is simply lower in pecuniary terms and higher in "privileges, perquisites, or fringe benefits" (p. 780).

But since costs must be incurred to monitor each other, each input owner will have more incentive to shirk when he works as part of a team, than if his performance could be monitored easily or if he did not work as a team. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 780

Why not solve this by having everyone own everything, or by letting the market police teams? The one-owner route is bracketed quickly: slavery is prohibited, people are risk averse about borrowing to buy all assets, and purchase–resale spreads can exceed rental costs (p. 779). The market route fails for two stated reasons (p. 781): first, outsiders who might bid to replace shirking members must themselves detect shirking, which by definition is costly when only team output is observable; second, a challenger who accepts a smaller reward share still bears less than the full output loss his shirking causes, so his incentive to shirk is at least as large as the incumbent's.

2.3 The monitoring solution: who monitors the monitor? (paper §III)

The constructive move is specialization in monitoring. One person specializes in checking the input performance of team members — measuring output, apportioning rewards, observing input behavior as clues to marginal productivity, and giving assignments (the coach-and-captain contrast: the coach sends in plays, the captain observes and reports at close hand, p. 782). But specialization recreates the incentive problem one level up: who monitors the monitor? Market competition among would-be monitors is a constraint but, for the reasons just given, an imperfect one. The decisive second constraint is to "give him title to the net earnings of the team, net of payments to other inputs" (p. 782). The residual claimant disciplines himself because shirking as a monitor now costs him directly, and he earns his residual by reducing shirking — both through the prices he pays input owners and through observing and directing how inputs are used (p. 782).

To discipline others he must also be able to revise individual contracts without renegotiating the whole team, which is why the residual-claimant position is fused with membership rights (p. 783):

It is this entire bundle of rights: 1) to be a residual claimant; 2) to observe input behavior; 3) to be the central party common to all contracts with inputs; 4) to alter the membership of the team; and 5) to sell these rights, that defines the ownership (or the employer) of the classical (capitalist, free-enterprise) firm. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 783

The emergence of this structure requires two necessary conditions: (1) productivity can be raised through team production whose marginal outputs are costly to measure directly, and (2) it is economical to estimate marginal productivity by observing or specifying input behavior (p. 783). Where input activity is a good clue to output — dock workers, assembly lines — detailed direction works; where it is not — lawyers, advertising specialists, doctors — monitoring by watching is ineffective and freer rein plus profit sharing appears instead (p. 786). The relation between each member and the owner is, on this reading, simply a "quid pro quo" contract; the employee can terminate as readily as the employer, so long-term contracts "are not an essential attribute of the firm," and "authoritarian," "dictational," or "fiat" attributes are not relevant to its conception or efficiency (p. 783). 2.4 Organizational variety as metering variety (paper §IV–V)

The framework is then run across alternative organizational forms, each read as a different answer to the same shirking problem. Profit-sharing firms work only for small teams: equal division leaves each member bearing a larger share of his own shirking losses, and incentives to shirk are "positively related to the optimal size of the team under an equal profit-sharing scheme" (p. 786); for unequal sharing the incentive to avoid shirking can be measured by the Herfindahl index (fn. 10). Socialist firms (Yugoslavia) illustrate political constraints: where all employees share the residual by law, losses from enhanced monitor shirking exceed gains from reduced employee shirking, so workers' committees arise to monitor the monitor — recommending termination of a manager's contract (pp. 786–787). The corporation confronts the diffusion of residual claims across many stockholders: limited liability protects small investors, freely salable shares let dissenters exit ("the right to sale of corporate shares without approval of any other stockholders," p. 788), and the policing of managerial shirking comes from outside competition by would-be managers and from takeover or proxy battles that concentrate votes into decisive blocs (pp. 788–789). In a celebrated footnote the authors recast stockholders as "merely the less risk-averse or the more optimistic member of the group that finances the firm" — investors whose residual claim does not primarily serve a monitoring function (fn. 14, pp. 788–789). Mutual and nonprofit firms cannot capitalize improved management into share prices, so competition among would-be monitors is weaker and "one should, therefore, find greater shirking in nonprofit, mutually owned enterprises" (p. 790). Partnerships suit artistic and professional teams — small, self-monitoring, and likelier among relatives or long-standing acquaintances who know each other's work habits (p. 790) — and employee unions appear as specialist monitors hired by employees to police hard-to-meter employer performance such as contingent fringe benefits (p. 790). Section V adds that "team spirit" and loyalty are economically rational: every member prefers a team in which no one, not even himself, shirks, and loyalty is a cheap way to move realized rates of substitution toward true ones (p. 790).

2.5 Which inputs does the firm own? (paper §VI–VII)

Because the residual-claimant monitor must be able to pay promised amounts in losses, he commits wealth; "residual claimants owners of the firm will be investors of resalable capital equipment in the firm" — inputs with high resale value relative to initial cost and long expected use within the firm (p. 791). Ownership, rather than rental, is predicted where user cost and depreciation are cheapest to detect by watching use: the destructible hammer is owned rather than rented because careless use in the owner's absence is hard to meter from the hammer afterward, while trucks tend to be driver-owned because "it is relatively cheap for the driver to police the care taken in using a truck" (p. 792). Finally, Section VII generalizes the firm into "a highly specialized surrogate market" (p. 793): by monitoring many inputs, the employer accumulates superior knowledge of their productive talents, "sells" that information to employees by placing them in good input combinations, and can recombine heterogeneous resources more cheaply than a general market search — the basis of Alchian and Demsetz's prediction that Japanese-style lifetime employment should breed large conglomerate firms (pp. 793–794).

Three examples deserve closer attention because they carry the heaviest argumentative weight. The putting-out system is aimed squarely at transaction-cost theories: bringing weavers to a central power source reduced the cost of negotiating contracts, yet what emerged was the factory — an expansion of the firm. The reason, the authors insist, is that "team production in the joint use of equipment became more important": interactions among workers through shared machines made marginal-productivity measurement harder even as contract formation got cheaper, while centralized activity made managing input behavior easier (p. 784). The example is deliberately chosen as a case where a market-transaction-cost theory and their metering theory predict different organizational responses.

The hammer example (p. 792) demonstrates the input-ownership logic. An indestructible hammer with a readily detectable marginal product has zero user cost and can be rented; a destructible hammer whose careless use causes extra depreciation is more cheaply monitored by watching the use than by inspecting the hammer afterward. An absentee owner therefore charges a higher rental for the expected abuse, making owner-use cheaper than renting — "the valid content of the misleading expressions about ownership being more economical than renting" (p. 792). The professional-athlete footnote (fn. 16) pushes the same principle to its limit: when a team owner holds the athlete's future services, the athlete bears less of the wealth loss from declining performance, so the owner must monitor his physical condition — which is why, the authors conjecture, athletes' future services are owned rather than hired (p. 792).

The comparative-institutional observations — Yugoslavia's worker-managed firms, nonprofit shirking, unions as employee monitors, partnerships among relatives — all function as crude natural experiments: political or legal constraints force a particular residual-claim arrangement, and the theory predicts the compensating control device that appears (workers' committees vetoing managers, greater shirking in nonprofits, union specialists policing fringe benefits). The authors are candid about the evidentiary status of these cases. Of the voting-versus-nonvoting-share evidence they write that "our casual evidence deserves no more than interest-initiating weight" (fn. 14, p. 789), and of the sports-league interpretation they admit no empirical test exists to separate the overinvestment rationale from plain monopoly restriction (fn. 15, p. 791). The paper's own testable implications are listed as qualitative predictions — about which kinds of inputs firms own, where profit sharing is viable, and where more shirking should be observed — rather than quantified hypotheses.

4. Position in the literature

The paper's chief interlocutor is Ronald Coase (1937), and its relation to "The Nature of the Firm" is both deferential and corrective. Alchian and Demsetz accept the transaction-cost orientation while stripping out the authority story (p. 783):

Our view of the firm is not necessarily inconsistent with Coase's; we attempt to go further and identify refutable implications. Coase's penetrating insight is to make more of the fact that markets do not operate costlessly, and he relies on the cost of using markets to form contracts as his basic explanation for the existence of firms. We do not disagree with the proposition that, ceteris paribus, the higher is the cost of transacting across markets the greater will be the comparative advantage of organizing resources within the firm; it is a difficult proposition to disagree with or to refute. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 783

The complaint is that Coase's framework, "insofar as it had heretofore been developed," stops too early (p. 784):

Coase's analysis insofar as it had heretofore been developed would suggest open-ended contracts but does not appear to imply anything more—neither the residual claimant status nor the distinction between employee and subcontractor status (nor any of the implications indicated below). And it is not true that employees are generally employed on the basis of long-term contractual arrangements any more than on a series of short-term or indefinite length contracts. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 784

Frank Knight (1921) is the second ancestor to be challenged. Knight's risk-distribution rationale — the entrepreneur as the bearer of uncertainty — is treated as an afterthought that does not explain the residual claimant's monitoring role (p. 785):

We deduce the system of paying the manager with a residual claim (the equity) from the desire to have efficient means to reduce shirking so as to make team production economical and not from the smaller aversion to the risks of enterprise in a dynamic economy. We conjecture that "distribution-of-risk" is not a valid rationale for the existence and organization of the classical firm. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 785

Within the paper the authors position themselves against and alongside a tight circle of contemporaries: they credit Stewart Macaulay's (1963) account of non-contractual relations for why open-ended contracts work, invoke S. The relation to Harold Demsetz's own earlier paper, "Toward a Theory of Property Rights" (1967), is interpretive rather than textual: Alchian and Demsetz (1972) do not cite it, but the two papers form one program. Demsetz (1967) argued that property rights emerge to internalize externalities when the gains from internalization exceed its costs — his Labrador fur-trade case shows intensified beaver hunting generating private land rights. Alchian and Demsetz (1972) apply exactly that internalization logic inside the firm: shirking in team production is an externality that team members impose on one another, and assigning a residual claim to a central monitor is the institutional device that internalizes it. This is the classical PRT line that the Week-3 arc then formalizes and extends: Williamson (1975, 1985) recasts the same contracting problems through opportunism and asset specificity, but restores a hierarchical governance role that Alchian and Demsetz had denied; Grossman and Hart (1986) and Hart and Moore (1990) formalize ownership as residual control rights over assets, shifting the central friction from monitoring to ex-ante investment and hold-up (see Hart 1995); Kim and Mahoney (2005) read property-rights theory as a systems view of economic organization feeding strategic management; the ownership-competence agenda (Foss, Klein, and co-authors) revives a Knightian emphasis on judgment in asset ownership; and Magelssen (2020) supplies the arc's empirical application of PRT ownership predictions. 5. Limitations

5(a) The authors' own stated limitations

  • They treat team production as "an essential (necessary?) condition" for the firm and flag their own uncertainty with the question mark; the wheat-quality example shows that non-team sources of high policing cost might call forth similar arrangements, and they concede they "have not intensively analyzed other sources" — their preferred interpretation is "current and readily revisable conjecture" (pp. 784–785).
  • They do not explain why jointly used resources are not all owned by one person; the question is bracketed with three quick reasons — slavery is prohibited, risk aversion may block borrowing to buy all assets, and purchase–resale spreads may exceed rental costs (p. 779).
  • Team size is treated as exogenous even though the degree of residual-claim centralization affects it; this is admitted as an approximation (fn. 10, p. 786).
  • The rejection of the risk-distribution rationale is itself labeled a conjecture ("distribution-of-risk is not a valid rationale," p. 785), and the final "firm as private market" interpretation is introduced as "highly conjectural but possibly significant" (p. 794).
  • The definition is deliberately narrow: the common usage of "firm" is "so turgid of meaning that we can not hope to explain every entity" to which the name attaches; conglomerates and holding companies may be investment devices along Knightian lines rather than firms in their sense (p. 785).
  • Several empirical observations are offered with explicit caveats: the voting-versus-nonvoting-share evidence "deserves no more than interest-initiating weight" (fn. 14, p. 789), and no empirical test separates the overinvestment from the monopoly account of sports leagues (fn. 15, p. 791).

5(b) Independent judgment

  • [paper claim] The firm exists because it meters team production more cheaply than market exchange. This claim is asserted as the paper's central result and is conditional on the two necessary conditions stated at p. 783.
  • [evidence] The supporting material is uniformly illustrative — cargo loading, faculty perks, hammers, Japan, Yugoslavia — with no systematic data; the authors themselves grade parts of it "casual" (fn. 14, p. 789). The paper's own testable implications (which inputs are owned, where profit sharing survives, where shirking concentrates) are qualitative and were not operationalized here.
  • [inference] The monitoring theory does not by itself pin down the firm's boundary — why this particular set of inputs forms a team, and at what scale the team stops growing. The authors treat team size as exogenous (fn. 10) and locate the firm's advantage in cheaper recombination of inputs already inside (pp. 793–794), which explains internal scope better than it explains why a given asset is owned rather than contracted. Modern PRT supplies the boundary account through asset-level residual control rights (Grossman and Hart 1986), a complement Alchian and Demsetz did not attempt.
  • [open question] In the large public corporation the residual claim is dispersed across many stockholders who, by the paper's own footnote 14, are investors rather than monitors. The residual-claimant discipline that closes the "who monitors the monitor" chain at p. 782 is then effectively absent, and Alchian and Demsetz fall back on takeover contests and outside management competition. Whether those devices are strong enough to substitute for a concentrated residual claim is asserted rather than shown.
  • [paper claim] Shirking, not opportunistic hold-up, is the friction that matters, and it arises specifically from the non-separability of team output. Modern PRT relocates the friction to ex-ante investments and ex-post bargaining over quasi-rents (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart and Moore 1990); nothing in this paper reconciles the two mechanisms or says when each dominates.
  • [open question] "Metering cost" is never measured independently of the organizational outcomes it is used to explain, which creates a risk of circularity: firms are observed, therefore metering must have been costly. The paper gestures at testable implications (kinds of inputs owned; more shirking in nonprofits) but provides no metric for detection cost that an outside researcher could take to data — the gap the arc's empirical applications try to fill.

6. Conclusion

What the paper establishes is a definition and a mechanism. The classical capitalist firm is a contractual structure — joint input production; several input owners; one party common to all input contracts; rights to renegotiate any input's contract independently; the residual claim; and the right to sell the whole central position — and the mechanism that explains it is the reduction of shirking through specialized, residual-claimant monitoring of team production. The summary restates the anti-Coasean thesis in its strongest form (p. 794):

The essence of the classical firm is identified here as a contractual structure with: 1) joint input production; 2) several input owners; 3) one party who is common to all the contracts of the joint inputs; 4) who has rights to renegotiate any input's contract independently of contracts with other input owners; 5) who holds the residual claim; and 6) who has the right to sell his central contractual residual status. The central agent is called the firm's owner and the employer. No authoritarian control is involved; the arrangement is simply a contractual structure subject to continuous renegotiation with the central agent. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 794

From this single mechanism the authors derive a surprising amount of structure: when profit sharing is viable, how corporations, nonprofits, unions, and partnerships each respond to the shirking problem, which inputs firms own rather than rent, and why the firm can be read as a privately owned market that collates knowledge of heterogeneous inputs. For a reader of the Week-3 arc, the strategic takeaway is one line: the party who must be trusted to police others is the party given the claim — and the membership rights — that make policing worthwhile. Design the residual-claim and renegotiation rights to match the metering problem of the team, and ownership structure follows; try to run the team on authority, and the authority turns out to be an illusion.

The paper ends by inverting Coase's question: where Coase asked why markets fail and firms succeed, Alchian and Demsetz close by asking whether markets — not firms — carry the property-rights defects that internal organization corrects. — Alchian and Demsetz (1972), p. 795
