A Resource-Based View of the Firm

What strategic choices become easier to see when a firm is analyzed through its resources?

The shift in analytical perspective

Author’s argument. Wernerfelt proposes examining a firm through the resources that support its products. The resource and product perspectives are connected: a product draws on several resources, while a resource may contribute to several products. Examining only one product market can obscure the value of a resource across the firm’s activities. The introduction explicitly acknowledges Penrose and Rubin as predecessors. (pp. 171–172.)

The article asks when resources yield high returns, how firms can develop resource positions, and how these considerations affect growth and acquisition. Its resource concept includes tangible and intangible assets associated with the firm over time. The argument is a strategy framework with an illustrative model; it does not present a validated checklist or an original empirical test.

Resource position barriers

A resource position barrier exists when an incumbent’s position makes resource acquisition less attractive for later firms by worsening their costs or potential revenues. Resource advantages depend on the competitive setting: possession by itself does not establish a barrier, and a barrier has to support an economically useful application. Wernerfelt adapts competitive-force reasoning to the resource side, including supplier power, competition among users and substitutes. (pp. 172–175.)

Resource position Mechanism discussed Question for a concrete application
Machine capacity Scale and potential effects on the returns available to later capacity Does extra capacity deter profitable entry or merely burden its owner?
Customer loyalty Prior relationships make winning customers harder for rivals Which customers and purchasing conditions sustain this effect?
Production experience Accumulated output changes production costs Can entrants obtain equivalent knowledge by another route?
Technological leadership Initial knowledge can facilitate further development How quickly does diffusion or a technological shift erode the lead?

Our interpretation. The concept is relational. The relevant question is what an incumbent position does to a rival’s feasible economics. A rare input that has no profitable use has little strategic value; an input with many equivalent substitutes may not protect returns. These questions prepare the discussion for Barney’s later treatment of value, rarity, imitation and substitution while preserving Wernerfelt’s distinct focus on position barriers.

The connection between resource and product markets

Wernerfelt argues that a resource barrier and a product-market entry barrier need to be considered together. A resource position matters when it can be deployed in a product market. A product barrier may also be vulnerable to a rival that develops relevant resources in another market and then enters. This creates a reason to look beyond the immediate set of product-market competitors. (pp. 173–175.)

Our illustration. A producer may build manufacturing experience in one product and then enter an adjacent product that uses similar processes. The initial market is a place to earn revenue and a setting in which capabilities develop. Whether this works depends on the transferability of the resource and the competitive response. This example illustrates the argument rather than reproducing a tested case from the paper.

Growth and the resource–product matrix

The resource–product matrix asks which resources support which products and where additional resource development could permit entry. It helps make two activities visible at once: exploiting current resources and developing resources that will support later activity. Wernerfelt’s two-period model illustrates a sequential entry logic when experience is jointly produced with products. A first activity can become a stepping stone into a second. (pp. 176–179.)

Decision Resource-side question Limit of the article’s answer
Enter another market Which current resources improve the entry economics? A conceptual link does not establish market attractiveness.
Develop a resource Which products can help build it, and where else could it be used? The dynamic model covers a particular experience-based setting.
Acquire a business Which complementary resource combinations become possible? Realizing complementarity requires organizational implementation.
Sequence expansion Which first move lowers the cost or raises the value of later moves? The best sequence depends on the resource and its development process.

The matrix should be presented as an analytical aid. The paper does not claim that a diagram can settle valuation or integration problems. Its conclusion explicitly leaves resource identification, combinations across divisions and implementation systems underdeveloped. (pp. 179–180.)

Acquisitions and the problem of valuation

Acquiring a business can supply resources that are difficult to purchase individually. Their value depends partly on how they fit the buyer’s existing resource position, so the same target can be worth different amounts to different acquirers. Wernerfelt treats such combinations as a reason to analyze acquisition through resources. (pp. 174–175.)

Our synthesis. Read this alongside Barney’s strategic-factor-market paper and the later value-capture literature. A valuable combination does not by itself specify who receives the surplus: the acquisition price, bargaining, employee mobility and integration costs all matter. The question moves from identifying complementarity to explaining why a buyer can capture a return after paying for the target.

Evidence, interpretation and classroom use

The paper’s support consists of economic reasoning, resource examples and the simple growth model. Its most distinctive contribution is the connection among resource positions, multiple products and the sequence of development. Barney (1991) supplies a later set of attributes associated with sustained advantage; Peteraf (1993) sharpens the conditions for rents. These papers can be compared as answers to related but different questions.

For Yupeng’s presentation, a useful sequence is: define a resource position barrier; show one resource linked to two products; explain how an incumbent’s position affects a rival; then identify an unresolved implementation issue. The strongest discussion question is whether a proposed resource is specified independently enough that its presence could be observed before performance is known.
