Agency Theory:
From Interest Alignment to Organizational Systems

Organizing question: how can cooperation be sustained when information, objectives, and decision rights are distributed across participants? The review separates compensation, monitoring, authority, relational commitments, social preferences, and task allocation, then compares their assumptions and evidence.

The six course readings supply the backbone. The 34 additional papers below provide mechanisms, empirical tests, and debates. Classical agency models sit alongside organizational and social mechanisms that change their predictions, with AI studies included as carefully delimited adjacent evidence. Each digest follows pitch → question → argument → evidence → findings → position → limitation and states its source coverage.

Read the synthesis first: six connected streams

Delegation and governance. Ross begins with delegated choice; Fama and Fama–Jensen broaden the analysis to managerial labor markets, decision management, decision control, and residual claims. This is already an organizational argument beyond a bonus contract. Labor-market discipline, boards, and claim structures can interact, but their existence does not establish that every agency problem disappears. Fama retains conditions under which anticipated wage revisions do not fully discipline behavior. Read Mahoney’s criticism of optimistic interpretations against those qualifications. See Ross, Fama, and Fama–Jensen.

From incentive intensity to incentive systems. Holmström’s information analysis makes the value of a measure depend on what it reveals beyond existing observations. His team model exposes the difficulty created by shared output and budget balance. Holmström–Milgrom then show how strong incentives for a measurable task change attention to poorly measured tasks. Baker distinguishes noisy measurement from distortion between the metric and the actual objective. Together these arguments motivate joint analysis of pay, tasks, assets, and discretion. See observability, multitask agency, and performance measurement.

Commitments that cannot be fully enforced. Subjective evaluation, informal delegation, and continued cooperation rely on the future relationship. Baker–Gibbons–Murphy connect formal payments, intervention rights, and self-enforcing relational agreements; Levin further characterizes dynamic incentives. Relational contracting exists within and across boundaries. Ownership changes reneging temptations and continuation values, so neither “trust” nor “being in the same firm” can replace the mechanism. See 1994, 1999, 2002, and Levin.

Tests of particular incentive changes. Lazear’s piece-rate study involves both incumbent behavior and workforce sorting; the total productivity change cannot all be attributed to greater effort by existing workers. Bandiera and colleagues incorporate peer relationships, managerial behavior, and selection. Prendergast’s review emphasizes that evidence of a response to incentives does not establish optimal contract choice or discriminate among all relevant organizational theories. See Lazear, Bandiera 2005, 2007, and Prendergast.

Behavioral assumptions and the outcome being evaluated. Stewardship theory proposes pro-organizational managerial motivation. Cooperation experiments reveal punishment and behavior beyond immediate material self-interest; control can also signal distrust. Motivation meta-analyses require distinctions among reward categories, tasks, and outcomes. These works challenge particular simple behavioral assumptions while supplying variables for richer contracting models. Identify the exact prediction under challenge. See Davis et al., Fehr–Gächter, Falk–Kosfeld, Deci et al., and Cerasoli et al..

AI and organization design. Algorithmic-control research examines how technologies allocate, evaluate, and regulate work. Tong and colleagues distinguish the effects of deploying AI feedback from disclosing its source. Generative-AI experiments and field evidence often test tool access and task performance. They inform us about skills, tasks, and information, but without measuring incentives, authority, or boundaries they cannot independently establish which theory of the firm is validated. See Kellogg et al., Tong et al., Brynjolfsson et al., and Vaccaro et al..

Delegation, governance, and residual claims

1973

The Economic Theory of Agency: The Principal’s Problem

Stephen A. Ross · American Economic Review
Original source · DOI not verified

Pitch: The compact formal starting point for treating compensation as a device that shapes delegated choice.

Research question: How should a principal reward an agent whose decisions affect the principal’s welfare, when their preferences need not coincide?

Argument / mechanism: An agency relationship connects a principal’s objective to another person’s optimizing behavior. The principal chooses a compensation arrangement while anticipating the agent’s response. This makes the agent’s decision rule part of the contracting problem rather than an externally imposed assumption.

Evidence / design: Formal economic analysis of a principal and an agent with utility functions; no empirical sample or causal estimate. Full six-page article consulted, including the contracting problem, similarity condition, and concluding discussion.

Findings: The paper identifies special utility and payoff structures for which motivating the agent is compatible with Pareto-efficient sharing. In general, the motivational constraint can conflict with that benchmark. Its similarity condition aligns the parties’ attitudes toward risky payoffs.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: place this before Jensen–Meckling to distinguish the general delegated-choice problem from the later explanation of ownership structure and agency costs. It supplies a vocabulary for asking whose objective a contract implements.

Limit / reader assessment: The derivation uses restrictive assumptions and, in part, a first-order approach; later work clarifies the validity requirements. Bargaining and general-equilibrium agency markets are left for further development. This is not a complete corporate-governance theory.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted (pp. 134–139); formal proof not independently replicated · Published article pp. 134–138, principal problem, similarity condition and conclusion; p. 139 references; USP-hosted PDF

1980

Agency Problems and the Theory of the Firm

Eugene F. Fama · Journal of Political Economy
10.1086/260866

Pitch: Managerial labor markets can discipline managers even when they do not hold the residual claim.

Research question: How can separation of management and risk bearing survive competitively?

Argument / mechanism: Treat the firm as a set of contracts among self-interested factors. Managers supply decision services; security holders supply risk bearing. Internal monitoring and external labor-market assessment affect the manager’s future opportunities and compensation, making current performance consequential beyond the current pay contract.

Evidence / design: Conceptual contracting argument with an illustrative wage-revision model; no causal comparison of governance systems. The introduction and conclusion are consulted, including the explicit qualification about incomplete ex post settling up.

Findings: Under particular conditions, anticipated wage revision can offset the gain from shirking. Fama explicitly does not claim that the labor market always produces complete settling up; insufficient future wage effects leave incentive problems unresolved.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: this broadens the mechanisms considered after Jensen–Meckling. Equity ownership, monitoring, and career-based discipline should be compared rather than treating direct ownership as the only alignment channel. It also leads naturally to formal career-concerns models.

Limit / reader assessment: Competitive selection and adequate information are substantial assumptions. Finite careers, noisy inference, and distorted managerial actions can weaken the discipline. The survival of a corporate form alone is not a causal test of its efficiency.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Article pp. 288–290 and 306, introduction and conclusions

1983

Separation of Ownership and Control

Eugene F. Fama, Michael C. Jensen · The Journal of Law and Economics
10.1086/467037

Pitch: Separate proposing and implementing decisions from ratifying and monitoring them.

Research question: How can organizations exploit specialized knowledge while decision makers bear little of the resulting financial risk?

Argument / mechanism: Decision management comprises initiation and implementation; decision control comprises ratification and monitoring. When risk bearing is separated from decision management, separating the two decision functions can constrain self-serving choices while retaining specialization. When knowledge is concentrated and scale small, combining decision functions with residual claims can be economical.

Evidence / design: Conceptual comparative analysis of corporations, partnerships, mutuals, and nonprofits. Organizational examples illustrate propositions; they are not a sampled empirical identification strategy.

Findings: Boards and internal decision hierarchies are interpreted as mechanisms for decision control. The argument links complexity and dispersed specific knowledge to specialization, while recognizing the risk-sharing costs of restricting residual claims to decision makers.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: this connects Mahoney’s agency-cost discussion to a concrete map of organizational roles. For an actual firm, identify who proposes, approves, executes, and evaluates before labeling it centralized or decentralized.

Limit / reader assessment: The organizational-survival logic can understate power, institutional constraints, and inefficient persistence. Separation on an organizational chart is not evidence that monitoring is independent or effective in practice.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Article pp. 301–306, introduction and decision-process definitions; local Week 3 copy identity verified

1983

Agency Problems and Residual Claims

Eugene F. Fama, Michael C. Jensen · The Journal of Law and Economics
10.1086/467038

Pitch: The restrictions on residual claims help explain why different organizational forms perform different activities.

Research question: Why do open corporations, partnerships, mutuals, and nonprofits distribute residual risk and claims differently?

Argument / mechanism: Residual claims allocate uncertain net cash flows after promised payments. Freely transferable claims permit risk diversification and specialized management; restricting claims to decision agents can reduce agency conflicts but sacrifices those benefits. More specialized forms respond to particular contracting problems among producers, customers, and donors.

Evidence / design: Comparative theoretical propositions and organizational illustrations, not an empirical ranking of forms. The introduction, residual-claim definitions, and concluding comparative propositions are consulted.

Findings: The case for open corporations strengthens with the benefits of risk sharing and specialized management, demands for committed wealth and organization-specific assets, and lower costs of decision control. Alternative claim restrictions can address conflicts that unrestricted common stock would create.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: this complements the companion paper: one maps decision processes, the other maps cash-flow claims. Together they encourage comparing bundles of governance features instead of treating ownership as a single scalar.

Limit / reader assessment: The analysis concerns private organizations and uses competitive-survival reasoning. Nonprofit status does not remove all agency problems, and the propositions do not establish that observed organizational forms are universally efficient.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Article pp. 327–333 and 345–349, definitions and comparative conclusions

Information, measurement, and incentive systems

1979

Moral Hazard and Observability

Bengt Holmström · The Bell Journal of Economics
10.2307/3003320

Pitch: The information principle behind deciding what belongs in a performance contract.

Research question: When does an additional signal improve a contract if the agent’s action cannot be directly observed?

Argument / mechanism: Output combines effort with uncertainty, so strong output incentives expose a risk-averse agent to risk. Additional information is useful when it improves inference about action beyond what the existing contractible outcome already reveals. Conditional informativeness is therefore central to the value of monitoring.

Evidence / design: Formal moral-hazard model and mathematical characterization, with no field data. The opening and information discussion are consulted; the technical proof is not independently reproduced.

Findings: A signal can improve incentive provision even if it does not directly measure output. Conversely, a signal that adds no relevant information conditional on existing observations offers no corresponding informational gain under the model’s assumptions.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: this clarifies Mahoney’s monitoring versus incentive-contract discussion. A dashboard deserves inclusion because it helps distinguish effort from noise, not simply because the number is available. It also prepares the team paper’s treatment of relative evaluation.

Limit / reader assessment: The result assumes the signals can be used in the contract and abstracts from many costs of constructing or manipulating measures. It does not imply that every recorded metric should receive a positive bonus weight.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Article pp. 74–77, opening, model, and stated information results; first five PDF pages OCR

1982

Moral Hazard in Teams

Bengt Holmström · The Bell Journal of Economics
10.2307/3003457

Pitch: Team production makes the allocation of the budget itself an incentive instrument.

Research question: Can a team distribute its entire output among members and still give everyone efficient effort incentives? What makes peer comparisons useful?

Argument / mechanism: When only aggregate output is contractible, each member receives only part of the marginal benefit created by effort. In the studied setting, balancing the budget within the team constrains efficient incentives. An outside principal can administer rewards and penalties without returning all realized output to members.

Evidence / design: Formal multiagent moral-hazard analysis. The paper separately examines free riding and information in relative performance evaluation; these are theoretical results rather than measured organizational effects.

Findings: Separation of labor and ownership can help by relaxing the budget-balance requirement. Comparisons among agents matter for the information they supply about individual action, especially when common uncertainty can be filtered out. Competition itself has no independent productive merit in this model.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: use this alongside Alchian–Demsetz and Holmström’s subeconomy paper. Ask whether the manager is monitoring effort, supplying informative comparisons, or controlling the incentive budget; these mechanisms are analytically distinct.

Limit / reader assessment: The impossibility reasoning is conditional on the model’s information and sharing restrictions. Communication, repeated interaction, social preferences, and richer individual signals can change the feasible arrangements.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Article pp. 324–327, abstract and opening free-rider analysis; local scanned PDF first five pages OCR

1987

Aggregation and Linearity in the Provision of Intertemporal Incentives

Bengt Holmström, Paul Milgrom · Econometrica
10.2307/1913238

Pitch: Explains why simple linear pay can emerge from a rich dynamic incentive problem.

Research question: Why are real compensation schedules often linear when elementary agency models can prescribe complicated outcome-contingent payments?

Argument / mechanism: An agent can adjust actions over time as uncertainty unfolds. With constant absolute risk aversion and the specified stochastic environment, the richness of those controls makes complicated nonlinear rewards vulnerable to adjustment. Aggregated accounts can retain the information needed for optimal pay.

Evidence / design: Formal discrete- and continuous-time models; the latter lets the agent control drift in noisy accounts. Introduction and concluding interpretation consulted; no empirical sample and no independent proof replication.

Findings: Within this environment, optimal compensation is linear in the relevant accounts or observed linear aggregates. The result supplies a tractable framework for comparing incentive intensity, risk, and multiple activities.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: the important bridge to the 1991 multitask model is methodological. A linear contract need not be an arbitrary shortcut; under defined conditions it summarizes a richer dynamic problem. This helps explain why the later organizational analyses can use simple pay equations.

Limit / reader assessment: Linearity is conditional on the preference and technology assumptions. The authors themselves qualify the extent to which their Bayesian model establishes practical robustness. It does not prove that thresholds or nonlinear contracts are always inefficient.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Article pp. 303–305 and 325–326, introduction and concluding interpretation

1991

Multitask Principal–Agent Analyses: Incentive Contracts, Asset Ownership, and Job Design

Bengt Holmström, Paul Milgrom · The Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization
10.1093/jleo/7.special_issue.24

Pitch: Incentives allocate attention among tasks, so strong rewards on one dimension can reduce total value.

Research question: Why may fixed wages and limits on worker discretion be efficient even when a useful output measure exists?

Argument / mechanism: A worker divides effort across activities with different measurability. Increasing rewards for the measured activity draws attention away from poorly measured but valuable work. Contract design therefore interacts with asset ownership, restrictions on outside activities, and how tasks are grouped into jobs.

Evidence / design: Formal multitask principal–agent model based on the linear framework of the 1987 paper. Teaching, output quality, and equipment care are illustrations, not causal field tests. Opening argument and model motivation consulted.

Findings: Weak explicit incentives can be optimal because they avoid distorting effort allocation. Separating tasks into jobs can mitigate the conflict; ownership changes incentives for care of assets and therefore changes the desirable compensation arrangement.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: this is a direct bridge between agency theory and Mahoney’s organizational analysis. For every proposed performance measure, identify the valuable work it omits and ask whether job redesign should accompany the pay change.

Limit / reader assessment: The sign and size of distortion depend on substitution across activities, measurement, and technology. The result does not establish that fixed wages are best in all complex jobs, or that measurement improvements always harm performance.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Article opening pp. 24–28; author-hosted scanned PDF first five pages OCR

1994

The Firm as an Incentive System

Bengt Holmström, Paul Milgrom · American Economic Review
Original source · DOI not verified

Pitch: Employment and independent contracting can be understood as alternative incentive systems.

Research question: Why do pay intensity, asset ownership, and freedom from direct controls often move together?

Argument / mechanism: Several instruments affect a worker’s task portfolio. Strong incentives on one activity can unbalance attention; coordinated changes in ownership, pay, and discretion can instead reinforce one another. The model distinguishes complementarities in the objective from the additional assumptions needed for observed cross-sectional covariation.

Evidence / design: Formal organizational analysis connected to existing evidence, particularly sales-force organization. The paper does not conduct a new randomized comparison; empirical examples motivate and illustrate the theoretical restrictions.

Findings: Under the specified conditions, high-powered incentives, worker ownership, and greater freedom form a complementary bundle. The authors explicitly show why complementarity alone does not guarantee that every parameter change moves all instruments together.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: connect this to Mahoney’s comparative institutional approach. Changing only commission rates while leaving asset control and work restrictions fixed may be a poor test of the theory because the mechanism concerns a coordinated system.

Limit / reader assessment: Observed correlations between practices need not identify complementarity or efficiency. Unobserved parameters and the direction of exogenous changes matter. The argument gives conditional comparative statics, not a universal employee-versus-contractor ranking.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Article pp. 972–976, introduction and system-complementarity qualifications; first five pages OCR

1983

An Analysis of the Principal-Agent Problem

Sanford J. Grossman, Oliver D. Hart · Econometrica
10.2307/1912246

Pitch: A methodological foundation for solving incentive problems without mistaking a local optimum for incentive compatibility.

Research question: How can the principal find an optimal contract when the agent’s global choice cannot safely be replaced by a first-order condition?

Argument / mechanism: The paper separates implementing a specified action at minimum cost from selecting which action the principal wants to implement. Under the stated independence of preferences over income lotteries from action, implementation can be formulated as a convex programming problem.

Evidence / design: Formal mathematical analysis. Only the indexed original abstract and opening are consulted in this pass; attempted full-PDF downloads failed. No empirical design or effect estimate is claimed.

Findings: The abstract establishes an alternative to a generally invalid stationary-point approach and uses it to characterize optimal incentive schemes and the determinants of incentive difficulty. Detailed theorem conditions beyond the accessible opening are not summarized here.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: this is a useful technical checkpoint for the seminar. Whenever a simple agency model is presented, ask whether the proposed action is the agent’s best choice among all feasible deviations. A solved equation is not by itself that verification.

Limit / reader assessment: Abstract/opening-only coverage limits the specificity of this digest. Full reading is required before adopting propositions, monotonicity claims, or comparative statics from the paper in original research.

1979

Risk Sharing and Incentives in the Principal and Agent Relationship

Steven Shavell · The Bell Journal of Economics
10.2307/3003319

Pitch: The classic tradeoff between insuring the agent and inducing the desired action.

Research question: How should the fee depend on outcomes and imperfect action information when principal and agent differ in risk attitudes?

Argument / mechanism: The fee both distributes outcome risk and changes the agent’s incentive to act. A schedule that insures the agent well can weaken effort incentives when effort is hidden. Information about action can permit better alignment while reducing the need to expose the agent to outcome risk.

Evidence / design: Formal characterization of Pareto-optimal fee schedules with outcome-only information and with additional information about activity. Applications illustrate the logic; there is no empirical treatment effect. Introduction and applications are consulted.

Findings: The desirable schedule depends on the two parties’ attitudes toward risk and available information. A risk-neutral agent can in relevant cases bear the residual outcome risk; risk aversion makes information about conduct particularly valuable for balancing incentives and insurance.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: pair this with Holmström 1979 when unpacking Mahoney’s outcome-based and behavior-based arrangements. The central comparison concerns which uncertainty is borne by whom and what observable evidence can justify payment.

Limit / reader assessment: The analysis is conditional on the permitted schedules and modeled information. Wealth constraints, limited liability, enforcement costs, and endogenous measurement may alter practical prescriptions; policy illustrations are not empirical validation.

1992

Incentive Contracts and Performance Measurement

George P. Baker · Journal of Political Economy
10.1086/261831

Pitch: A precise performance measure can still reward the wrong action.

Research question: What happens when contractual performance differs from the principal’s true objective?

Argument / mechanism: A measure can react to effort differently from firm value. Even a risk-neutral worker may then choose actions that increase pay while adding little value. The relevant alignment concerns the relationship between how actions change the measure and how they change the principal’s payoff.

Evidence / design: Formal incentive model with examples of gaming, sales commissions, and relative evaluation. No new empirical sample or estimated causal effect; article introduction and principal argument consulted.

Findings: Perfectly eliminating risk is insufficient to restore first-best incentives when the measure is distorted. The efficiency and optimal strength of incentives depend on the measure’s relation to the objective, giving a way to diagnose why stronger incentives can have weak value effects.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: this adds a second diagnostic to Holmström’s information principle. Ask both whether the measure reveals effort and whether it induces valuable effort. In a strategy seminar, sales volume, margins, customer retention, and longer-run value provide distinct potential objectives.

Limit / reader assessment: Applications are illustrations and the model does not supply an empirical estimate of distortion for a particular metric. A high correlation between measured outcomes and value need not establish alignment of marginal incentives.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Article pp. 598–600, introduction, result summary and performance-measure motivation

Relational contracts and informal authority

1994

Subjective Performance Measures in Optimal Incentive Contracts

George Baker, Robert Gibbons, Kevin J. Murphy · The Quarterly Journal of Economics
10.2307/2118358

Pitch: Subjective bonuses can correct bad metrics, provided the employer can credibly promise to pay.

Research question: When do objective and subjective performance measures complement one another, and when can formal contracts undermine informal incentives?

Argument / mechanism: Objective measures can distort behavior because they omit contributions to value. A supervisor may assess those contributions, but a discretionary bonus is vulnerable to reneging. Repeated interaction makes the promise credible only when future relational gains outweigh the immediate benefit from breaking it.

Evidence / design: Repeated-game incentive-contract theory with motivating business examples. The examples describe distorted measures; they are not a causal test of the model. Introduction and summary of results consulted in the published scan.

Findings: Explicit and implicit contracts can reinforce one another, yet sufficiently attractive explicit contracting can also weaken the penalty for abandoning an implicit agreement. The paper therefore derives conditional complementarity and substitution rather than assuming one universal relation.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: connect this to Mahoney’s monitoring and bonding categories and to Baker 1992. Better measurement changes both immediate incentives and the continuation value that supports managerial promises; institutional design has to account for both channels.

Limit / reader assessment: Self-enforcement and credible continuation values are central assumptions. The analysis does not resolve favoritism or differing beliefs about subjective assessments, and business anecdotes cannot establish the prevalence of the predicted mechanism.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Article pp. 1125–1128, introduction and summary of results; scanned PDF first five pages OCR

1999

Informal Authority in Organizations

George Baker, Robert Gibbons, Kevin J. Murphy · The Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization
10.1093/jleo/15.1.56

Pitch: Delegation within a hierarchy can depend on a reputation for leaving decisions with subordinates.

Research question: How can subordinates exercise authority when a boss retains the right to overturn their decisions?

Argument / mechanism: Informal delegation is supported by a relational contract. An informed boss may promise not to overturn unattractive projects to preserve the subordinate’s initiative; an uninformed boss may instead discipline abuse by withdrawing future authority. The information structure changes whose reputation and reneging temptation constrain the relationship.

Evidence / design: Two formal repeated-interaction models: informed and uninformed boss. The local copy is an author manuscript dated November 1998 for the 1999 journal article; abstract and opening substantive discussion consulted.

Findings: Delegation can be efficient yet infeasible if a boss cannot credibly refrain from intervention. Better information can change reneging incentives and, in the model, reduce joint welfare. Divestiture provides a distinct way of moving formal decision rights.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: pair directly with assigned Aghion–Tirole. Track information-based real authority separately from a superior’s credible promise to respect delegated choices. Similar observed autonomy can arise through different mechanisms.

Limit / reader assessment: The assertion that internal decision rights are not contractible is a modeling premise. Organizational and legal environments vary, and the paper does not estimate how often either informational configuration governs actual firms.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Author manuscript abstract and pp. 1–3, dated November 3, 1998; journal publication 1999

2002

Relational Contracts and the Theory of the Firm

George Baker, Robert Gibbons, Kevin J. Murphy · The Quarterly Journal of Economics
10.1162/003355302753399445

Pitch: Ownership changes which informal promises trading partners can sustain.

Research question: Why can the same relational agreement be feasible inside a firm but fail between firms, or vice versa?

Argument / mechanism: Asset ownership changes the parties’ options after reneging. These outside options alter the immediate gains from breaking promised payments or delivery arrangements. Repeated interaction then supports different feasible contracts under integration and nonintegration, so the boundary decision serves the relationship.

Evidence / design: Formal repeated-game comparison of spot and relational governance under alternative ownership. No new causal empirical test. Published PDF introduction and discussion of relational enforcement consulted; the initially reused local file was rejected as a different paper.

Findings: Relational contracting operates both within and across firm boundaries. Integration can improve or worsen the sustainable agreement, depending on how it changes reneging temptations. The theory connects asset control with the continuation value of cooperation.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: this is the strongest bridge in this set from agency incentives to property-rights theory and TCE. Compare ownership jointly with the informal promises it enables; buying an asset changes more than a static bargaining position.

Limit / reader assessment: The framework characterizes supportable relationships rather than explaining how participants discover shared expectations. Enforcement assumptions and outside options are decisive. Claims about actual alliances or integrations require additional evidence.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Published article pp. 39–42, introduction and relational-enforcement discussion; verified Washington-hosted PDF

2003

Relational Incentive Contracts

Jonathan Levin · American Economic Review
10.1257/000282803322157115

Pitch: Self-enforcement places an upper bound on how powerful a promised incentive can be.

Research question: How should a long-term informal contract handle hidden information and moral hazard when neither party can commit perfectly?

Argument / mechanism: Current compensation must remain credible in light of future surplus. The dynamic enforcement constraint limits promised transfers, so optimal incentive design trades off motivational gains against the temptation to abandon the relationship. The paper studies when stationary agreements suffice and how information changes feasible rewards.

Evidence / design: Formal repeated-contracting analysis covering hidden information, moral hazard, and subjective performance measures. Published author-hosted PDF abstract and introduction consulted; no empirical identification design.

Findings: In the studied environments, optimal contracts can often be stationary. Hidden information may lead to the same inefficient effort across cost realizations; moral-hazard contracts can use two compensation levels. With subjective measurement, termination following poor performance may be part of the optimal arrangement.

Position in the literature: Reader synthesis: this provides the enforcement foundation behind the Baker–Gibbons–Murphy sequence. For Mahoney-based discussion, ask which future surplus backs a promise and whether the proposed bonus exceeds what the relationship can credibly support.

Limit / reader assessment: These are conditional optimal-contract results, not universal features of employment. Limited commitment, information structure, and the available transfer technology matter; designing the shared understanding needed to implement an equilibrium remains a separate problem.

Source and coverage: Full text consulted: selected substantive sections; not a proof-by-proof reading · Published article pp. 835–837, abstract and introduction

Empirical tests of incentive design

2000

Performance Pay and Productivity

Edward P Lazear · American Economic Review 90(5): 1346-1361
10.1257/aer.90.5.1346

Pitch: Pay changes alter both what existing workers do and which workers join or remain. This is a concrete empirical starting point for separating moral hazard from selection.

Research question: Does replacing hourly pay with piece rates raise productivity through worker effort, workforce composition, or both, and how should those channels be distinguished empirically?

先读综合：六条研究线怎样接起来？

第一条线解释委托与治理。Ross 从委托决策入手；Fama 以及 Fama–Jensen 将视野扩展到经理人市场、决策管理、决策控制与剩余索取权。这里的研究对象已经超出单一奖金合同。劳动市场约束、董事会和索取权配置可以相互配合，但其存在本身不能证明所有代理问题都被消除。Fama 的讨论保留了未来工资调整不足的情况，Mahoney 对乐观解释的批评应结合这些条件阅读。见 Ross、Fama、Fama–Jensen。

第二条线把“激励力度”推进为“激励体系”。Holmström 的信息分析说明，指标价值取决于它在已有信息之外提供了什么；团队模型引出预算平衡与共同产出下的激励难题。Holmström–Milgrom 的多任务分析进一步说明，对易测量任务施加强激励，会改变对难测量任务的注意力分配。Baker 则区分测量噪声与指标对真正目标的扭曲。由此，需要共同解释工资、任务、资产与行动自由。见 1979 信息分析、1991 多任务、1992 绩效测量。

第三条线处理无法完全执行的承诺。主观评价、非正式授权与跨期合作都依赖未来关系。Baker–Gibbons–Murphy 把正式合同、干预权和关系契约的自我执行条件连接起来；Levin 进一步刻画动态关系激励。企业内部和企业之间都可以存在关系契约，边界改变的是违约诱因和继续合作的价值。因此“有信任”或“在同一家公司”都不足以替代机制分析。见 1994、1999、2002、Levin。

第四条线检验具体激励变化。Lazear 的计件工资研究同时涉及在职员工行为和员工排序，因而不能把总生产率变化全部解释为努力增加。Bandiera 等人的研究把同事关系、经理行为与员工选择纳入解释。Prendergast 的综述提醒读者，对“人会响应激励”的支持，并不自动意味着已经证实契约选择最优，或已在不同组织理论之间作出区分。见 Lazear、Bandiera 2005、2007、Prendergast。

第五条线改变行为假设或评价对象。Stewardship 理论提出管理者可能具有亲组织的动机。合作实验展示对他人的奖惩和非纯物质收益行为；控制还可能传递不信任。关于内在动机的元分析则要求区分奖励类型、任务和结果指标。它们为 agency 的简单行为设定提出挑战，也为更丰富的契约与组织模型提供变量；应检查具体被挑战的是哪一条预测。见 Davis 等、Fehr–Gächter、Falk–Kosfeld、Deci 等、Cerasoli 等。

第六条线连接 AI 与组织设计。算法控制综述观察技术如何分配、评价和规制工作；Tong 等的现场研究区分 AI 反馈实际部署与披露其来源的效应。生成式 AI 的实验和现场证据则多在检验工具访问对任务表现的影响。它们可以说明技能、任务与信息发生了什么变化，但若没有同时测量激励、权威或组织边界，便不能直接回答“哪一种企业理论得到验证”。见 Kellogg 等、Tong 等、Brynjolfsson 等、Vaccaro 等。

委托、治理与剩余索取权

1973

The Economic Theory of Agency: The Principal’s Problem

Stephen A. Ross · American Economic Review
原文来源 · DOI 未确认

一句话贡献: 这篇短文为用报酬安排影响受托决策提供了形式化起点。

研究问题: 当代理人的决策影响委托人的福利、双方偏好又可能不同，委托人应如何设计报酬？

论证与机制: 代理关系把一个人的目标与另一个人的最优化行为连接起来。委托人在预判代理人反应的条件下选择报酬安排，因此代理人的决策规则本身进入契约问题。

证据与设计: 形式经济分析，没有经验样本或因果估计。已查读六页全文，包括契约问题、相似性条件及结尾讨论。

研究发现: 论文识别出激励代理人能够与帕累托有效分享兼容的特定效用和收益结构；一般而言，激励约束可能与该基准冲突。相似性条件使双方对风险收益的态度一致。

文献位置: 阅读者综合：可放在 Jensen–Meckling 之前，用于区分一般的受托选择问题与后续的所有权结构、代理成本解释；课堂上可据此追问契约实现了谁的目标。

限制与阅读者评价: 推导依赖限制性条件，部分采用一阶方法，后续研究进一步澄清其有效性要求。议价和一般均衡代理市场留待发展，文章并非完整公司治理理论。

1980

Agency Problems and the Theory of the Firm

Eugene F. Fama · Journal of Political Economy
10.1086/260866

一句话贡献: 即使经理不持有剩余索取权，经理人劳动市场也可能形成纪律。

研究问题: 管理与风险承担的分离为何能在竞争中存续？

论证与机制: 企业是自利生产要素之间的契约集合。经理提供决策服务，证券持有人承担风险；内部监控和外部劳动市场评价影响经理未来的机会与薪酬，使当前表现的后果延伸到当期契约之外。

证据与设计: 概念性契约论证及工资修正示例模型，没有治理制度的因果比较。本条查读引言与结论，特别保留事后清算不充分的限定。

研究发现: 特定条件下，预期工资修正能够抵消偷懒的收益。Fama 明确没有声称劳动市场总能实现完全事后清算；未来工资后果不足时，激励问题依然存在。

文献位置: 阅读者综合：在 Jensen–Meckling 之后加入职业市场纪律，与股权及监控比较，并自然引向 career concerns 模型。

限制与阅读者评价: 竞争选择与充分信息是重要条件。有限职业生涯、噪声及被扭曲的管理行动可能削弱纪律；公司形式的存续本身并非效率的因果检验。

1983

Separation of Ownership and Control

Eugene F. Fama, Michael C. Jensen · The Journal of Law and Economics
10.1086/467037

一句话贡献: 把提出与实施决策，同批准与监督决策区分开来。

研究问题: 决策者很少承担财务后果时，组织如何仍然利用专业知识？

论证与机制: 决策管理包括发起与实施，决策控制包括批准与监控。风险承担和决策管理分离时，两类决策职能的分离能够约束自利行为，同时保留专业分工。知识集中且规模较小时，将决策职能和剩余索取权结合可能更经济。

证据与设计: 对公司、合伙、互助组织和非营利组织的概念性比较。组织例子用于说明命题，没有抽样的经验识别设计。

研究发现: 董事会及内部决策层级被解释为控制机制。组织复杂性和专门知识分散有利于分工，而把剩余权利限制在决策者手中会牺牲风险分担。

文献位置: 阅读者综合：可将 Mahoney 的代理成本讨论转化为角色图，先识别谁提出、批准、执行和评价，再判断集中或分权。

限制与阅读者评价: 组织存续的效率逻辑可能低估权力、制度限制及低效持续性。组织图上的职能分离不能证明监督实际独立有效。

1983

Agency Problems and Residual Claims

Eugene F. Fama, Michael C. Jensen · The Journal of Law and Economics
10.1086/467038

一句话贡献: 剩余索取权的限制有助于解释不同组织形式为何适用于不同活动。

研究问题: 公开公司、合伙、互助组织及非营利组织，为何以不同方式分配剩余风险和权利？

论证与机制: 剩余索取权分配支付契约承诺之后的不确定净现金流。自由转让有利于分散风险和专业管理；将权利限制于决策者能减少部分代理冲突，却牺牲这些收益。特殊形式回应生产者、顾客或捐赠者之间的契约问题。

证据与设计: 比较性理论命题及组织例子，并非组织形式的经验排名。本条查读引言、剩余权利定义和总结性比较命题。

研究发现: 风险分担及专业管理收益更高、承诺财富与组织专用资产需求更大、决策控制成本更低时，公开公司的优势增强；其他限制可以处理普通股产生的特定冲突。

信息、测量与激励体系

1979

Moral Hazard and Observability

Bengt Holmström · The Bell Journal of Economics
10.2307/3003320

一句话贡献: 解释绩效契约中应纳入哪些信息的核心原则。

研究问题: 代理人的行动无法直接观察时，额外信号在什么条件下能改善契约？

论证与机制: 产出同时受努力与随机因素影响，较强的产出激励会让风险厌恶者承担风险。若额外信息能在已有产出信息之外帮助推断行动，监控就有价值，关键是条件信息增量。

证据与设计: 形式化道德风险模型及数学刻画，没有现场数据。本条查读开头与信息论述，没有独立重做证明。

研究发现: 信号即使不直接衡量产出，也可能改善激励；在既有观察条件下不增加相关信息的信号，则没有相应的信息收益。

文献位置: 阅读者综合：这有助于细化 Mahoney 对监控和激励契约的讨论。指标的意义在于帮助区分努力与噪声，也为团队论文中的相对绩效评价作铺垫。

限制与阅读者评价: 结论以信号可用于契约为条件，许多指标建设及操纵成本没有进入模型。不能从中推出所有记录指标都应该获得正的奖金权重。

1982

Moral Hazard in Teams

Bengt Holmström · The Bell Journal of Economics
10.2307/3003457

一句话贡献: 团队生产中，预算分配本身可以成为激励工具。

研究问题: 团队能否把全部产出分给成员，同时让每个人选择有效率的努力？成员间比较为什么有用？

论证与机制: 仅能对总产出订约时，每名成员只获得自身努力边际收益的一部分。在文中环境下，团队内部预算平衡限制有效激励。外部委托人可以管理奖励与惩罚，而无须把每种结果下的全部产出返还成员。

证据与设计: 多代理人道德风险的形式分析，分别讨论搭便车及相对绩效评价的信息作用，结论并非组织效果的经验估计。

研究发现: 劳动与所有权分离可通过放松预算平衡约束发挥作用。成员比较的价值来自识别个人行动，尤其是剔除共同不确定性；竞争本身在该模型中没有独立收益。

文献位置: 阅读者综合：与 Alchian–Demsetz 及 subeconomy 并读，区分管理者在监控努力、提供信息比较，还是控制激励预算。

限制与阅读者评价: 结论依赖信息与分享规则的限制。沟通、重复互动、社会偏好及更丰富的个体信号可能改变可实现的安排。

1987

Aggregation and Linearity in the Provision of Intertemporal Incentives

Bengt Holmström, Paul Milgrom · Econometrica
10.2307/1913238

一句话贡献: 解释为何丰富的动态激励问题可能产生简单线性报酬。

研究问题: 基本代理模型常产生复杂的状态依赖报酬，现实中为何常见线性制度？

论证与机制: 代理人可以在不确定性展开时调整行动。在常绝对风险厌恶和特定随机环境下，丰富的行动空间让复杂非线性报酬容易受到行为调整影响，汇总账户仍可保留最优报酬所需的信息。

证据与设计: 离散和连续时间形式模型，后者让代理人控制含噪账户的漂移。查读引言及结尾解释，没有经验样本，也没有独立重做证明。

证据与设计: 正式 QJE 研究分析5172名客服人员分阶段获得工具后的面板差异。本条选择阅读正式PDF的结果及薪酬相关结论；这是观察性推广设计。

研究发现: 每小时解决问题数平均增加15%；经验少、技能低者受益较多，高技能者速度略增但质量略降。学习和顾客互动也有部分改善。

一句话贡献: 随机写作实验表明AI既改变绩效，也重新分配起草和编辑的努力，使岗位重组成为可观察问题。

研究问题: 获得 ChatGPT 如何影响职业写作的时间与质量，哪些写作环节投入减少或增加？

论证与机制: 工具提供可供检查和修订的草稿，即使金钱奖励不变，任务技术与分工变化也可能提高产出。

证据与设计: 参与者被随机提供 ChatGPT 完成有报酬的职业写作任务。存档为 MIT 2023年3月工作论文，包含444人；正式 Science 版本与DOI另已核实。

研究发现: 可得版本报告完成更快、评分更高、差距缩小，努力从起草转向编辑。这检验工具使用机会，不是绩效工资强度的因果作用。

文献位置: 研讨连接：产出增加可能来自工具、技能或激励变化。起草变便宜、核验仍重要时，绩效指标应奖励什么？

一句话贡献: 人机组合可以优于单独的人，却仍不及人或AI中较好的一方，因此比较基准决定如何解释协作收益。

研究问题: 相对于人单独、AI单独以及两者较好者，人机结合何时提高绩效？

论证与机制: 协作需要有效分配判断权，获得额外建议本身不足以保证收益；基础能力和任务类型决定人的干预何时有益。

证据与设计: 预注册系统综述与元分析汇总106项实验、370个效应量，研究发表期为2020年1月至2023年6月。本条阅读开放PDF的摘要和讨论。

研究发现: 平均而言，组合弱于最佳单独选项，但强于人单独；决策任务出现损失，内容创作相对收益更高，双方基础能力也调节结果。

文献位置: 研讨连接：Noy–Zhang 和 Brynjolfsson 等人相对人类基准的提高不等于超过最佳替代方案。讨论授权时先明确基准。

限制与阅读者评价: 证据早于许多现有系统，任务、样本和设计差异较大，可能存在发表偏差。调节关系不等于最优授权的因果识别，绩效也不是保留人工监督的全部理由。
