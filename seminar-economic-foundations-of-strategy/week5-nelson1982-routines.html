Where do organizational capabilities reside, and why does competent performance persist while adaptation remains difficult?

Capabilities as organized performance

Author’s argument. Chapter 5 explains productive capability through organizational routines. Its scope fits large, complex organizations engaged in recurring activity particularly well. Each member has a repertoire of possible performances, but collective production requires members to recognize which contribution is called for and to coordinate it with others. The organization’s capability therefore depends on shared experience and a system of meaningful messages, as well as individual skills and equipment. (pp. 96–105.)

Messages include formal instructions, informal communication and changes in the work environment. A component arriving at a station can both enable an operation and indicate that it is time to perform it. A local vocabulary can make such coordination effective while making the process opaque to newcomers. The authors explicitly acknowledge Arrow’s treatment of organizational codes. (pp. 100–104.)

Routine as memory

Organizations retain much operational knowledge through repeated performance. Written records, equipment and individual memories contribute, but their usefulness depends on the organizational context in which they are interpreted. No individual has to hold an exhaustive account of the whole process. (pp. 99–107.)

Form of continuity Contribution Vulnerability
Individual repertoires Skilled execution and interpretation Turnover and skills that deteriorate through disuse
Shared experience Coordinated expectations and locally meaningful communication New members and unfamiliar combinations
Records and equipment External supports for memory Loss of context or physical disruption
Repeated performance Refreshes the connections needed for coordination Infrequently used capabilities become difficult to retain

The flexibility problem follows directly. A firm can distribute the knowledge required for a complex current task across many people. Maintaining many rarely used alternative performances is harder because those people must retain skills and meanings that receive little practice. Drills can help but cost resources. (pp. 105–107.)

Routine as truce

The chapter then adds motivation. Routine operation can include predictable delays, rule-breaking and imperfect effort. What matters is a stable accommodation among members, maintained by monitoring, rewards, expectations and other motivations. Participants can disagree while continuing to perform within familiar bounds. (pp. 107–112.)

Author’s argument. These arrangements form a truce. A change that appears technically sensible can threaten an established distribution of burdens, influence or rewards and provoke resistance. The cognitive and political sources of continuity therefore reinforce one another.

Our cross-week interpretation. This provides a direct connection to agency and internal organization. A productive routine carries both knowledge and an accommodation among interests. Studying only its technical efficiency can miss why it persists; studying only incentives can miss the coordination knowledge that changing it would disrupt.

Control, replication and imitation

Task What has to be accomplished Advantage or difficulty
Control Keep a routine working as people, equipment and inputs change Requires selection, training, monitoring and occasional adaptation.
Replication Recreate performance in a new setting The existing operation supplies a template, trainers and experienced personnel.
Imitation Reproduce another firm’s economic success The target is imperfectly observed and may be unavailable as a template.
Contraction Respond to sustained adversity Financial pressure and an unstable truce can make behavior difficult to predict.

The chapter treats replication as costly and time consuming. Even internal transfer can fail because of tacitness, teaching difficulty, reluctance to cooperate or a different social setting. Perfect replication appears later as a simplifying modeling assumption, which the chapter’s discussion explicitly qualifies. (pp. 112–124, 134–136.)

Routines and innovation

Routine operation can generate puzzles that trigger search. Existing reliable routines also provide components for new combinations, and innovation efforts can follow patterned heuristics even when their results are uncertain. The authors explicitly connect this account to Schumpeter. (pp. 128–134.)

Existing coordinated activityPuzzles and performance problemsHeuristic search and recombinationUncertain innovationsRevised routines

Our synthesis. This makes persistence and change parts of a single explanation. Familiar routines can supply reliable components for innovation while narrowing the search space and protecting established interests. Later dynamic-capability arguments inherit this problem: how can organizations develop patterned ways of changing their resource base without defining those patterns through eventual success?

Evidence and scope

Chapter 5 provides conceptual reasoning and illustrations, not an original empirical test. Its broad use of routine includes both operational patterns and higher-level problem-solving heuristics. The authors acknowledge that organizations whose principal activity is producing change fit the recurring-operation model less neatly. The later evolutionary models are outside the assigned reading.

For discussion, identify a routine’s memory function and truce function separately. Then ask which part a proposed organizational change would disturb. This supplies a concrete bridge from knowledge and capabilities to incentives, authority and internal conflict.
